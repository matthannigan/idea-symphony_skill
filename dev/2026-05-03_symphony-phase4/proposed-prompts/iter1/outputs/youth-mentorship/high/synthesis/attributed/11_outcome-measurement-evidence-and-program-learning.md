### Question 66: Outcome measurement without overburdening teens or making mentors feel surveilled [User Q]

**Full question** [User Q]: The program's real goal is meaningful relationship — but standard metrics (attendance, GPA, recidivism) are lagging proxies that can feel transactional and surveillance-like. What measurement approach honors the depth of the work, remains light enough not to burden mentors or teens, captures leading indicators detectable within weeks, preserves a teen's sense of being valued as a person, and still gives the foundation evidence worth funding?

* **Make structured mentor reflection — not teen surveys — the primary measurement instrument.** Six of seven personas converge on this architectural shift: the teen is observed by an adult who knows them well, rather than asked to fill out forms. Specific protocols vary — voice memos, monthly two-line logs, dignity-grids that separate "what I did" from "what I noticed," weekly "what surprised me" prompts — but the move is unanimous: measurement runs through the mentor's eyes, not through teen-facing instruments. This turns measurement into a mentor learning practice rather than an assessment imposed on the teen.
  * **Mentors note one micro-moment per week when the teen showed up differently — no teen participation required.** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper: "ask mentors to notice and document one micro-moment per week when the teen showed up differently—a conversation where the teen volunteered something personal, initiated contact, asked for help with something beyond logistics." *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Asymmetric observation: mentor tracks own growth on three prompts; teen chooses their own progress markers.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker: weekly mentor template — "What surprised me?", "Where did we get stuck?", "What did I learn about this teen?" — paired with teen-chosen markers like "days I felt heard." *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Two-minute monthly mentor voice memo doubles as supervision and data collection.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker: "What was the most important moment this month?" and "What's one thing I'm unsure about?" — self-directed reflection that doubles as early-warning supervision. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Mentor observation grid separates "what I did" from "what I noticed" — preserves dignity and removes surveillance language.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Mentor logs as primary signal align with trust-based philanthropy and qualitative-evidence trends.** Original detail from The Futurist: structured mentor reflection (not surveillance-style tracking) is increasingly recognized by foundations as valid evidence. *—The Futurist*
  * **Mentor initiation log + monthly 15-minute phone spot-check protocol.** Original detail from The Pragmatist: "number of times this week I initiated contact or brought something intentionally to discuss"; coordinator asks one open question per month: "What's one thing you've learned about [teen] that surprised you?" *—The Pragmatist*

* **Let the teen co-design or choose the metric — flip the frame from "the program is assessing you" to "we're tracking something you care about."** Five personas converge on inverting the locus of metric authority. The mechanism varies (intake conversation; quarterly check-ins; teen-curated artifacts), but the move is the same: the teen names what would matter to see shift, and the program tracks that.
  * **At intake, teen and mentor pick one thing that would matter to see shift over the year.** Original detail from The Analogist: "Some teens will care about GPA, others about staying out of court, others about having an adult who listens." *—The Analogist*
  * **Open quarterly prompt — "What's something that's felt different in your life?" — collected in the teen's own language.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate: no multiple-choice options; let the teen frame what matters. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Personal goal chosen by the teen becomes the metric, owned by both parties.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate: "the goal itself becomes the metric" — measurement embedded in natural relationship rhythm. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Self-directed evidence: teen keeps a "proof pile" of artifacts they chose to keep — photos, texts, voice memos, completed applications.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker: at year-end, teen and mentor review the pile together — evidence the teen chose to keep, not evidence the program extracted. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Participatory data practice: invite mentors and teens together to identify one teen-chosen metric (e.g., "trying something scary," "sleeping better").** Original detail from The Futurist: aligns with the trust-based-philanthropy / equity-centered-evaluation trajectory. *—The Futurist*

* **Use school counselor data as the foundation-facing proxy — institutional data the teen already provides to other systems.** Five personas point to school-held attendance, discipline, and engagement data as the right place to source funder-facing metrics, because the teen is not separately surveyed and the school already collects it.
  * **School confirms attendance and discipline data quarterly with guardian consent built into enrollment.** Original detail from The Pragmatist: "We partner with schools to understand context, not to generate program metrics." *—The Pragmatist*
  * **School counselor adds one factual sentence per semester (with permission).** Original detail from The Systems Thinker: triangulation through institutional data the teen already provides. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Retrofit school and court records to build a baseline without burdening the teen.** Original detail from The Analogist: pull existing data on attendance, performance, incident history rather than running an intake survey. *—The Analogist*
  * **Three independent vantage points at baseline (school, justice, teen self-report) becomes evidence-registry-aligned.** Original detail from The Futurist: positions program for WWC / SAMHSA expectations within 2-3 years. *—The Futurist*
  * **Embed baseline collection into the first month of mentor conversations, not an intake interview.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate: data collection becomes part of relationship building rather than re-traumatizing assessment. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Two personas argued against monthly micro-surveys — concentrate touchpoints rather than spread them.** Survey-fatigue concern is named most sharply by Pragmatist (every-6-months pulse, not quarterly) and Futurist (3-month, 9-month, 12-month).
  * **3-4 question pulse every 6 months administered by a neutral third party (school counselor).** Original detail from The Pragmatist: three items — "Do you feel heard?", "Has [mentor] helped you think through something that mattered?", "Would you keep meeting if you could?" *—The Pragmatist*
  * **15-minute teen check-ins at 3-month, 9-month, 12-month marks; mentors track only their own reflections between these.** Original detail from The Futurist: aligns with digital-health-platform pattern of minimal, high-signal touchpoints. *—The Futurist*

* **A "light touch" is not light if it still encodes the assessment frame.** The Devil's Advocate is the load-bearing dissenter here: even a quarterly survey can shift the relationship toward evaluation. The implication is structural — embed observation in the relationship itself, not search for a smaller surveillance tool.
  * **Even a "lightweight" survey can shift the relationship toward evaluation — "I am here to provide data," not "to be known."** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate: instead, mentors document their own observations in a journal shared with the teen — measurement becomes mutual visibility. *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 67: Instrument selection, weighting, and the pull toward easy proxies that distort mentor effort

**Full question**: What are the observable, measurable proxies for relationship quality over a one-year arc, how should each metric be weighted, and what risk exists that mentors steer effort toward what is easy to count rather than what matters most?

* **Weight relationship-quality leading indicators above 50%; weight easy-to-collect behavioral metrics below 20%.** Five personas commit to a specific weighting in this direction. The architecture is shared: relational signals first (mentor-initiated contact, teen comfort, counselor observation of behavioral shift), behavioral proxies (attendance, GPA) as downstream validation only.
  * **Relational leading indicators weighted 70%, behavioral outcome proxies 30%.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate: tells mentors "the relationship is the intervention; everything else is noise from a healthier relationship." *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Mentor-documented relational moments 50%, school-counselor observable shifts 35%, mentor self-reported satisfaction 15% — explicitly downweighted because too easy to game.** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper: invert the measurement logic, publicly. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Relationship signals 50%, teen-reported teen-identified outcome 30%, organizational metrics (attendance, mentor retention) 20%.** Original detail from The Pragmatist: "the trap is that easy metrics naturally float to 70%+ because they're cheap to collect; weighting them low from the start prevents drift." *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Relationship-quality and trust indicators carry more than 50%; meeting-attendance below 15%.** Original detail from The Futurist: NIH-funded resilience research supports this hierarchy. *—The Futurist*
  * **Tier 1: direct teen report of trust/known-ness; Tier 2: mentor specific-example observation; Tier 3: external corroboration; Tier 4 (lowest weight): attendance/participation as fidelity check only.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker: the hierarchy redirects mentor effort from box-checking toward knowing. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Cap the metric list at three.** Two personas independently flagged severe metric proliferation as a primary failure mode; both prescribe an explicit ceiling.
  * **Allow only three metrics per year, reselected annually — sacred and stable so mentors internalize them as operating principles, not compliance noise.** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Build a constraint into the system: program uses exactly three outcome metrics, never more.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker: prevents drift toward complex, burdensome, low-validity measurement frameworks. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Triangulate every metric across at least two independent sources; mono-source metrics get weighted to zero.** Three personas converge on this discipline as the primary defense against self-report inflation.
  * **Volunteer self-report effort and impact are inflated by 40-60% in published research; pair every mentor self-assessment with teen self-assessment and third-party observation; metrics unsupported by at least two other sources get weighted to zero.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **For each major outcome, use three independent proxies: mentor observation, teen self-report, school counselor input.** Original detail from The Futurist: surfacing where proxies diverge is itself diagnostic. *—The Futurist*
  * **Borrow meteorology's coherence-across-streams approach: measure the same outcome through three independent pathways and look for movement in the same direction.** Original detail from The Analogist. *—The Analogist*

* **Make proxy distortion visible: assume metrics will shape behavior and design that intentionally.** Three personas argue measurement systems should be designed with the deformation in mind — not pretend it isn't happening.
  * **Quarterly "metric distortion risk" log: "Which of our three metrics could a mentor unconsciously optimize for at the expense of the teen's genuine growth?" — name the risk, name the guardrail.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Publish the weighting logic and the evidence behind it: "We weight this at X% because research shows…" — transparency lets you adjust midstream and prevents the weights from calcifying.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Quarterly "what we measured vs. what we learned" chart shown to mentors — if attendance is hit but trust statements are flat, name the pull and discuss it.** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Measurement equity audit by teen profile.** The Devil's Advocate alone surfaces this — distinct enough to preserve as a separate insight: what gets measured shapes who gets served. If outcomes cluster by referral source or family-stability profile, the measurement system is biasing selection.
  * **Disaggregate all metrics early in year one by teen profile (school referral vs. JJ referral, first teen for this mentor vs. repeat, family stability level); if outcomes cluster by profile, you've discovered the measurement system is biasing selection — adjust immediately.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Productive tension to hold: capture struggle, not satisfaction.** The Analogist surfaces a distinctive reframe — measure what mentors *resist* measuring, weight for tension rather than harmony.
  * **Mentor writes one paragraph per month of something surprising the teen said or did; the act of articulating surprise forces presence; generic paragraphs reveal disengagement.** Original detail from The Analogist: "darkest path" / "music for tension, not harmony" framings — capture instances of productive tension navigated, not instances of smooth satisfaction. *—The Analogist*

### Question 68: Baseline data, comparison strategy, and alignment with evidence-registry trajectories

**Full question**: Without a control group, attributing program impact to outcomes like chronic absenteeism (currently 35%) is methodologically difficult. What baseline data should be collected, what comparison strategy gives the most defensible signal, and should design align with What Works Clearinghouse / SAMHSA trajectories?

* **Use a delayed-enrollment waitlist as both comparison group and equity intervention.** Four personas converge on the waitlist as the cleanest causal-inference design that doesn't withhold service permanently — demand exceeds supply naturally, so the wait is finite.
  * **Recruit 60 teens, mentor 30, put 30 on a waitlist for year 2; compare one-year outcomes — causal inference without withholding service.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Demand will exceed supply (40 mentors, 60+ referrals) — the natural waitlist becomes the comparison cohort, methodologically defensible *and* ethically sound.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Lean baseline + waitlist control comparing attendance trends over 12 months from school data; cost negligible, validity high enough for a $15K grant report.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Within-person comparison as a fallback when no waitlist is available — each teen as their own control across baseline, 3, 6, 12 months.** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Collect a deliberately lean baseline — only what's actionable; prefer existing institutional data over new intake surveys.** Five personas converge on minimizing the intake burden, with specific lists.
  * **Three-to-four data points only: teen's self-identified goal, current school attendance from school record, one mentor observational prompt.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Four baseline points: school attendance (last semester), juvenile-justice referral status, teen's adult-connection self-report, mentor's stated motivation.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker: anything more feeds the metric-creep problem. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Pull baseline from school and court records that already exist — no intake survey burden on the teen.** Original detail from The Analogist: "baseline is ready-made." *—The Analogist*
  * **Embed baseline into the first month of mentor conversations, not a discrete intake interview.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate: prevents replicating the assessment trauma teens have already experienced. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Lead-indicator baseline only — "Does this teen have trusted adults? Does the teen report loneliness? Can the teen identify one person at school who knows them?"** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper: cheaper, faster, more predictive of match success than a retrospective GPA pull. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Align with What Works Clearinghouse standards now, but as a downstream consideration — not as a constraint that distorts the program.** All seven personas engage the registry-alignment question, but with sharp variation. The pragmatic consensus is to plan for it, not bend to it.
  * **Hire a research consultant ($5K in year one) to map data collection to WWC standards — collect baselines on validated short instruments, not custom ones.** Original detail from The Analogist. *—The Analogist*
  * **Start with WWC Tier 2 design (matched historical cohort or waitlist), plan to scale to Tier 3 in year two or three.** Original detail from The Pragmatist: send a one-page evaluation protocol to the funder 30 days after launch — shows rigor, invites feedback, prevents year-2 surprises. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Design for *local learning* first; map to WWC / SAMHSA after two years of data.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker: organizations that design for registry alignment first often miss the signals that matter locally. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Build in measurement of outcomes already defined by the registries (chronic absenteeism, school engagement, resilience) — doesn't constrain the program but signals readiness to contribute to evidence base.** Original detail from The Futurist. *—The Futurist*
  * **WWC framework can distort small faith-based programs (forces randomization, treats mentors as intervention vehicles) — propose a SAMHSA replication-study framework instead, document the program model, allow other organizations to compare findings.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate: the alternative evidence-bar that fits the context. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Submit a narrative-based theory-of-change framework to registries as an emerging practice with leading indicators; be transparent about limits.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate: funders who understand the community will respect honesty more than inflated claims. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Contact WWC reviewers early: "If we wanted to be registrable, what would you minimally need?" Design toward minimum requirements, not by trying to exceed them.** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Three personas endorsed matched-historical-cohort; the Devil's Advocate refines this to a synthetic-control approach across multiple comparable neighborhoods.** Convergence on the *form* of the comparison; divergence on rigor.
  * **Match a prior-year cohort on school, age, chronic-absenteeism status, JJ involvement; document where the cohorts differ.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Compare to a matched historical cohort post-hoc from school and justice administrative data; supplement with qualitative comparison.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate: keeps integrity with the community while losing some rigor. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Five-year-prior matched cohort (same school, similar demographics) — name the assumption openly.** Original detail from The Analogist. *—The Analogist*
  * **Synthetic-control across three similar non-mentoring neighborhoods is methodologically sharper than a single matched historical cohort; "matched historical cohort" is methodologically weak and looks like avoidance of rigor.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate: refinement and partial dissent. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Two personas independently named bringing in a university research partner now, not in year two.**
  * **Local university research team (masters student + faculty advisor) designs valid instruments and provides access in exchange for thesis material.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Identify a local university evaluator or evaluation firm at launch (even pro bono) to implement baseline collection and comparison design correctly.** Original detail from The Futurist. *—The Futurist*

* **Plan a phased funding model that matches measurement progression to year — and negotiate the three-year arc with the funder upfront.** The Devil's Advocate alone surfaces this; preserved as a load-bearing single-persona insight.
  * **Year 1 = "relationship and trust building," Year 2 = "leading indicators," Year 3 = "long-term outcomes" — communicate upfront that some impacts won't be visible in year one and request a three-year funding commitment.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 69: Match dissolution feedback loop and selection bias in the public success story

**Full question**: What structured debrief protocol should the organization apply to dissolved matches so findings are systematically fed back into screening, training, and matching logic — and what safeguards ensure youth whose stories are shared have genuinely consented and that program communications do not create reputational pressure to present a rosier picture than the data supports?

* **Treat every dissolved match as the program's most valuable diagnostic data; conduct a structured no-blame debrief within two weeks.** All seven personas converged on this. The protocols converge on form (separate conversations with mentor, teen, coordinator; timing within two weeks; structured prompts; pattern analysis quarterly).
  * **NTSB accident-investigation framing: no-blame, structured debrief; same questions every time; coded for patterns across 5-10 dissolutions.** Original detail from The Analogist. *—The Analogist*
  * **Mandatory case conference within one week, bringing mentor, teen's counselor, coordinator, and (optionally) teen — not to blame but to extract learning.** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **20-minute structured conversation within two weeks; one-page dissolution note (probable cause, early warning signs, one specific change to screening or training); 2-3 anonymized dissolution stories surfaced in the next mentor cohort training.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Three-person learning conversation; output is a one-page implication summary fed back into matching, training, or screening logic; running list reviewed quarterly.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Detailed feedback gathered separately from both parties with neutral facilitators; specific questions about preceding weeks, training gaps, miscalibrated matching, venue constraints; findings feed every system.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Confidential dissolution-debrief database; quarterly synthesis; pattern findings feed directly into training and screening (e.g., "mentors without childcare support have 40% higher dissolution rates").** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Treat repeated dissolution patterns as urgent diagnostic information, not program failure; pattern surfaces structural problems (cohort training gaps, referral-source misalignment) before next cohort.** Original detail from The Futurist. *—The Futurist*

* **Three personas explicitly noted: dissolution learning must close the loop to *screening*, not just training.** A common implementation gap: programs collect dissolution data but only act on it through training. Distinguishing this as a separate insight.
  * **Dissolution insights become new screening questions — e.g., "Will this mentor be able to tolerate being misunderstood by a teen from a different background?"** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Dissolution-by-design-phase metric — when did the match end? — reveals which subsystem failed (screening, matching, ongoing support, or transition planning) and where to invest reinforcement.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Pattern detection at the cohort level: if three mentors from one volunteer cohort experience early dissolution, investigate before the next cohort.** Original detail from The Futurist. *—The Futurist*

* **Intentional inversion of attention — debrief the messiest dissolutions deepest, not just the cleanest matches.** The Analogist alone surfaces this with two distinct framings. Single-persona insight, preserved because it reverses the natural attention-allocation pattern.
  * **UX "darkest path" testing: intentionally debrief the 2-3 most difficult match dissolutions in depth — the cleaned-up successes look great in reports but teach nothing; the difficult cases show where selection, training, and support are brittle.** Original detail from The Analogist. *—The Analogist*
  * **Lessons-Learned / military After-Action Review format: all youth stories (including dissolutions) get written as "What were we trying to do? What actually happened? Why? What will we do differently?" — shifts the organizational narrative from "we are great at this" to "we are learning from everything."** Original detail from The Analogist. *—The Analogist*

* **Separate teen consent for storytelling from program-participation consent; teen assent must be active, ongoing, granular, and revocable.** All seven personas converged on this. The mechanism varies (consent-at-publication, consent micro-renewal three months before publication, teen as co-author, anonymity as default, teen edit rights), but the principle is unanimous: guardian consent at intake is not enough.
  * **Consent micro-renewal three months before any story publication: ask the teen directly: "Is this how you want to be known? Are there details we should change? Would you rather we didn't tell this story?"** Original detail from The Analogist. *—The Analogist*
  * **Active, ongoing, granular consent, revocable at any time — separate from program participation consent.** Original detail from The Futurist: aligns with emerging trauma-informed and equity-centered practice. *—The Futurist*
  * **Story-sharing consent obtained from both teen and guardian, with anonymity as default; teen writes their own narrative if they want to share.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate: changes the power dynamic from program-documenting-teen to teen-choosing-disclosure. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Stories shared publicly at match end (not during), with explicit teen consent; declining is unremarkable and unquestioned.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate: serves the teen, not the reverse. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Tiered consent — teens under 15 or from JJ referral must have option to review and edit the story; teen as co-author, not subject.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Annual binary consent question with three options: yes / no / yes-but-not-real-name.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Tiered consent — guardian consent for program participation, separate teen-only consent for any public storytelling.** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Five personas named the same dynamic: structural defenses are needed against the natural pull toward rosier-than-true narratives.** Faith-based and donor-facing contexts create predictable narrative pressure; programs need pre-built mechanisms that survive the social pressure.
  * **Third-party evaluator (low-cost grad student) audits reported outcomes against raw data quarterly, submits confidential report to board identifying any framing that misrepresents outcomes.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Confidential story archive captures the full spectrum (transformational, steady-progress, stabilized-after-rough-start, dissolved); board-level requirement that any data presentation includes match-dissolution rates, unmet needs, systemic barriers; explicit governance rule that pressure to hide negative data is reportable.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Program-health metrics (relationship quality, mentor burnout, dissolution reasons) are kept separate from impact metrics (GPA, attendance, recidivism) — internal scrutiny vs. external framing — and the impact metrics are framed conservatively.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Pre-publication audit: if 60% of matches are steady or positive and 40% dissolve or plateau, public narrative reflects that ratio — share a story of a match that didn't work out as often as a triumph.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Counter-narrative as deliberate practice: deliberately produce a "counter-narrative" featuring a match that took months to establish trust, a mentor who struggled, a teen whose progress was invisible until month 10.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Separate raw internal data from external storytelling explicitly — congregational updates and foundation reports include dissolution rates and ending-related insights, not just success stories.** Original detail from The Futurist. *—The Futurist*

### Question 70: What if we measured outcomes by what mentors learned

**Full question**: What if the program only measured what mentors learned and how mentors changed? What would it mean if the 40 congregation adults became significantly more capable of sitting with discomfort, navigating unfamiliar institutional systems, and tolerating ambiguity? What does inverting the measurement direction reveal?

* **All seven personas saw value in measuring mentor learning; six explicitly said pursue it as a parallel-track outcome rather than a replacement for teen outcomes.** This is the pivotal "and not instead" constraint that kept the inversion from creating a different kind of failure mode.
  * **Add — don't replace — a mentor-learning measurement stream alongside teen outcomes; the funder didn't fund adult development.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Parallel measurement system: mentor reflections on misconceptions corrected, capacity for discomfort, institutional navigation, social-network expansion.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Pre/post 5-point instrument administered to mentors and a waitlist control: "I can tolerate prolonged discomfort when a teen is processing conflict"; "I understand the barriers teens in this neighborhood face." Likely 1-2 point improvement vs. near-zero in control.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Pre-post comparison of mentor's articulation of systemic barriers, blind spots, institutional navigation, tolerance for ambiguity — pattern across 30+ mentors becomes evidence of community capacity-building.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Apprenticeship model: master deepens understanding by having to explain it; quarterly mentor-reflection prompt: "What has this teen taught me about my own assumptions, my own resilience, my own capacity to sit with difficulty?"** Original detail from The Analogist. *—The Analogist*
  * **Asset-based and reciprocal mentorship trajectory: track mentor outcomes explicitly using same relational indicators applied to teens.** Original detail from The Futurist: positions program as community transformation, not youth intervention. *—The Futurist*
  * **Inverted measurement reveals adult capacity-building as the real intervention: "What is one assumption I held about this teen that was wrong?"** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Five personas argued this reframe also recruits a different volunteer pool: saviors → learners.** A second-order benefit — recruiting language and program framing change when mentor learning is a stated outcome.
  * **Recruit with language about willingness to be unsettled — measure mentor learning through structured interviews where they reflect on moments they felt challenged or surprised.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Reframe public messaging: "We pair caring adults with teens who challenge us all to grow" — attracts curious-about-own-growth volunteers, not just want-to-help volunteers.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Frame mentor learning as the program's *real* output to funders: durable community capacity, not individual servicing.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **"40 adults from this community became fluent in the world of high-poverty urban schooling, juvenile justice systems, and the specific barriers facing teens" — adult literacy as community asset, with pre-post knowledge measurement.** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Reciprocal-learning narrative includes mentor stories alongside teen stories in foundation reports — increasingly resonates with funders and aligns with equity-centered evaluation.** Original detail from The Futurist. *—The Futurist*

* **Single-persona caution: inverting the measurement direction risks tokenizing the teen — repositioning the teen as a teaching object for adult development.** The Devil's Advocate alone names this. Distinct enough to preserve as a separate insight; otherwise the reframe risks a different paternalism.
  * **If the program narrative becomes "the church members learned compassion from the teens," the teen is repositioned as a teaching object for adult development. Mitigate: ensure the teen learning system is equally robust and equally publicized; include teens in evaluation design.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 71: What if a hundred successful matches changed the neighborhood's social physics

**Full question**: What outcomes would you measure if the unit of transformation were the community rather than the individual? How would you design the program differently if that were the goal?

* **Five personas converged on network-density and bridging-capital metrics — measure the relational infrastructure, not individual outcomes.**
  * **Weak-ties and structural-holes framing: how many new adult-teen connections; how much cross-system communication; network analysis tools show whether connective tissue is forming.** Original detail from The Analogist. *—The Analogist*
  * **Social-bridge construction: how many teens previously isolated from institutional adults now have at least one additional institutional relationship; how many mentors who previously had no relationships with teens from high-poverty schools now do.** Original detail from The Constraint Flipper. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Network mapping pre and post: "Name up to 5 people from different ages/groups you trust." Cross-age trust as the community-level signal.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Bridging capital across community divides; geometric outcomes are increasingly recognized in community-development literature.** Original detail from The Futurist. *—The Futurist*
  * **Dyad → overlapping dyads → emergent network unit-of-analysis; by year three if 40 mentors created 100+ pairings, network density becomes the leading indicator.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*

### Question 73: Outcomes as lagging indicators and the need for leading proxies

  * **Operational target: mentor initiates 3+ weeks per month; detectable by week 3 and predicts month-12 outcomes far better than attendance.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
