# Summary: Outcome Measurement, Evidence, and Program Learning

**Central Tension**: The teens this program serves have already been processed, assessed, and surveyed by schools and juvenile justice — and any measurement system the program builds on top of that risks becoming one more institutional voice telling the teen they are a case to be tracked rather than a person to be known.

---

## Executive Summary

Measurement here is not a tool for proving impact — it is itself an intervention. The question is not "what should we measure?" but "what intervention do we want our measurement system to be?" Surveillance-pattern measurement reproduces the very institutional posture the program is trying to outgrow; relational-pattern measurement deepens the relationship the program is trying to build. The architectural move that follows from this — convergent across nearly every persona — is to shift the primary measurement instrument from teen-administered surveys to brief, structured mentor reflection (voice memos, weekly observation prompts, dignity-grids that separate "what I did" from "what I noticed"), with school counselors providing the foundation-facing behavioral data the teen already supplies to the school system. This is not a lighter version of the existing measurement model; it is a different model.

Within that architecture, the leading indicators that matter — detectable in weeks rather than years — are mentor-initiated contact frequency, the relational asymmetry flip (when the teen begins initiating contact and asking for advice), and the conversation shift from logistics to meaning. All seven personas converge on these. Lagging outcomes (attendance, GPA, JJ compliance) follow from a healthy relationship; they are validation, not the engine. A leading-indicator dashboard updated monthly with risk-stratification ("on track / building / at risk") allows early support of struggling matches — but only after a wait-and-observe window of approximately six months prevents the program from intervening in pairs that are simply in their early cooling phase.

Year-1 baseline should be lean (3-4 actionable data points, mostly retrieved from existing school and court records rather than collected through new intake surveys), with a delayed-enrollment waitlist serving double duty as comparison cohort and equity intervention. Match dissolution is the program's single richest data source — every dissolution gets a structured no-blame debrief within two weeks, fed back through screening and matching logic, not just training. Year-3 outcomes are the teen's own narrative, captured in their voice; persistence of the relationship past formal closure is itself a primary outcome.

The structural defenses required throughout — quarterly third-party audit of reported outcomes, deliberate counter-narrative practice, separate-from-parents teen consent for any public storytelling, and granular revocable cross-system data consent — exist because faith-based and donor-facing contexts predictably create rosier-than-true narrative pressure. Without pre-built mechanisms, the program will drift toward the wrong measurement equilibrium not through bad faith but through ordinary social pressure.

## Key Themes

**Architectural reframe (categorical):** Mentor observation is the primary measurement instrument; teen-administered surveys are the failure mode. This is not a tweak; it is a categorical reframe of who is being measured and how. Measurement runs through the mentor's eyes, with the teen as a co-designer of what is tracked rather than a subject of assessment. Measurement is itself an intervention — what intervention do we want it to be?

### The teen is the owner of their own story
Convergent across five personas in the cross-system question and reinforced throughout: any synthesized view of the teen's progress belongs to the teen, not to the program, not to the school, not to the foundation. This applies to data-sharing architecture (year-end summary returned to the teen as a gift; teen as curator of their own narrative review at month 6 and month 12), to consent (separate teen-only consent for any public storytelling, granular and revocable), and to year-3 outcome reporting (teen voice authored, not adult-mediated). The shift from program-documenting-teen to teen-choosing-disclosure is a power redistribution disguised as a measurement decision.

### Mentor observation is the primary instrument; teen surveys are the failure mode
A six-of-seven architectural convergence — what makes this a theme rather than just an answer is that it inverts the unit of measurement. The teen is noticed, not assessed. The mentor's structured reflection is the data; the teen's experience is the relationship deepening. This pattern recurs at every question level (year-1 baseline embedded in early conversations rather than intake survey; year-3 teen-voice rather than program-administered survey; cross-system data routed through mentor as one-sentence-summary rather than raw data integration) and is what holds the architecture together against funder pressure for harder metrics.

### Relationship-quality leading indicators outweigh behavioral proxies — and proxy distortion must be designed for, not denied
Five-persona convergence on weighting (relational signals 50-70% of total weight; attendance and meeting completion below 15-20%). What gets measured shapes effort — that is documented across multiple lines of work — so the program must publish weighting logic, run quarterly distortion-risk logs, and triangulate every metric across at least two independent sources to prevent the 40-60% volunteer self-report inflation that is well-documented in published research. The cap-at-three-metrics constraint, named independently by two personas, is a structural defense against metric proliferation.

### Match dissolution is the program's most valuable diagnostic data
Universal seven-persona convergence on a structured no-blame debrief within two weeks of any match ending, with NTSB / military After-Action Review framings to fix the no-blame norm. The dissolution data feeds back into screening (not just training) — three personas independently named this loop-closure as the typical implementation gap. A "dissolution-by-design-phase" timing metric reveals which subsystem failed (screening, matching, ongoing support, or transition planning). And five personas converged on the structural defenses required against the rosier-than-true narrative pressure that faith-based and donor-facing contexts predictably create — third-party quarterly audit, full-spectrum confidential story archive, board-level requirement to surface dissolution rates in any data presentation, deliberate counter-narrative as practice.

### Community-level outcomes emerge — but only when programs are visible and porous
A five-persona convergence on network-density metrics (weak-tie formation, bridging capital, social-cohesion survey at year-1 and year-3), with a design implication that pairs operating in private don't change neighborhood physics. The Devil's-Advocate-anchored counter-test is load-bearing here: community-level outcomes are hard to attribute, expensive to measure rigorously, and slide into magical thinking unless rigorously defined with concrete operational targets — and there is a real risk of using teens as props in a community-development story if the program optimizes for community visibility at the expense of individual teen needs.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- **Replace the planned teen intake survey with a lean retrofitted-baseline approach.** Pull current attendance from school records, JJ status from referral source, capture teen's self-identified goal in conversation during the first month, and document mentor's stated motivation. Three-to-four data points; no new instrument.
- **Hire a $5K research consultant in year one to map measurement to WWC Tier 2 standards and SAMHSA replication-study framework.** Document design choices in a one-page evaluation protocol; send to the funder within 30 days of launch.
- **Set up the mentor structured-reflection instrument.** Pick one of: weekly three-prompt template ("What surprised me? Where did we get stuck? What did I learn about this teen?"), monthly two-minute voice memo, or monthly mentor-initiated-contact log. Pilot with the first 10 mentors before standardizing.
- **Engage a pro bono privacy lawyer to map FERPA, JJ confidentiality, and faith-based program-specific privacy rules** before designing cross-system data-sharing — what is legally permissible determines the architecture.
- **Negotiate three-year measurement portfolio with the foundation.** One hard outcome, one mid-range outcome, one narrative outcome. Communicate that some impacts will not be visible in year one. Request a three-year funding commitment or structured re-evaluation.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- **Establish the leading-indicator dashboard with three indicators tracked monthly:** mentor-initiated contact (operational target 3+ weeks per month), teen self-reported felt-known-ness (1-10 scale, 30 seconds quarterly), and conversation-depth shift from logistics to meaning. Risk-stratify matches at month 3.
- **Establish the structured dissolution debrief protocol with one-page implication note** fed back through screening, training, and matching logic — not just training. Capture dissolution-by-design-phase timing metric.
- **Build separate-from-parents teen consent process for any storytelling.** Granular, revocable, with anonymity as default; teen as co-author rather than subject.
- **Set up quarterly third-party evaluator audit** of reported outcomes against raw data — confidential report to the board that catches reframing pressure before it calcifies.
- **Set up data-sharing agreements with school district and JJ caseworkers** under the discrete-consent / annual-refresh / data-sunset architecture; assign a "data steward" role to translate across systems without centralizing data.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- **Conduct a leading-indicator validation study by year 2** — measure leading indicators against actual lagging outcomes before treating them as actionable program signals (Campbell's Law). Wait-and-observe window of six months prevents over-reactive intervention.
- **Implement year-3 narrative-as-outcome with longitudinal partnership.** Hire a $2K-$3K oral-history journalist or graduate student to conduct 8-10 recorded interviews; pair year-1 teen voice with year-3 teen voice; commission a year-3 counter-narrative non-completion story alongside success stories.
- **Run a community-level cohesion survey at year-1 baseline and year-3 followup** — three items: "Do you know any teens in this neighborhood by name? Would you intervene if you saw one in danger? Do you believe adults here care about young people?"
- **Consider a parallel mentor-learning measurement stream** — pre/post mentor articulation of systemic barriers, blind spots, institutional navigation, tolerance for ambiguity — as community-capacity outcome alongside (not instead of) teen outcomes. Recruit subsequent cohorts with "Are you willing to be unsettled?" framing.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- Trust-based philanthropy and equity-centered evaluation are real trends — funders are increasingly willing to accept structured mentor observation as valid evidence if it is honest and structured.
- A natural waitlist (40 mentors, 60+ referrals) provides the cleanest causal-inference design without withholding service.
- Existing school and JJ administrative data eliminate most intake-survey burden — baseline is already collected by other systems.
- A parallel mentor-learning track positions the program as community-capacity-building, not just youth-servicing — durable, replicable, and harder for funders to refute than fragile teen outcome claims.
- The natural waitlist becomes the fairness-and-rigor case simultaneously: equity intervention plus comparison cohort.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- Faith-based and donor-facing contexts predictably create rosier-than-true narrative pressure — without structural defenses (third-party audit, full-spectrum story archive, board-level reporting requirement, deliberate counter-narrative), the program will drift through ordinary social pressure, not bad faith.
- Volunteer self-report effort and impact are inflated by 40-60% in published research; mono-source metrics will mislead unless triangulated and weighted accordingly.
- Adding mentoring data to school behavioral data and JJ compliance data creates a more comprehensive surveillance apparatus on teens already over-surveilled — the technical design must avoid intensifying the surveillance the program is trying to interrupt.
- Community-level outcomes are difficult to attribute, expensive to measure rigorously, and become magical thinking unless defined with concrete operational targets — and there is a real risk of using teens as props in a community-transformation story.
- Leading indicators not validated against actual lagging outcomes will lead to program churn and misallocation of resources — Campbell's Law applies, especially in the first six months when pairs may be in early cooling that bonds later.
- WWC randomization requirements may force shrinking the program or treating mentors as intervention vehicles — a measurement bar that distorts the program rather than improves it.

**Trade-offs**:
- Methodological rigor (waitlist, validated instruments, registry alignment) vs. intake burden on teens already heavily assessed by schools and JJ — the program must hold both, primarily by routing rigor through institutional data and mentor observation rather than teen-administered instruments.
- Light-touch measurement (low surveillance, high trust) vs. funder-evidence demands (numbers, comparisons, registry alignment) — softening this trade-off requires negotiating a portfolio of three measurement endpoints upfront, including narrative as legitimate evidence.
- Mentor-transformation framing (community capacity-building) vs. funder expectation that the program is about teen outcomes — the parallel-track-not-replacement constraint resolves this, but only if the mentor-learning narrative does not eclipse the teen-learning narrative and inadvertently tokenize teens as teaching objects for adult development.
- Cross-system triangulation power (rich diagnostic information) vs. teen-surveillance cost (already over-observed by other systems) — the design move that holds both is routing the synthesized view back to the teen as their tool, not the system's.
- Leading-indicator responsiveness (intervene early when matches struggle) vs. wait-and-observe discipline (don't disrupt pairs in their early cooling phase) — six-month observation window before intervention is the consensus middle path.
