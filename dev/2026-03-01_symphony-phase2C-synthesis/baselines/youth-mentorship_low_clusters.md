## Cluster 1: Safety, Screening & Mandatory Reporting Protocols

**Description:** The safeguarding infrastructure — volunteer screening (background checks and beyond), mandatory reporting protocols, early-warning monitoring for deteriorating matches, liability insurance structures, and incident response. Also covers the core tension between protocol rigor and relational authenticity: rigid safety systems can filter out effective mentors, rupture trust at the moment of disclosure, and push mentors into surveillance roles that undermine the program's purpose. Spans risk-stratification (Analyst), skeptical pressure-testing (DA, Questioner), and systemic boundary analysis (ST).
**Question count:** 14
**Contributing personas:** Analyst (3), Devil's Advocate (4), Questioner (4), Systems Thinker (3)
**Primary dimension:** Analytical
**Sample questions (up to 5):**
- [Analyst Q1] Risk stratification framework — defensible, evidence-based volunteer screening that balances safety with inclusivity; disqualifying criteria with predictive validity vs. liability-driven criteria.
- [Analyst Q2] Failure mode analysis from programs with serious incidents — common failure points in safeguarding protocols, early warning systems, response mechanisms.
- [Devil's Advocate Q2] Dangerous mismatching — subtle incompatibilities (stereotype reinforcement, trauma triggers, inappropriate emotional dependency) that background checks miss; real-time monitoring and early-warning indicators.
- [Devil's Advocate Q4] Legal liability beyond background checks — insurance structures, incident response protocols, and legal frameworks when things go wrong; balancing protection with authenticity.
- [Questioner Q7] Background checks give false confidence — safeguards for mentors who pass screenings but lack emotional maturity, cultural competence, or boundaries.

## Cluster 2: Matching, Relationship Trajectory & Exit

**Description:** How mentor-mentee pairs are formed, sustained, and gracefully ended — matching variables and their predictive validity, early signals of relationship success/failure, boundary permeability, mismatch exit pathways, and the question of whether rigid matching protocols vs. more fluid multi-encounter approaches produce better relationships. Includes the identity of the "right" mentor pool (congregation adults vs. young adults vs. peer-to-peer) and the meaning-of-match itself (demographic similarity vs. complementary pairing, one-on-one vs. peer networks). Also covers exit and graduation design.
**Question count:** 13
**Contributing personas:** Analyst (3), Appreciative Inquirer (1), Audience Advocate (1), Questioner (6), Systems Thinker (1), Devil's Advocate (1)
**Primary dimension:** Strategic
**Sample questions (up to 5):**
- [Analyst Q7] Matching criteria hierarchy — which variables (demographics, life experience, communication style, interests) most strongly correlate with longevity; optimize for compatibility vs. complementary pairings.
- [Analyst Q8] Early intervention indicators — observable signals in the first 6-8 weeks most reliably predicting relationship success/failure; systematic check-in mechanisms.
- [Analyst Q12] Exit transition planning — graduation and exit processes for pairs completing one-year commitment; distinguishing natural dissolution from beneficial ongoing connection.
- [Questioner Q11] What makes us think we can predict successful matches? — speed-mentoring approach where teens meet multiple mentors and provide input.
- [Questioner Q12] Demographic similarity assumption — whether cross-gender, cross-cultural, cross-background matches create more growth than pairing like with like.

## Cluster 3: Program Structure, Commitment Length & Meeting Design

**Description:** The temporal and structural frame of the program itself — the one-year commitment assumption, volunteer attrition / graduated-exit protocols, weekly-meeting cadence, public vs. private meeting spaces, alternative cycles (3-6 month intensives, seasonal cycles, peer mentoring, 18-24 month extended commitments). Also the volunteer lifecycle economics (recruitment → onboarding → service → departure) and the cost-structure tradeoffs between continuous recruitment investment vs. retention investment. Pulls together scheduling questions that are structural rather than safety-related.
**Question count:** 8
**Contributing personas:** Analyst (2), Devil's Advocate (2), Questioner (4)
**Primary dimension:** Strategic
**Sample questions (up to 5):**
- [Devil's Advocate Q1] Volunteer attrition breaking mentor bonds — one-year commitment assumes volunteers follow through; graduated-exit protocols and co-mentor arrangements buffering youth against abandonment.
- [Devil's Advocate Q9] One-year timeframe as artificial constraint — research shows meaningful relationships take 18-24 months; alternative structures (seasonal cycles with natural on/off ramps).
- [Questioner Q1] One-year commitment length assumption — shorter intensive cycles (3-6 months) with clear milestones and natural exit points.
- [Questioner Q13] Formal "breakup" process — structured check-ins at 3, 6, 9 months where either party can opt out without stigma.
- [Questioner Q15] Weekly meetings frequency/structure — flexible contact with texting between in-person meetings, longer sessions less frequently, informal gatherings.

## Cluster 4: Mentee Experience, Belonging & Voice

**Description:** The lived experience of teens in the program — building trust with wary youth who distrust authority adults, accessibility (transportation, scheduling, language, hidden costs), voluntary-vs-mandatory referral dynamics, genuine agency and consent, youth voice in program design, "at-risk" labeling and narrative framing that either defines or dignifies mentees, and serving teens who are actively struggling rather than just ideal participants. The equity-and-dignity core of the brainstorm, carried almost entirely by Audience Advocate.
**Question count:** 11
**Contributing personas:** Audience Advocate (10), Questioner (1)
**Primary dimension:** Human-centered
**Sample questions (up to 5):**
- [Audience Advocate Q1] Building trust with wary youth — structuring initial meetings to recognize teens arriving with profound distrust of authority adults; the emotional journey from first contact through relationship formation.
- [Audience Advocate Q5] Transportation and scheduling realities — logistics for both teens and mentors with unreliable transportation/unpredictable schedules; without creating shame around attendance.
- [Audience Advocate Q6] Beyond the "ideal" participant — serving teens who are actively struggling (inconsistent attendance, emotional dysregulation, immediate crises).
- [Audience Advocate Q9] Voluntary vs. mandatory participation — genuine agency when teens are referred through school counselors or juvenile justice; meaningful consent when they may feel pressured.
- [Audience Advocate Q10] Youth voice in program design — involving mentees in shaping structure, activities, expectations beyond token feedback toward shared decision-making.

## Cluster 5: Mentor Preparation, Support & Wellbeing

**Description:** The mentor-side infrastructure — training philosophy (compliance vs. strengths-based), self-disclosure calibration, cultural/contextual preparation, mentor burnout and vicarious trauma, staff-led vs. peer-led support models, emotional equipping over time, and the question of whether training should frame mentoring as capability-discovery or rule-compliance. Spans appreciative framing (AI), structural-equity concern about cultural competence (AA), risk of mentor trauma/projection (DA), and system-level peer-vs-staff support dynamics (ST).
**Question count:** 7
**Contributing personas:** Appreciative Inquirer (2), Audience Advocate (2), Devil's Advocate (1), Systems Thinker (2)
**Primary dimension:** Human-centered
**Sample questions (up to 5):**
- [Appreciative Inquirer Q10] Training that ignites existing capacity — design as discovery process helping mentors recognize and build on strengths; stories, exercises, reflections unlocking innate capacity.
- [Audience Advocate Q3] Mentor vulnerability and approachability — training mentors to share appropriate aspects of their own stories, especially with teens who experienced adult betrayal.
- [Audience Advocate Q4] Cultural and contextual understanding — preparing mentors for specific cultural, economic, neighborhood contexts; preventing well-meaning mentors from reinforcing "at-risk" assumptions.
- [Devil's Advocate Q3] Mentor burnout and emotional trauma transfer — supports preventing vicarious trauma and unconscious projection; detecting when a mentor is no longer emotionally equipped.
- [Systems Thinker Q3] Shifting the Burden in mentor support — staff-led intervention vs. peer networks; preventing mentor dependency on staff while ensuring struggling pairs get support.

## Cluster 6: Community, Family & Institutional Ecosystem

**Description:** The relational and institutional web around the mentor-mentee pair — family hesitation and partnership, faith-based hosting assumptions in secular/other-faith contexts, referral pathways from schools and juvenile justice, data privacy across institutional boundaries, parental consent barriers for the most at-risk youth, regulatory-capture risk from carceral-system extension, multi-stakeholder coordination, and community skepticism about outside interventions and past harms. The institutional-context cluster — what it means that this program exists inside a church inside a city with a history inside institutional systems the teens already distrust.
**Question count:** 12
**Contributing personas:** Analyst (2), Audience Advocate (1), Devil's Advocate (5), Systems Thinker (3), Questioner (1)
**Primary dimension:** Strategic
**Sample questions (up to 5):**
- [Analyst Q10] Multi-stakeholder coordination costs — information flow, decision rights, coordination protocols across schools, juvenile justice, families, social services.
- [Audience Advocate Q2] Family hesitation and skepticism — addressing concerns of parents/guardians skeptical of outside help; ensuring they feel like partners rather than subjects.
- [Devil's Advocate Q6] Faith-based assumptions in secular contexts — preventing implicit/explicit proselytizing while staying authentic to host values; when mentor's faith expression makes mentee uncomfortable.
- [Devil's Advocate Q7] Juvenile justice referral paradox — mandatory reporting requirements could undermine trust; reconciling reporting obligations with safe harbor for risky-behavior disclosure.
- [Systems Thinker Q8] School and juvenile justice referral pathways — inheriting goals/metrics from referral institutions; Shifting the Burden where external systems depend on mentorship to address problems they can't solve.

## Cluster 7: Outcomes, Measurement & Impact Attribution

**Description:** How the program demonstrates and measures success — outcome metrics that credibly show impact to funders without distorting relationship-building, attribution methodology when mentees are in multiple interventions simultaneously, short-term vs. multi-year feedback loops, leading indicators vs. trailing outcomes, value quantification, and the measurement-as-burden risk where reporting requirements conflict with the relationship itself. Also asks what gets measured when measurement is reframed as reinforcement of strengths rather than surveillance.
**Question count:** 10
**Contributing personas:** Analyst (2), Appreciative Inquirer (1), Devil's Advocate (1), Systems Thinker (2), Questioner (1), Appreciative Inquirer (1, ripple effects Q7), Appreciative Inquirer (1, 3-year celebration Q5), Appreciative Inquirer (1, mentors' vision Q8)
**Primary dimension:** Analytical
**Sample questions (up to 5):**
- [Analyst Q6] Value quantification framework — metrics credibly demonstrating impact to funders; data collection without unreasonable burdens or perverse incentives.
- [Analyst Q11] Outcome attribution challenge — isolating mentorship effects when mentees are in multiple interventions; contribution analysis rather than causation.
- [Devil's Advocate Q10] Success measurement becoming a burden — reporting requirements conflicting with relationship-building; minimalist metrics capturing genuine impact without perverse incentives.
- [Systems Thinker Q6] Delay structures and delayed feedback loops — multi-year impact timeline vs. quarterly/annual funding cycles; leading indicators that don't incentivize superficial engagement.
- [Appreciative Inquirer Q12] Measurement that energizes — outcome measurement reinforcing strengths-based philosophy; mentors and teens observing their own growth.

## C1 Comparison (Per-Persona vs. Flat Pooling)

C1 (from Phase 2B P1/C1 carryover into Phase 2C) asks whether per-persona clustering — each persona pre-organizing their own questions into clusters before synthesis sees them — helps or hurts cross-persona synthesis vs. flat pooling.

### Pass A: Per-persona clustering preserved

When persona-internal cluster structure is honored before cross-persona synthesis, the Synthesize personas in this dataset present roughly:

- **Analyst:** ~4 internal clusters (safety/risk-framework, economic/lifecycle, matching/monitoring, outcomes/coordination)
- **Appreciative Inquirer:** ~4 internal clusters (peak-moments/assets, community/ripples, mentor-teen-vision, training/matching/measurement)
- **Audience Advocate:** ~3 internal clusters (mentee-trust/access, family/community, voice/framing)
- **Devil's Advocate:** ~4 internal clusters (attrition/mismatching/trauma, liability/power/faith, referral-paradox/consent, timeframe/measurement/scaling/community-skepticism)
- **Questioner:** No persona-supplied cluster headings — questions flow as a flat sequence, though commitment-length (Q1, Q13, Q15), at-risk-labeling (Q2, Q4, Q5), safety-vs-connection (Q6, Q7, Q8, Q9, Q10), matching (Q11, Q12), and peer-mentoring (Q3, Q14) threads are visible.
- **Systems Thinker:** All 12 questions are supplied with bolded-title headings that encode system archetypes (Shifting the Burden, Success to the Successful, Delay structures, Tragedy of the Commons, referral pathways as burden-shifting).

Synthesizing within these persona-supplied frames yields a 7-cluster cross-persona map closely matching the one above (C1-C7). Systems Thinker's archetype labels in particular are *distinctive content* that should survive into the output rather than being stripped as cluster-structural markup — they carry vocabulary no other persona generates (Shifting the Burden appears twice for ST, once in C5 mentor support and once in C6 referral pathways; Success to the Successful for mentor selection in C5; Tragedy of the Commons for mentor time in C5; Delay structures for outcomes in C7).

### Pass B: Flat pooling

Treating all 75 questions as a flat list and clustering bottom-up yields the same 7 themes. The cluster boundaries shift slightly:

- "Matching" and "Program Structure" attach more strongly together under flat pooling (one "Relationship Architecture" super-cluster) because commitment-length and matching-cadence questions both shape the relationship frame; persona-aware clustering keeps them separate because Questioner's commitment-length thread is distinct from the matching thread in source structure.
- AI's 12 questions under flat pooling distribute more heavily into C7 (Outcomes) because three of AI's visioning questions (Q5 three-year, Q7 ripples, Q8 mentor transformation) share outcome-framing DNA. Persona-aware keeps some of them in C5 (Mentor Prep) because AI's internal structure groups them with Q1 (peak moments) and Q4 (teens' stories of adults).
- DA Q8 (parental consent for high-risk youth) is borderline between C4 (Mentee Experience/Voice — the mentee's dignity is implicated) and C6 (Community/Institutional — the parent relationship and institutional distrust). Flat pooling tips toward C6; persona-aware keeps it in C6 under DA's "faith/referral/consent" internal cluster.
- Systems Thinker Q2 (natural stabilizer — family expansion) sits in both C2 (Matching) and C6 (Community) under flat; persona-aware keeps it in C2 because ST's internal framing emphasizes the relationship-trajectory dynamics rather than the family ecosystem itself.

### Difference, Bias Check & SP1 Recommendation

- **Difference:** Marginal — same 7 clusters, boundary differences on 6-8 borderline questions (heavier than food-truck/low because youth-mentorship has more cross-cluster AI questions and a genuinely ambiguous Matching/Mentor-Prep boundary). Both passes agree on which questions are convergent and which are unique. Both passes identify Safety (C1), Matching (C2), and Community/Institutional Ecosystem (C6) as high-density convergence zones.
- **Bias check:** Per-persona clustering preserves Systems Thinker's distinctive archetype vocabulary (Shifting the Burden, Success to the Successful, Delay structures, Tragedy of the Commons) as a genuine signal (not as bias inflating cluster count). AI's four internal clusters do not over-anchor cross-persona structure — flat pooling absorbs them into C2/C5/C7 without distortion. No evidence of cluster inflation from persona-supplied structure.
- **SP1 recommendation:** **Flat pooling for the synthesis step.** With 75 questions and 6 personas, flat pooling produces equivalent cluster quality with a simpler prompt and fewer instructions to track. Two additive instructions specific to this cell: (1) preserve Systems Thinker's distinctive archetype vocabulary (Shifting the Burden, Success to the Successful, Delay structures, Tragedy of the Commons) in output questions where it appears, because that vocabulary is distinctive content, not cluster-structural markup; (2) preserve Audience Advocate's dignity-and-voice framings in C4 questions — 10 of 12 AA questions live in C4 and carry the program's equity lens; flat-pooling risks AA being under-represented because it doesn't heavily converge with other personas in the same cluster.
