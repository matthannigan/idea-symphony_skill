### Cluster C1 — Safety, Screening & Mandatory Reporting Protocols

**Must include (3):**
1. **[Merged from CG1: Anal Q3, Quest Q6, Quest Q8, ST Q12]** How should mandatory reporting protocols be designed so mentors can navigate the moment of reportable disclosure without rupturing the relationship — including calibration between over- and under-reporting, the system dynamic where teens learn not to share and mentors learn not to ask once confidentiality is conditional, and training and protocol design that preserves the teen's experience of the mentor as a safe person even when institutional reporting is legally mandated?
2. **[Merged from CG2: Anal Q1, Quest Q7, ST Q4]** What screening framework balances genuine safety with inclusivity — distinguishing disqualifying criteria with demonstrable predictive validity from liability-driven ones, capturing qualities (emotional maturity, cultural competence, boundary navigation) that criminal records don't reveal, and avoiding the screening-rigor cascade in which increasingly strict criteria filter out community members with personal justice-system experience who might connect most powerfully with at-risk teens?
3. **[CG5 Q A / DA Q2 preserved]** Real-time monitoring of deteriorating mentor-mentee relationships — what early-warning indicators identify subtle incompatibilities that background checks miss (stereotype reinforcement, trauma triggers, inappropriate emotional dependency) before they cause harm, without creating a surveillance environment that undermines the relationship?

**Should include (2):**
4. **[Merged from CG6: Anal Q9, ST Q7]** How should mentor role boundaries be delineated so they enable genuine relationship development while preventing boundary drift — including decision frameworks for gray areas (gift-giving, communication channels, family involvement) and the reinforcing dynamic where mentors who navigate boundaries well earn trust and autonomy while struggling mentors face scrutiny that further undermines their confidence?
5. **[Anal Q2 unique]** Failure mode analysis from programs with serious incidents — common failure points in safeguarding protocols, early warning systems, and response mechanisms; controls most effective at prevention versus detection versus mitigation.

**Optional:**
- **[Quest Q9]** Liability insurance sufficiency — whether real risks (emotional harm, cultural mismatch, failed match dynamics, public reputational damage) are covered by standard policies. *Distinctive; cuttable.*
- **[Quest Q10]** Public-space-only mentoring design — how one-on-one mentorship changes if mentors and mentees never meet privately. *Distinctive provocation; cuttable at low effort.*

### Cluster C2 — Matching, Relationship Trajectory & Exit

**Must include (3):**
1. **[Merged from CG4: Anal Q7, Quest Q11, Quest Q12, AI Q11]** What actually predicts a successful match — including which variables (demographics, life experience, communication style, interests, availability, proximity) empirically correlate with longevity, whether elaborate matching algorithms outperform speed-mentoring approaches where teens meet multiple mentors and provide input, whether demographic similarity or cross-background pairings produce more growth, and what less-visible elements of compatibility ('relational potential') have created powerful mentor-mentee bonds in practice?
2. **[CG5 Q B / Anal Q8 preserved]** What observable signals in the first 6-8 weeks of mentor-mentee interaction most reliably predict long-term relationship success or failure, and what systematic check-in mechanisms would identify struggling pairs early enough for meaningful intervention without surveillance undermining relationship authenticity?
3. **[AA Q11 unique]** Match criteria and mismatch handling — when mentor-mentee matches aren't working, how do we create exit pathways that protect the teen's dignity and sense of worth, avoid reinforcing experiences of rejection or abandonment, and still provide opportunities for successful connections?

**Should include (2):**
4. **[Anal Q12 unique]** Exit transition planning — what data or analytic frameworks help design graduation and exit processes for mentor-mentee pairs completing the one-year commitment, and how to distinguish relationships that should naturally dissolve from those where ongoing connection remains beneficial but outside formal program structures.
5. **[Quest Q14 unique]** Peer mentorship among participating teens — whether mentee-to-mentee relationships create stronger support networks than adult-teen pairs alone, especially around issues adults can't fully understand.

**Optional:**
- **[AI Q6]** Mentorship relationship teens would rave about — what a teen would say to a friend about their mentor beyond "they're nice." *Vision-casting; overlaps CG4 but distinct frame; preserve.*
- **[AI Q3]** 20-year intergenerational relationships in the neighborhood — what natural relationships formed and what can be learned about longevity. *Strengths-based source of matching intelligence; preserve.*
- **[ST Q2]** Natural stabilizer or hidden ceiling — family expansion of successful pairs creates new capacity constraints. *Distinctive systems framing; cuttable.*
- **[Quest Q5]** "Meaningful relationship" culturally specific definition — mentorship around teens' existing relationship models vs. middle-class norms. *Overlaps CG4 and CG7 (power); preserve as relationship-definition question.*

### Cluster C3 — Program Structure, Commitment Length & Meeting Design

**Must include (2):**
1. **[Merged from CG3: DA Q1, DA Q9, Quest Q1, Quest Q13]** Why is the one-year commitment the right temporal frame — given research that meaningful relationships often take 18-24 months, given volunteer attrition risk where quitting mid-year feels like abandonment, and given that shorter intensive cycles (3-6 months) with natural on/off ramps might attract qualified volunteers — and what alternative structures (graduated exits, seasonal cycles, structured 3/6/9-month check-ins with no-stigma opt-out) might better match both youth developmental timelines and mentor capacity?
2. **[Quest Q15 unique]** Weekly meetings frequency and structure — how mentorship might flourish with more flexible contact (texting between in-person meetings, longer sessions less frequently, informal gatherings around shared activities) rather than scheduled weekly meetings.

**Should include (1):**
3. **[Quest Q3 unique]** Adult volunteers from congregation assumption — whether young adult mentors (18-25) who relate more authentically to teens' experiences (even with less life stability) should be recruited alongside or instead of the older pool.

**Optional:**
- **[Anal Q4]** Unit economics of $15K seed grant across background checks, insurance, training, coordination; sustainable cost per pair; scale at which fixed costs dilute. *Useful financial frame; cuttable.*
- **[Anal Q5]** Volunteer replacement and lifecycle costs — continuous recruitment vs. retention investment. *Overlaps CG3 temporally; cuttable.*
- **[DA Q11]** Scaling through volunteers without quality collapse — red-line criteria triggering cap rather than dilute. *Distinctive risk framing; preserve if space allows.*
- **[ST Q9]** Volunteer pool constraints and self-selection — effective mentors being the busiest; constant recruitment treadmill. *System-dynamics framing; preserve.*
- **[AI Q9]** Natural relationship dynamics — program structures facilitating rather than constraining authentic connection; formal requirements to simplify. *Appreciative complement to CG3 merge; preserve.*

### Cluster C4 — Mentee Experience, Belonging & Voice

**Must include (3):**
1. **[Merged from CG7: AA Q9, AA Q10, DA Q5]** How can the program actively redistribute power to youth rather than paying lip service to mutuality — recognizing the amplified adult-child dynamic when mentors are middle-class and mentees are juvenile-justice-involved from high-poverty neighborhoods — including ensuring meaningful consent when participation feels mandated, giving youth real agency over relationship structure/topics/continuation/exit, and involving current and prospective mentees in shaping program structure beyond token feedback?
2. **[Merged from CG8: AA Q12, Quest Q2]** How do we talk about and position this program in ways that acknowledge challenges and risks without defining teens primarily by their "at-risk" status — recognizing the deficit-based frame influences mentor perceptions, shapes relationship dynamics, and affects teens' self-conception — and what language and frameworks support rather than undermine teens' developing identities?
3. **[AA Q1 unique]** Building trust with wary youth — how to structure initial mentor-mentee meetings to recognize that teens referred through juvenile justice or school counselors may arrive with profound distrust of adults in authority positions, and what the emotional journey looks like from first contact through relationship formation.

**Should include (2):**
4. **[AA Q6 unique]** Beyond the "ideal" participant — how the program design serves teens who are actively struggling (inconsistent attendance, emotional dysregulation, immediate crises) rather than just those who can reliably show up and engage as expected.
5. **[AA Q5 unique]** Transportation and scheduling realities — meeting logistics accommodating real-world constraints for both teens and mentors with unreliable transportation or unpredictable work/school schedules, without creating shame around attendance challenges.

**Optional:**
- **[AA Q7]** Language and communication accessibility — limited English, lower literacy, different communication styles; preventing invisible exclusion. *Distinctive accessibility territory; preserve if space.*
- **[AA Q8]** Hidden costs of participation — transportation, time off work, missed opportunities, meeting food; no stigma around financial assistance. *Access-barrier framing; preserve.*
- **[Quest Q4]** Assumptions about what teens actually want — have we asked them directly, or projecting adult priorities. *Meta-question; overlaps CG7; preserve as short standalone.*

### Cluster C5 — Mentor Preparation, Support & Wellbeing

**Must include (3):**
1. **[CG9 Q A / AA Q4 preserved]** How can we prepare mentors to understand and respect the specific cultural, economic, and neighborhood contexts that shape these teens' daily experiences, and what happens when well-meaning mentors inadvertently reinforce assumptions about "at-risk" youth?
2. **[CG9 Q B / DA Q3 preserved]** Working with at-risk teens exposed to trauma, violence, and systemic poverty takes a significant psychological toll — what supports prevent mentors from becoming overwhelmed, experiencing vicarious trauma, or unconsciously projecting their own issues onto mentees, and how can the program detect when a mentor is no longer emotionally equipped to serve effectively?
3. **[CG11 all three preserved / ST Q3 + ST Q5 + ST Q10 preserved distinct]** Three system-archetype framings of mentor support that should survive as distinct questions because merging would strip ST's distinctive vocabulary:
   - **(ST Q3 — Shifting the Burden):** Should struggling mentor-mentee pairs be supported through staff-led intensive intervention or mentor peer networks? Staff-led solutions can create mentor dependency on professional intervention and prevent mentor-to-mentor learning from developing its own reinforcing loop — how can the program design avoid eroding mentor capacity while still ensuring struggling pairs get support?
   - **(ST Q5 — Success to the Successful):** When mentors who demonstrate early success receive recognition, additional resources, and priority placement for new matches, the program becomes dependent on a small group of star mentors rather than building distributed capacity — how can matching and support systems create distributed resilience rather than hero dependency?
   - **(ST Q10 — Tragedy of the Commons):** Mentors have limited relationship capacity, but as the program succeeds multiple stakeholders compete for this scarce resource — how can the program create a shared resource management system for mentor attention and energy, allocating through shared norms and governance rather than market mechanisms or bureaucratic rationing?

**Should include (2):**
4. **[AA Q3 unique / CG10]** Mentor vulnerability and approachability — how to train mentors to share appropriate aspects of their own stories in ways that build genuine connection, particularly when working with teens who may have experienced adult betrayal or inconsistency, and what boundaries protect both parties.
5. **[AI Q10 unique]** Training that ignites existing capacity — rather than framing training as "what you must do" or "what you're not allowed to do," how could we design it as a discovery process helping mentors recognize and build on strengths they already have; what stories, exercises, or reflections unlock their innate capacity to connect.

**Optional:**
- **[AI Q1]** Peak moments of connection — an adult in this community who made a lasting difference in a teenager's life; what made it click, what conditions allowed it to flourish. *Strengths-based raw material for training; preserve.*
- **[AI Q2]** Hidden assets in the volunteer pool of 40 interested adults — unique life experiences/skills not recognized as mentorship assets. *Distinctive strengths-inventory; preserve.*
- **[AI Q4]** Teens' stories of adult support — patterns in what qualities teens describe in adults who "got them." *Mentee-voice method for mentor-prep; preserve.*
- **[ST Q1]** Self-amplifying cycle of mentor success stories and the death-spiral from early setbacks; stabilizing the reinforcing loop. *Systems archetype; distinctive preserve.*

**Floor notice:** C5 carries heavy optional density (4 items) because AI contributes 5 of 12 questions to C5 and ST contributes 4 of 12. The must+should floor of 5 (3 must + 2 should) is strict; optional items provide the buffer against over-compaction.

### Cluster C6 — Community, Family & Institutional Ecosystem

**Must include (3):**
1. **[Merged from CG14: Anal Q10, DA Q7, ST Q8, ST Q11]** How can the program maintain its core youth-centered developmental purpose while managing the inherited expectations of school and juvenile justice referrals — including the Shifting the Burden dynamic where overloaded external systems depend on mentorship to address problems they can't solve, the carceral-system-extension risk where juvenile-justice mandatory reporting turns mentors into surveillance agents, the data-privacy paradox where siloed information creates blind spots but shared information creates legal vulnerability, and coordination costs across schools, juvenile justice, families, and social services?
2. **[DA Q12 preserved per CG15]** Community skepticism and past harms — urban communities with high poverty and juvenile justice involvement have experienced failed interventions, broken promises, and exploitative research from outside organizations; why would this program be different, and how specifically does it demonstrate respect for community knowledge, involve existing community leaders, and avoid repeating patterns of saviorism or extractive service?
3. **[AA Q2 unique]** Family hesitation and skepticism — formal and informal mechanisms addressing parents' and guardians' concerns about outside help; ensuring they feel like partners rather than subjects.

**Should include (1):**
4. **[DA Q6 preserved per CG15]** Faith-based assumptions in secular contexts — how the program prevents implicit or explicit proselytizing while staying authentic to the host organization's values; what happens when a mentor's faith expression makes a mentee uncomfortable.

**Optional:**
- **[DA Q8 preserved per CG15]** Parental consent barriers for high-risk youth — unstable home situations, absent parents, parents who distrust institutions; serving youth who cannot easily obtain consent without creating exploitable loopholes. *Consent-barrier dimension; preserve — if dropped, CG15's equity coverage is reduced.*
