# youth-mentorship — low — Convergence Baseline

**Input:** 75 Synthesize-group questions from `test-data/youth-mentorship_low.md`
**Convergence groups (P):** 15
**Questions in convergence (Q):** 51 of 75 (68%)
**Convergence density:** 68%
**Unique (non-convergent) Synthesize questions:** 24 of 75 (32%)

Convergence here means two or more Synthesize-persona questions address the same concrete concern such that synthesis can either (a) merge them into one richer question that anchors in both source texts, or (b) preserve all distinct voices when the framings differ enough that merging would strip persona-specific value.

Youth-mentorship is a high-convergence topic because the core tensions (safety-vs-connection, authority-vs-trust, reporting-vs-confidence, commitment-vs-flexibility, measurement-vs-relationship) recur across 4-5 personas each, driving convergence density above the food-truck/low benchmark (64%) and well above the space-party/low benchmark (57%).

## Convergence Group 1: Mandatory reporting, trust, and the safety-vs-authentic-connection tension

**Theme:** How mandatory reporting obligations, confidentiality, and safety protocols interact with the trust-building core of the relationship — including the concrete moment of reportable disclosure, the teach-learn loop that causes teens to stop sharing real things, and whether safety rigor can protect youth while also preserving the conditions under which they'll disclose real need.
**Cluster:** C1 (Safety, Screening & Mandatory Reporting Protocols)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q3] Reporting threshold calibration — mandatory reporting protocols distinguishing concerning patterns from normal behaviors; decision trees preventing under-reporting and reflexive over-reporting.
- [Questioner Q6] Safety protocols preventing authentic connection — rigid boundaries and mandatory reporting creating distrust, especially for teens who already distrust authority.
- [Questioner Q8] Mandatory reporting conflicts with relationship trust — how mentors navigate disclosure that triggers reporting; damage at the most vulnerable point.
- [Systems Thinker Q12] Mandatory reporting as system intervention — the disclose/report/rupture dynamic; mentees learn not to share; mentors learn not to ask; designing protocols that preserve relationship safety while fulfilling legal obligations.
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "How should mandatory reporting protocols be designed so that mentors can navigate the moment of reportable disclosure without rupturing the relationship — including the calibration between over- and under-reporting, the system dynamic where teens learn not to share and mentors learn not to ask once they realize confidentiality is conditional, and the specific training and protocol design that preserves the teen's experience of the mentor as a safe person even when institutional reporting is legally mandated?"

## Convergence Group 2: Volunteer screening — background checks, predictive validity, and exclusion of effective mentors

**Theme:** Whether background-check-based screening captures what actually matters (emotional maturity, cultural competence, boundary navigation) and whether rigorous screening filters out the very mentors who could connect most with at-risk teens — particularly community members who have personal experience with justice systems.
**Cluster:** C1 (Safety, Screening & Mandatory Reporting Protocols)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q1] Risk stratification framework — evidence-based screening balancing safety with inclusivity; disqualifying criteria with predictive validity vs. liability-driven.
- [Questioner Q7] Background checks give false confidence — safeguards for mentors passing screenings but lacking emotional maturity, cultural competence, boundaries.
- [Systems Thinker Q4] Screening rigor and the unintended consequence cascade — stricter checks shrink the mentor pool, particularly among community members with justice-system experience; mentors increasingly disconnected from mentees' lived experience.
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "What screening framework balances genuine safety with inclusivity — distinguishing disqualifying criteria with demonstrable predictive validity from those driven by liability fears, capturing qualities (emotional maturity, cultural competence, boundary navigation) that criminal records don't reveal, and avoiding the screening-rigor cascade in which increasingly strict criteria filter out community members with personal justice-system experience who might connect most powerfully with at-risk teens?"

## Convergence Group 3: Volunteer attrition, commitment length, and the one-year assumption

**Theme:** Whether the one-year commitment is the right frame, how the program buffers youth against attrition and abandonment, and what alternative temporal structures (3-6 month cycles, 18-24 month extensions, seasonal cycles) might better match both research on relationship formation and mentor capacity.
**Cluster:** C3 (Program Structure, Commitment Length & Meeting Design)
**Questions:**
- [Devil's Advocate Q1] Volunteer attrition breaking mentor bonds — graduated-exit protocols, co-mentor arrangements buffering youth against abandonment.
- [Devil's Advocate Q9] One-year timeframe as artificial constraint — research showing 18-24 months; alternative seasonal cycles with natural on/off ramps.
- [Questioner Q1] One-year commitment length — shorter intensive cycles (3-6 months) with clear milestones and natural exit points that might not discourage qualified volunteers.
- [Questioner Q13] Formal "breakup" process — structured check-ins at 3, 6, 9 months where either party opts out without stigma.
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "Why is the one-year commitment the right temporal frame — given research that meaningful mentoring relationships often take 18-24 months to generate measurable outcomes, given volunteer attrition risk where quitting mid-year may feel like abandonment to youth, and given that shorter intensive cycles (3-6 months) with natural on/off ramps might attract qualified volunteers who won't commit to a year — and what alternative structures (graduated exits, seasonal cycles, structured 3/6/9-month check-ins with no-stigma opt-out) might better match both youth developmental timelines and mentor capacity?"

## Convergence Group 4: Matching — predictive variables, outcomes, and whether matching is really predictable

**Theme:** Which matching variables actually correlate with relationship success, whether elaborate matching algorithms work better than speed-mentoring approaches where teens provide input, and whether demographic similarity improves outcomes or cross-background matches create more growth.
**Cluster:** C2 (Matching, Relationship Trajectory & Exit)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q7] Matching criteria hierarchy — which variables strongly correlate with longevity; optimize for compatibility vs. complementary pairings.
- [Questioner Q11] Predict successful matches assumption — speed-mentoring approach where teens meet multiple mentors.
- [Questioner Q12] Demographic similarity assumption — cross-gender, cross-cultural, cross-background matches creating more growth.
- [Appreciative Inquirer Q11] Matching chemistry beyond demographics — less visible elements creating powerful bonds; reading for relational potential.
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "What actually predicts a successful match — including which variables (demographics, life experience, communication style, interests, availability, proximity) empirically correlate with relationship longevity, whether elaborate matching algorithms outperform speed-mentoring approaches where teens meet multiple potential mentors and provide input on comfort, whether demographic similarity or deliberate cross-background pairings produce better growth, and what less-visible elements of compatibility ('relational potential') have created powerful mentor-mentee bonds in practice?"

## Convergence Group 5: Early-warning monitoring of struggling pairs (safety-side and relationship-side)

**Theme:** The overlap between detecting dangerous mismatches (harmful dynamics, trauma triggers, inappropriate dependency) and detecting pairs that are failing to click — both require observable signals and systematic check-in mechanisms without creating surveillance.
**Cluster:** C1 (Safety) with tie to C2 (Matching)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q8] Early intervention indicators — observable signals in first 6-8 weeks; systematic check-in mechanisms without excessive surveillance.
- [Devil's Advocate Q2] Dangerous mismatching scenarios — real-time monitoring systems and early-warning indicators identifying deteriorating relationships.
**Merge recommendation:** **Preserve all distinct.** Two meaningfully different framings: (a) Analyst Q8 is about *relationship-quality early warning* (pairs that aren't clicking) — the trajectory of a healthy-but-struggling match; (b) DA Q2 is about *safety early warning* (harmful dynamics) — subtle incompatibilities creating harm that screening missed. Merging would collapse these into one surveillance-system question that fails both purposes. Synthesize into 2 questions: Question A (safety monitoring) preserves DA Q2 emphasizing harm detection — "What real-time monitoring systems and 'early warning' indicators can identify deteriorating mentor-mentee relationships — including subtle incompatibilities that background checks miss (stereotype reinforcement, trauma triggers, inappropriate emotional dependency) — before they cause harm, without creating a surveillance environment that undermines the relationship?" Question B (relationship trajectory) preserves Anal Q8 emphasizing healthy-match development — "What observable signals in the first 6-8 weeks of mentor-mentee interaction most reliably predict long-term relationship success or failure, and what systematic check-in mechanisms would identify struggling pairs early enough for meaningful intervention without the surveillance undermining relationship authenticity?"

## Convergence Group 6: Boundary permeability and role-scope

**Theme:** Defining appropriate mentor role boundaries — where supportive engagement ends and problematic enmeshment begins — and how the boundary system itself becomes a self-reinforcing dynamic where mentors who navigate boundaries well earn autonomy and struggling mentors face scrutiny.
**Cluster:** C1 (Safety) with tie to C5 (Mentor Prep)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q9] Boundary permeability spectrum — delineating mentor role boundaries; decision frameworks distinguishing supportive engagement from enmeshment across gray areas (gifts, communication channels, family involvement).
- [Systems Thinker Q7] Boundary management as a system intervention — rigid boundaries preventing authentic connection, loose boundaries creating role confusion; the reinforcing dynamic where well-navigating mentors earn autonomy.
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "How should mentor role boundaries be delineated so they enable genuine relationship development while preventing boundary drift — including decision frameworks for gray areas (gift-giving, communication channels, family involvement) and recognition that the boundary system itself creates a reinforcing dynamic where mentors who navigate boundaries well earn trust and autonomy while struggling mentors face scrutiny that further undermines their confidence and relationship quality?"

## Convergence Group 7: Power imbalances, teen agency, and voice in program design

**Theme:** The structural power asymmetry between middle-class adult mentors and at-risk teens — and how the program redistributes power through agency over relationship structure, voluntary consent when referrals feel mandated, and authentic youth voice in program design rather than token feedback.
**Cluster:** C4 (Mentee Experience, Belonging & Voice)
**Questions:**
- [Audience Advocate Q9] Voluntary vs. mandatory participation — genuine agency and choice when teens are referred through school counselors or juvenile justice; meaningful consent.
- [Audience Advocate Q10] Youth voice in program design — involving mentees in shaping structure, activities, expectations; beyond token feedback to authentic shared decision-making.
- [Devil's Advocate Q5] Power imbalances in vulnerable relationships — adult-child power dynamic amplified when mentors are middle-class and mentees are juvenile-justice-involved from high-poverty neighborhoods; actively redistributing power.
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "How can the program actively redistribute power to youth rather than paying lip service to mutuality — recognizing that the adult-child dynamic is amplified when mentors are middle-class volunteers and mentees are juvenile-justice-involved teens from high-poverty neighborhoods — including ensuring meaningful consent when participation feels mandated by school or court referral, giving youth real agency over relationship structure, topics, continuation, and exit, and involving current and prospective mentees in shaping program structure and expectations beyond token feedback?"

## Convergence Group 8: "At-risk" labeling, narrative framing, and identity

**Theme:** The deficit-based frame built into "at-risk youth" language, how it shapes mentor perceptions and teens' self-conception, and how program narrative and identity framing can acknowledge challenges without defining teens primarily by their risk status.
**Cluster:** C4 (Mentee Experience, Belonging & Voice)
**Questions:**
- [Audience Advocate Q12] Narrative and identity framing — talking about the program in ways acknowledging challenges without defining teens primarily by "at-risk" status.
- [Questioner Q2] "At-risk" label and expectations — framing teens as at-risk from the start influencing mentor perceptions, dynamics, teens' self-conception.
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "How do we talk about and position this program in ways that acknowledge challenges and risks without defining teens primarily by their 'at-risk' status — recognizing that the deficit-based frame influences mentor perceptions, shapes relationship dynamics, and affects teens' own self-conception in ways that could undermine the program's success — and what language and frameworks support rather than undermine teens' developing identities?"

## Convergence Group 9: Cultural competence and the "at-risk" assumption by mentors

**Theme:** How mentors are prepared for the specific cultural, economic, and neighborhood contexts of these teens, and the risk that well-meaning but unprepared mentors reinforce stereotypes, project their own experience, or undermine authenticity.
**Cluster:** C5 (Mentor Preparation, Support & Wellbeing)
**Questions:**
- [Audience Advocate Q4] Cultural and contextual understanding — preparing mentors for cultural, economic, neighborhood contexts; preventing well-meaning reinforcement of "at-risk" assumptions.
- [Devil's Advocate Q3] Mentor burnout and emotional trauma transfer — vicarious trauma, unconscious projection onto mentees; detecting when mentor is no longer emotionally equipped.
**Merge recommendation:** **Preserve all distinct.** Two meaningfully different framings: (a) AA Q4 asks about *pre-engagement preparation* — how to train mentors on cultural context before the relationship starts, preventing stereotype reinforcement; (b) DA Q3 asks about *in-service deterioration* — how mentors become overwhelmed by trauma exposure and project their own issues during the relationship. Merging would collapse preparation and ongoing wellbeing into one training question. Synthesize into 2 questions: Question A (pre-engagement cultural preparation) preserves AA Q4 — "In what ways can we prepare mentors to understand and respect the specific cultural, economic, and neighborhood contexts that shape these teens' daily experiences, and what happens when well-meaning mentors inadvertently reinforce assumptions about 'at-risk' youth?" Question B (ongoing mentor wellbeing and trauma) preserves DA Q3 — "Working with at-risk teens exposed to trauma, violence, and systemic poverty takes a significant psychological toll — what supports prevent mentors from becoming overwhelmed, experiencing vicarious trauma, or unconsciously projecting their own issues onto mentees, and how can the program detect when a mentor is no longer emotionally equipped to serve effectively?"

## Convergence Group 11: Mentor support structure — staff-led vs. peer vs. reciprocal

**Theme:** How the program supports mentors — through staff-led intervention, peer-to-peer networks, or reciprocal structures where mentors receive through their participation — and the feedback loops that either develop or erode mentor capacity over time.
**Cluster:** C5 (Mentor Prep)
**Questions:**
- [Systems Thinker Q3] Shifting the Burden in mentor support — staff-led solutions making mentors dependent vs. peer networks; preventing erosion of mentor capacity.
- [Systems Thinker Q5] Success to the Successful in mentor selection — star mentors receive recognition, additional resources, priority placement; concentrating attention on a subset; distributed resilience.
- [Systems Thinker Q10] Tragedy of the Commons in mentor time — multiple stakeholders competing for mentor capacity; coordination mechanisms allocating attention through shared norms rather than market mechanisms.
**Merge recommendation:** **Preserve all distinct.** Three different system archetypes applied to mentor support — merging would strip the distinctive archetype vocabulary. Each archetype is a different structural concern: Shifting the Burden (dependency creation), Success to the Successful (star-mentor concentration), Tragedy of the Commons (shared-resource overuse). Preserve all three as distinct C5 questions, each retaining its archetype name. This is a canonical SP1 test: if synthesis collapses these three into one "mentor support" question, it has stripped ST's distinctive contribution.

## Convergence Group 12: Outcome measurement, funder pressure, and relationship burden

**Theme:** How the program demonstrates impact to funders without reporting requirements that distort the relationship-building work or create perverse incentives — and the short-term-metrics-vs-multi-year-outcome feedback mismatch.
**Cluster:** C7 (Outcomes, Measurement & Impact Attribution)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q6] Value quantification framework — metrics credibly demonstrating impact to funders without unreasonable administrative burden or perverse incentives.
- [Devil's Advocate Q10] Success measurement becoming a burden — funder pressure for data vs. relationship-building; minimalist metrics without perverse incentives.
- [Systems Thinker Q6] Delay structures and delayed feedback loops — multi-year impact vs. quarterly funding cycles; leading indicators not incentivizing superficial engagement.
- [Appreciative Inquirer Q12] Measurement that energizes rather than burdens — outcome measurement reinforcing strengths-based philosophy; mentors and teens observing their own growth.
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "What outcome measurement approach credibly demonstrates program impact to funders and stakeholders without the reporting burden conflicting with relationship-building — including leading indicators that don't incentivize superficial short-term engagement despite the mismatch between multi-year impact timelines and quarterly funding cycles, minimalist metrics that capture genuine impact without creating perverse incentives that distort pairs into data points, and measurement design that can itself reinforce the strengths-based philosophy by helping mentors and teens notice and celebrate growth as it unfolds?"

## Convergence Group 14: Institutional referral pathways — inheriting goals from schools and juvenile justice

**Theme:** What it means when the program accepts referrals from school counselors (FERPA, academic-support framing) and juvenile justice (confidentiality, compliance framing) — and how the mentorship program maintains its core purpose vs. being pulled into being surrogate counselors or probation officers.
**Cluster:** C6 (Community, Family & Institutional Ecosystem)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q10] Multi-stakeholder coordination costs — information flow, decision rights, coordination across schools, juvenile justice, families, social services; bottlenecks and misaligned incentives.
- [Devil's Advocate Q7] Juvenile justice referral paradox — mandatory reporting could undermine trust; reconciling reporting with safe harbor; preventing the program from becoming extension of carceral system.
- [Systems Thinker Q8] School and juvenile justice referral pathways — inheriting goals and metrics from referral institutions; Shifting the Burden where external systems depend on mentorship to address problems they can't solve.
- [Systems Thinker Q11] Data privacy and information sharing paradox — fragmented information systems; over-sharing creates legal vulnerability, under-sharing creates blind spots.
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "How can the program maintain its core youth-centered developmental purpose while managing the inherited expectations that come with school and juvenile justice referrals — including the Shifting the Burden dynamic where overloaded external systems depend on mentorship to address problems they can't solve themselves, the carceral-system-extension risk where juvenile-justice referrals turn mentors into surveillance agents, the data-privacy paradox where siloed information creates blind spots but shared information creates legal vulnerability, and the coordination costs across schools, juvenile justice, families, and social services when decision rights and information flow are misaligned?"

## Convergence Group 15: Community skepticism, past harms, and organization history

**Theme:** Why communities with high poverty and juvenile justice involvement are skeptical of outside programs, the track record of failed interventions and saviorism, and whether the faith-based organization's 20-year presence is an asset or another institution to be distrusted.
**Cluster:** C6 (Community, Family & Institutional Ecosystem)
**Questions:**
- [Devil's Advocate Q12] Community skepticism and past harms — why this program would be different; respect for community knowledge, existing leaders, avoiding saviorism.
- [Devil's Advocate Q6] Faith-based assumptions in secular contexts — proselytizing risk; when mentor faith expression makes mentee uncomfortable.
- [Devil's Advocate Q8] Parental consent barriers for high-risk youth — unstable home situations, distrust of institutions; serving youth who cannot easily obtain consent.
**Merge recommendation:** **Preserve all distinct.** Three meaningfully different framings: (a) DA Q12 is about *community-level trust* — the program's relationship to the neighborhood and its history of failed interventions; (b) DA Q6 is about *organizational values and proselytizing* — the faith-host's potential imposition on secular/other-faith mentees; (c) DA Q8 is about *family-level consent* — structural barriers to parental consent for the most at-risk youth. Merging strips the distinct institutional entry points. Synthesize into 3 questions, one per DA question, each preserving the distinctive framing.
