**Input:** 72 Synthesize-group questions from `test-data/space-party_low.md`

**Convergence density:** 57%

Convergence here means two or more Synthesize-persona questions address the same concrete concern such that synthesis can either (a) merge them into one richer question that anchors in both source texts, or (b) preserve all distinct voices when the framings differ enough that merging would strip persona-specific value.

## Convergence Group 1: Sensory overwhelm and fright triggers

**Theme:** Whether dim lighting, alien imagery, loud rocket sounds, and other classic space motifs frighten or overwhelm 5-year-olds, and how to design for diverse sensory thresholds.
**Cluster:** C3 (Safety, Sensory Accessibility & Inclusion)
**Questions:**
- [Questioner Q2] How might the party overwhelm or frighten some children? What if the "darkness" of space creates anxiety rather than wonder?
- [Audience Advocate Q5] Designing for sensory differences — Space themes often involve darkness, loud sounds, or chaotic movement...
- [Devil's Advocate Q1] What space-themed elements could actually frighten 5-year-olds? Dark lighting, "alien" decorations, or loud rocket sounds...
- [Audience Advocate Q9] Physical safety in transformed environments — reduced lighting, dry ice fog effects, slippery fabrics...
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "How might the immersive space environment (darkness, loud effects, chaotic movement, fog, alien imagery) overwhelm, frighten, or unsafely surprise 5-year-olds with varied sensory thresholds — and what specific design choices preserve the theme's wonder while protecting children with sensory sensitivities, anxiety, or different processing needs?"

## Convergence Group 2: Hidden and full budget definition

**Theme:** What the actual budget envelope is when hidden space-specific costs (dry ice, blacklights, metallic paints, specialty crafts) are accounted for, and what counts as a real cost beyond money (time, cleanup).
**Cluster:** C1 (Budget, Cost & Resource Strategy)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q1] What is the realistic budget range for a space-themed 5th birthday party serving 11 children, and which cost categories typically offer the highest return...
- [Analyst Q3] What are the trade-offs between DIY versus purchased solutions across decoration, activity materials, and food, considering both financial cost and parent time investment?
- [Devil's Advocate Q5] Are we budgeting for the hidden costs of space-themed execution? (dry ice, blacklights, metallic paints, unusual craft supplies)
- [Questioner Q5] What does "budget-friendly" mean in this specific context? What's the actual budget range, and what are the hidden costs we haven't considered (time, cleanup, specialized materials)?
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "What is the realistic full-cost budget envelope for a space-themed 5th birthday party for 11 children — including space-specific hidden costs (dry ice, blacklights, metallic paints, specialty crafts), DIY-vs-purchased trade-offs, and non-monetary costs (parent time, cleanup) — and which categories deliver the highest return on parent satisfaction and child engagement?"

## Convergence Group 3: Activity-developmental capability mismatch

**Theme:** The risk that adults plan space activities (model rockets, science experiments, sustained planet identification) that exceed typical 5-year-old fine motor, attention, or instruction-following capability.
**Cluster:** C2 (Activity Design, Pacing & Engagement)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q6] How should activity difficulty be calibrated for 5-year-olds with varying developmental readiness, and what backup modifications should be prepared if activities prove too easy or too complex?
- [Devil's Advocate Q2] Are we overestimating what 5-year-olds can realistically do independently? Space activities often involve fine motor skills, sustained attention, or following multi-step instructions...
- [Devil's Advocate Q9] What if the parents' space vision exceeds the children's attention spans? Adults often find space concepts fascinating while 5-year-olds may need constant novelty and movement...
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "Are planned space activities (fine motor crafts, multi-step science demos, sustained-attention games) calibrated to typical 5-year-old developmental capability and attention — including backup modifications for developmental variance and faster-paced alternatives when adult-fascinating concepts prove too abstract or slow for the group?"

## Convergence Group 4: Children who don't engage with the planned theme/activities

**Theme:** Designing for children whose interests, temperament, or in-the-moment state diverge from the planned space activities — both other guests and the birthday girl herself.
**Cluster:** C4 (Birthday-Girl Agency & Theme Authenticity) for the birthday-girl frame; C3 (Inclusion) for guests
**Questions:**
- [Questioner Q3] What happens to children who don't engage with the planned activities? If some kids aren't interested in rockets or astronomy, what alternative experiences exist?
- [Devil's Advocate Q7] What if the birthday girl doesn't engage with the space theme as expected? Warning signs and mid-party pivot strategies...
- [Audience Advocate Q6] Beyond the "ideal" 5-year-old participant — accommodations for variations in physical ability, neurodivergence, attention span, or familiarity with group play...
**Merge recommendation:** **Preserve all distinct.** Three meaningfully different framings: (a) Questioner asks about the inclusion of disengaged guests, (b) DA asks about real-time pivot if the birthday girl rejects the theme, (c) AA asks about pre-designed flexibility for varied participant profiles. Merging would lose the temporal axis (during-event pivot vs. pre-event design) and the subject axis (other children vs. birthday girl). Synthesize into 2 questions: one merging Questioner Q3 + AA Q6 (designed flexibility for varied guests), one preserving DA Q7 (mid-party pivot specifically for the birthday girl).

## Convergence Group 5: Memorability — what children actually remember

**Theme:** Whether the party generates lasting memory beyond surface enjoyment, and which elements actually stick with 5-year-olds vs. which are adult-noticed-only.
**Cluster:** C5 (Memory, Wonder & Delight)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q9] What measurable indicators would determine party success beyond surface-level enjoyment (child exhaustion, parent stress, social dynamics, retained learning), and how might these inform future planning?
- [Analyst Q10] What distinguishes a memorable party from a standard one from a 5-year-old's perspective, and which elements generate the strongest positive recall after the fact?
- [Appreciative Inquirer Q5] The party the children remember for years — imagine the children at age 8 looking back on this 5th birthday party. What do they still talk about?
- [Questioner Q4] What will children actually remember from this party six months later? Are we investing effort in elements that adults notice but children don't?
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged with preserved sub-question.** Primary merge: "What elements of this party will 5-year-olds actually remember six months and three years later, and which observable indicators (during and after) signal that effort is landing on child-memorable moments rather than adult-noticed details?" Preserve AI Q5 separately because its imaginative-projection framing ("imagine the children at age 8 looking back") is a method as well as a question — the imaginative exercise has design utility distinct from the analytical version.

## Convergence Group 6: Theme authenticity — is the party for the child or the theme?

**Theme:** Whether the space theme genuinely connects to the birthday girl, what "space" means to a specific 5-year-old, and the meta-question of who the celebration centers.
**Cluster:** C4 (Birthday-Girl Agency & Theme Authenticity)
**Questions:**
- [Audience Advocate Q1] Centering the birthday girl's vision — co-creating the party so she feels genuine ownership, and discovering what she actually understands about space rather than assuming...
- [Appreciative Inquirer Q2] What the birthday girl already loves — existing fascinations as foundation for party elements that feel deeply authentic...
- [Questioner Q1] What does "space" actually mean to a 5-year-old? When we say space-themed, are we thinking astronauts and rockets, or stars and planets, or aliens and fantasy?
- [Questioner Q9] What are we actually celebrating — the child or the theme? Is the space theme genuinely about the daughter's interests, or is it about the parents' vision?
- [Questioner Q15] What would need to be true for this to be remembered as "the best birthday ever" from the child's perspective — not the parents', not the guests', but specifically the birthday girl's?
- [Audience Advocate Q8] True-to-child understanding of space — distinguishing playful imagination (astronauts, aliens, rockets) from concepts that might confuse or frighten (infinite void, separation from Earth)...
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged into 2 questions.** Question A (theme-authenticity-to-child): merge AA Q1 + AI Q2 + Quest Q1 + Quest Q9 — "What does 'space' specifically mean to this 5-year-old, what does she already gravitate toward in her play and curiosity, and how do we co-create the party so it reflects her vision rather than parental projection of what a space party should be?" Question B (best-birthday-from-her-perspective): preserve Quest Q15 — its evaluative-from-her-view framing is a distinct interrogation. AA Q8 (which space concepts are age-appropriate vs frightening) is convergent with CG1 fright-trigger questions and should land there, not in this merge.

*Note:* AA Q8 appears in both CG1 (frightening concepts) and CG6 (theme authenticity to child). Primary assignment is CG1 because the operational question is "which space concepts to avoid"; the theme-authenticity layer is secondary. SP1 should not double-count AA Q8.

## Convergence Group 7: Adult-child ratio and coordination

**Theme:** Supervision capacity, the number of adults required, and the single-point-of-failure risk when one parent owns one critical function.
**Cluster:** C6 (Logistics, Contingency & Coordination)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q8] Which logistical variables have the greatest impact on party success (timing, location, parent-to-child ratio, space requirements)...
- [Questioner Q7] What's the realistic parent-to-child ratio for supervision? Who's actually running these activities, and do they have capacity?
- [Devil's Advocate Q6] How much parent volunteer labor is this theme actually requiring? Some space activities need multiple adults simultaneously managing different stations...
- [Systems Thinker Q5] [Day-of coordination bottlenecks] How many adults are needed to avoid the "single point of failure" problem...
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "What is the realistic adult supervision capacity required for a space-themed party with 11 five-year-olds — including activity-specific multi-adult demands (rocket launches, station supervision), the realistic parent-to-child ratio, and the redundancy needed to avoid single-point-of-failure when one adult is consumed by an unexpected need (meltdown, food, injury)?"

## Convergence Group 10: Birthday escalation and friend-group arms race

**Theme:** The longer-arc risk that an elaborate themed party sets escalating expectations within the family across years and across the friend-group across hosts.
**Cluster:** C7 (Family, Parents & Group Dynamics) primary; C1 (Budget) secondary
**Questions:**
- [Devil's Advocate Q11] Are we creating unnecessary birthday escalation for future years? Elaborate themed parties can set expectations that spiral upward each year...
- [Devil's Advocate Q12] What if this space theme creates a "birthday party arms race" among the friend group? Other parents might feel pressure to match or exceed...
- [Questioner Q14] What ripple effects might this party create? Could it create expectations for future parties that become burdensome? What if other parents feel pressure to compete?
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "What ripple effects does this elaborate themed party risk creating — within the family (escalating expectations across the daughter's future birthdays) and across the friend-group (competitive pressure on other host families) — and how can we deliver a memorable space experience while implicitly modeling that creativity and connection matter more than spectacle or spending?"

## Convergence Group 11: Brittle plan points and contingency

**Theme:** Where the party plan is most fragile — weather, attendance variance, key-element failure, supply non-arrival.
**Cluster:** C6 (Logistics, Contingency & Coordination)
**Questions:**
- [Analyst Q7] What are the highest-probability failure modes (weather, activity flops, allergic reactions, injuries) and what minimal-cost preparations can mitigate these risks?
- [Questioner Q8] What if we planned for half the children but twice as many show up? What if weather changes outdoor plans? What if key elements don't arrive or don't work? Where are the brittle points?
- [Systems Thinker Q10] [Weather-or-not contingency systems] How do indoor-outdoor party plans create brittle systems vs. indoor-native plans that can expand outdoors if conditions allow...
- [Systems Thinker Q11] [Guest count elasticity] What breaks in the party system if attendance is 8 children instead of 11, or 14 instead of 11?
**Merge recommendation:** **Merged.** "Where is the party plan most brittle — across weather variance, guest-count elasticity (8 vs 11 vs 14), key-supply non-arrival, and activity-specific failure modes — and which minimal-cost contingency choices (indoor-native plans with outdoor bonuses, scalable activities, simple backups) reduce the count of plan elements that can fail catastrophically?"
