# Brainstorming Questions: Mobile App Feature Simplification / MVP Reset

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Defining Core Value and Keystone Features

1. **Locating the core — keystone features versus Success-to-the-Successful noise**: What constitutes the app's true core value, and how do we distinguish features that genuinely drive 30-day retention from features that only look popular because of the "Success-to-the-Successful" dynamic (popular features get more attention and investment, making them more popular, while neglected features atrophy)? Identify "keystone features" whose removal would disproportionately harm the ecosystem, map feature stickiness, dependency patterns, and correlation with retention, and account for the possibility that the real core is an unexpected combination of 2–3 features working together that siloed usage data cannot see. [Analyst, Systems Thinker, Questioner]

2. **What "core value" means before we can measure it**: Before optimizing for metrics, what does "core value" actually mean in practice — are we looking for the features most people use, the features that create the most passionate engagement, the job users are hiring us to do, or our true differentiator that no other fitness app provides regardless of current usage priority? [Questioner]

3. **No-unified-core failure mode**: What if the problem isn't focus but execution quality across all 15 features — the named Devil's-Advocate risk that no unified "core value" exists and the app simply cannot compete with dedicated alternatives, meaning simplification would leave us with nothing compelling? [Devil's Advocate]

4. **Amplifying what already sparks joy** *(AI orphan register)*: Among the 15 features, which ones have users responded to with enthusiasm, sustained engagement, or loyalty — and what structures, practices, and product principles would reliably amplify those existing strengths into the core of a simplified product? [Appreciative Inquirer]

## Topic Cluster 02: User Segmentation and Lived Context

5. **Cohort segmentation and gateway-vs-anchor features**: What distinct user cohorts exist (social users, solo trackers, content consumers, 25-year-old fitness enthusiasts vs. 55-year-olds easing in, single parents with 5 minutes, users managing chronic illness), and which features serve as "gateway drugs" versus "retention anchors" for each — so feature prioritization reflects segment-specific value rather than aggregate metrics? [Analyst, Audience Advocate]

6. **Accessibility and digital-literacy equity barriers**: Which features create structural barriers for users with limited tech literacy, non-English speakers, or users who rely on assistive technologies — and how might feature prioritization change if we centered these users rather than the loudest or most data-visible cohorts? [Audience Advocate]

7. **Unarticulated needs and normalized workarounds**: What gaps exist between what users say they want and what they actually do in their 47-second sessions, and what frustrations or workarounds have they normalized (because "that's just how apps work") that could illuminate the app's true core value? [Audience Advocate]

8. **Passionate-minority foothold or dead-end niche**: A feature with only 2% adoption but high retention among its users could be a foothold into an underserved niche that will grow, or a dead-end — what leading indicators distinguish "early adopters of the future core" from "enthusiastic users of a peripheral feature," and what do these micro-communities' lived experiences teach us about genuine value? [Systems Thinker, Audience Advocate, Questioner]

9. **Hidden assets inside the 2% daily cohort** *(AI orphan register)*: Among the 180K downloads and the 2% DAU, who are the users who found value — what specific use case, demographic, or peak-moment behavior distinguishes them, and what hidden assets in the current user base could seed a focused product? [Appreciative Inquirer]

## Topic Cluster 03: Metrics That Actually Signal Product-Market Fit

10. **Redefining the north-star metric beyond engagement**: The current metrics (2% DAU/MAU, 47-second sessions, 8% 30-day retention) signal product-market fit failure, but engagement itself is not the same as value delivered — a simpler app might have shorter sessions because users accomplish what they need faster. What leading indicators would capture core value delivery rather than time-on-task, and how do we distinguish "better engagement" from "fewer features masking the same problems"? [Analyst, Systems Thinker]

11. **A/B validation and post-launch dashboards**: What experimental design (including temporarily disabling a feature to see who notices) would let us scientifically validate that the chosen 3–4 features actually represent core value — and once relaunched, what early-warning signs and success metrics at 30/60/90 days would distinguish "users mourning removed features" from "genuine product-market fit" from "solved the wrong problem"? [Analyst, Questioner]

## Topic Cluster 04: Transition Strategy — Incremental vs. Relaunch

12. **Incremental vs. big-bang, and the drifting-goals trap**: What are the comparative risk profiles of gradual simplification versus a clean relaunch — considering user confusion, technical complexity, market perception, team bandwidth, and the Devil's-Advocate risk that "2.0" relaunches often fail because users have already moved on? Specifically, how do we avoid the "erosion of goals" pattern where incremental cuts keep justifying themselves to existing users ("just one more feature") and the product never becomes truly focused, while also avoiding a clean-relaunch shock the system cannot recover from? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker, Questioner]

## Topic Cluster 05: Unit Economics, Runway, and Technical-Debt Realities

13. **Runway math and unit economics of simplification**: With 8 months of funding and a team of 8, what does the cost structure look like per feature (engineering maintenance load, customer support burden, server costs, opportunity cost), what's the break-even analysis on how much reducing feature surface lowers burn rate versus extending runway, and at what point does simplification trade long-term viability for short-term survival? [Analyst]

14. **Fixes-that-Fail technical-debt explosion**: Cutting features doesn't guarantee a simpler codebase — features that look independent in the UI may share authentication, data models, notification systems, or analytics pipelines, creating a "Fixes-that-Fail" pattern where we cut features to simplify but the technical complexity remains (ghost code, hidden dependencies, broken integrations), consuming runway without delivering simplicity. How do we distinguish "delete-and-forget" features from "excise-carefully" features, map the dependency graph, and realistically timeline removal with buffer for unknown unknowns before the 8 months run out? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker, Questioner]

## Topic Cluster 06: Churn Risk and the Withdrawal Period

15. **Cascading churn and the 3–6 month withdrawal period**: What's the expected churn from affected user segments when features are cut, especially given the compounding risk that each passionate niche will abandon — and what happens during the 3–6 month "withdrawal" period when users reach for the meal planner, habit tracker, or meditation guide that no longer exists? Model the quantifiable trade-off between simplification and user loss in lifetime-value terms, set a maximum acceptable user loss, and identify what determines whether the balancing loop (frustration → disengagement → departure) or the virtuous cycle (relief → renewed engagement) dominates. [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker]

16. **Simplification experienced as loss, not refinement**: The team sees this as "refocusing," but users who relied on removed features may experience loss, betrayal, or the emotional weight of a broken commitment — how do we understand that experience of loss from the user's side and address it without collapsing it into generic "change management" messaging? [Audience Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 07: Communication, Trust Repair, and Co-Creation

17. **Communication without the Shifting-the-Burden dependency**: What communication strategy announces feature removal in a way that balances transparency, trust-building, and churn minimization — while avoiding the "Shifting-the-Burden" pattern where our "we're simplifying for you" reassurance replaces users' intrinsic capacity to find value on their own, creating a self-amplifying need for ever more handholding? What metrics tell us we're getting this right? [Analyst, Systems Thinker]

18. **Trust repair with burned-out users**: Users who stuck around despite poor experience may feel betrayed or skeptical — what would it take to demonstrate genuine commitment to their needs rather than business survival, and how might the relaunch become an opportunity to invite them into a new chapter (acknowledging past missteps, designing *with* the community rather than *for* them) rather than just shipping a smaller product? [Audience Advocate]

19. **Genuine user co-creation vs. post-hoc decision announcement**: What would real user co-creation look like in deciding which features survive — rather than communicating decisions *after* they're made — in a way that respects users' agency and investment in the app, and who has the power dynamics to be heard when "core" versus "nice-to-have" is being defined? [Audience Advocate]

20. **Framing simplification as focus, not subtraction** *(AI orphan register)*: Could we frame the simplification as "we're focusing on what matters most" rather than "we're taking things away" — and what small, low-risk experiments this month would test core-value hypotheses in a way that makes users feel part of the product's evolution rather than subjects of it? [Appreciative Inquirer, Questioner]

## Topic Cluster 08: Pre-Commit Validation and Decision Rigor

21. **Self-selection bias in user research**: Users who respond to surveys or interviews are likely the most engaged minority — what research methods can help us understand why the majority of the 92% abandon within 30 days without over-optimizing for the surviving power users who still care? [Devil's Advocate]

22. **One-person opinion is not "research," and false consensus on the team**: With one PM deciding which feedback matters and six engineers each likely championing features they personally built (a "false consensus" risk where ownership bias and team politics drive the outcome), what decision framework and what guardrails would objectively identify core value — rather than letting a single person's biases or the loudest internal voice define "essential"? [Devil's Advocate]

23. **The "simplify into nothing" trap — validate before cutting irreversibly**: What if the true issue isn't feature count but poor information architecture, onboarding, or discoverability — meaning users actually want these features but cannot find or understand them? How do we validate simplification will improve retention *before* we irreversibly remove features that might be working for some users, rather than assuming feature count is the failure mode? [Devil's Advocate, Questioner]

## Topic Cluster 09: Team Morale, Ownership, and Post-Cut Capacity

24. **Killing darlings without destroying motivation**: If engineers have spent months or years building features now being deleted, how do they stay motivated through the relaunch — especially when "core" features require working on code they didn't write and may not feel ownership over — and what's the smallest intervention that flips the dynamic from defensive attachment ("my feature") to proactive pruning? What mechanisms rebuild psychological investment after a round of deletions, and how do we help the team feel proud of simplification rather than feeling like they "failed" by building features now being cut? [Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker, Questioner]

25. **Slow-return-to-bloat after capacity frees up**: Six engineers maintaining 3 features suddenly have excess capacity — a self-amplifying opportunity, but also the risk that excess capacity turns into scope creep and the team slowly adds features back until they're bloated again. What structural constraints or processes would prevent the return to bloat while still letting the team use the newly available capacity to make remaining features dramatically better? [Systems Thinker]

26. **Team reimagined — strengths, energy, and aligned vision** *(AI orphan register)*: What kind of simplified product would align with the team's existing strengths (technical capabilities, design sensibilities, domain expertise) so the simplification feels like liberation rather than loss — and what would the team's day-to-day look like when they're energized, creative, and proud rather than burned out from maintaining 15 half-built features? [Appreciative Inquirer]

## Topic Cluster 10: Competitive Positioning and Market Category

27. **Simplifying against feature-accumulating competitors**: Market dynamics favor feature accumulation — every competitor's marketing emphasizes "more stuff" — and a stripped-down app will look feature-poor on comparison charts, potentially hurting downloads in the short term. Is this a "worse before better" situation where simplicity creates a compounding word-of-mouth and retention advantage feature-bloated competitors can't match, and what does the transition curve look like — where are the inflection points, and is the delay short enough that we survive it? [Systems Thinker]

28. **Category question: are we even a fitness app?**: Why do we assume we need to be in the fitness-app category at all — what if our actual value lies in an adjacent space we've accidentally stumbled into, and what if we're fundamentally pivoting to a different market and should be marketing to a different audience rather than optimizing for our existing one? [Questioner]

29. **Marketing breadth vs. focused delivery**: The bloated app attracts users by promising everything (workouts, meals, social, challenges), setting unrealistic expectations that lead to disappointment — a focused app attracts fewer initial downloads but potentially higher retention. What's the growth strategy that accepts this constraint rather than fighting it, and how do we align promise and delivery when alignment means turning away users who want the things we no longer offer? [Systems Thinker]

## Appended Questions (Perspective, Provocation, and Distinctive-Register Personas)

### Provocateur (Append)

1. **What if you kept exactly ONE feature and made it absurdly worse?** Imagine you stripped the app down to just workout tracking, but then removed all logging, history, progress visualization, and data—leaving only the immediate act of pressing a button that says "I worked out" before the app closes itself. What would this useless button reveal about what users actually crave from the tracking experience? *The real insight: is the value in the data or in the ritual of acknowledgment?*

2. **What if you launched a "competitor" that was intentionally terrible?** Create a clone app with all 15 features, but each one is broken in a specific, maddening way—workout tracking only logs every third workout, meal planning displays recipes you can't actually cook, social feed shows posts from people who don't exist. Market it aggressively to your existing users. What does watching them struggle with the anti-version reveal about which brokenness they'll tolerate and which sends them away forever? *The real insight: what if you could discover core value by studying which imperfections function as dealbreakers?*

3. **What if your 180K downloads were 180K different people who never opened the app twice?** Imagine the retention disaster is actually a feature—your app is designed for single-use transformation, like a ceremonial rite of passage after which users are "graduated" and should never return. How would you redesign the onboarding experience to complete someone's entire fitness journey in 47 seconds? *The real insight: what if the lifetime value model is wrong for this problem and the real opportunity is in perfect, self-contained moments?*

4. **What if you had to explain this app to someone from 1995 who has never seen a mobile phone?** They understand "fitness," "food," "friends," and "shopping" as physical, non-digital experiences. As you describe your app, which features make sense in their world and which become meaningless? What does the alien-ness of "social fitness" or "digital meal planning" reveal about assumptions you've never questioned? *The real insight: which parts of your product exist only because they're possible in software, not because they actually solve human problems?*

5. **What if the 2% who actually use the app daily are the problem, not the solution?** Imagine your power users are actually tumors—pathological attachments to features that represent the app's worst impulses, and their retention is preventing the product from evolving into something healthy for normal humans. What would happen if you actively tried to drive away your most engaged users? *The real insight: what if your retention metrics are optimized for a tiny minority of freak users while everyone else is correctly recognizing that the product doesn't serve them?*

### Storyteller (Append)

1. **What would the "first five minutes" experience look like for a returning user who opens the simplified app for the first time, and what emotional state should we be designing toward?** Imagine it's Tuesday morning and Sarah, who previously faced 15 features and 6 navigation tabs, opens the new version. Walk through what she sees, what she feels, and what she can accomplish in those critical first minutes. How do we ensure the transition from confusion to clarity happens within a single session? What does "success" look like in that first interaction—should she feel relief, excitement, curiosity, or something else entirely?

2. **How might the engineering team's daily work life transform when they shift from maintaining 15 half-built features to perfecting 3-4 world-class experiences, and what "micro-moments" of professional satisfaction will replace the current burnout cycle?** Walk us through the standup meeting six months from now. What language are engineers using to describe their work? How do conversations about quality, testing, and user feedback change when they're not spread thin across a feature graveyard? What moments of pride—seeing a user review, watching usage metrics, shipping a polished experience—replace the current reality of constant bug fixes and technical debt management? How does the team's relationship with the codebase itself evolve when they're not afraid to touch it?

3. **How might the press release, app store description, and launch announcement tell a story that frames simplification as innovation rather than retreat, and what metaphors or analogies would help users and investors see this as a bold step forward?** Consider the difference between "we're removing features because we couldn't make them work" versus "we've discovered what users actually need, and we're cutting away everything else." What story positions this as the company's bravest decision rather than its most desperate? How do we communicate to new users that this simplified version isn't a downgrade, but rather the first version of the product that actually works? What cultural references or parallels—from other companies, from product design history, from everyday life—could make this pivot feel obvious and inevitable in retrospect?

4. **How might the "moment of deletion" unfold for different types of users when features they've incorporated into their routines disappear, and what narrative threads could help them feel excited rather than abandoned?** Consider Marcus, who uses the supplement store feature weekly, versus Priya, who relies on the progress journal as her primary touchpoint. When they open the simplified app and discover these features gone, what happens in the seconds before they either delete the app or give the new version a chance? What story could the app tell them in that moment—and through what channels (push notifications, onboarding, email, in-app messaging)—that would transform a sense of loss into curiosity about the newly focused experience?

5. **What does the "funeral" scene look like when the team decides which features to cut, and how can we turn a potentially demoralizing process into a moment of clarity and shared purpose?** Imagine the product owner standing at the whiteboard with 15 feature cards, knowing that 11 of them are about to be "killed." How does that conversation unfold? What frameworks or rituals could transform this from a series of painful decisions into a collective act of strategic vision? How do we ensure that every person in that room—whether they built the meditation guide or the friend finder—leaves feeling energized about the new direction rather than mourning their specific contribution? What narrative about the company's future could make sacrificing the present feel like a victory rather than a loss?

### Visionary (Append)

1. **[What if instead of choosing 3-4 core features, we created a product with no features at all — just a single, coherent behavior that users engage in repeatedly?]** Imagine a fitness app with no tracking, no planning, no social features, no content — just one elegant, repeatable action that somehow produces fitness outcomes. What would that one action be? What if the app became a "one-button" product where pressing that button initiates a cascade of value without requiring navigation, configuration, or decisions?

2. **[What if fitness wasn't something you track, but something that emerges naturally through a fundamentally different relationship with your body?]** Instead of asking which features to keep or cut, what if we completely reimagined the category: What would a fitness product look like if it were designed around joy, emergence, and organic behavior change rather than metrics, tracking, and discipline? What if the core value isn't "helping people work out" but something entirely different that doesn't have a current analog in the fitness app space?

3. **[Imagine if the app could disappear entirely as an interface and instead become a presence that anticipates and supports without requiring active engagement?]** What would it mean to invert the relationship between user and app — where the app exists not as something you open, but as something that works on your behalf in the background, creating interventions at the moment they're needed without requiring you to navigate tabs or manage features? What would a zero-UI fitness product look like?

4. **[What if the 47-second average session isn't a problem to be solved, but the actual product — and what if we leaned into that rather than trying to extend engagement?]** What would it mean to embrace the micro-session pattern and design something that delivers maximum value in under a minute? Could this become a fundamentally new product category — the "instant fitness intervention" that exists in the interstitial moments of daily life? What if the app's role isn't to occupy attention but to punctuate it with brief, transformative experiences?

5. **[What if the core value isn't any single feature, but the emotional state users hope to achieve through using the product — and what if we could reverse-engineer from that destination rather than from existing features?]** Rather than asking "what features do people use most," what if we asked "what emotional transformation do people seek when they download this app" and built backward from there? What if the product that delivers that transformation doesn't look like a fitness app at all, but resembles something from a completely different domain (therapy, coaching, entertainment, community)?

### Connector (Append)

1. **Feature pruning as ecosystem succession** The challenge of cutting features mirrors ecological succession, where crowded ecosystems self-thin to let dominant species thrive. In forest ecology, "self-thinning" creates space for the strongest organisms to flourish. What if we viewed the 15 features as competing species in an ecosystem — how would we identify which features are the "keystone species" whose presence enables the entire ecosystem to function, and which are the invasive species crowding out the core value?

2. **The IKEA effect paradox in user attachment** There's a structural parallel between users attached to niche features and the psychological phenomenon known as the IKEA effect — people overvalue things they've invested effort in building or using. But behavioral economics also shows the "endowment effect" creates irrational attachment to things simply because we own them. How do we distinguish between genuine value-creating features and features users are irrationally attached to due to sunk-cost fallacy — and could we borrow techniques from "loss aversion" research to help users let go without feeling betrayed?

3. **Museum curation as a model for feature preservation** When museums face space constraints, they don't simply throw away lesser works — they rotate collections, loan to other institutions, or create digital archives. The 15 features likely each have some users (even if small). Rather than binary keep/kill decisions, what if we borrowed from museum curation practices: could some features be "rotated out" (temporarily disabled but preserved), "loaned" (spun off as separate apps), or "digitized archived" (export functionality for power users while removing from core experience)?

4. **Language death and feature migration** Linguistics studies how dying languages pass useful vocabulary to surviving languages before disappearing — structural elements get adopted, idioms get translated, but the language itself fades. This is different from simple deletion. What if we treated each feature like a dying language: what "vocabulary" (individual interactions, data structures, user habits) from cut features should be "migrated" into the surviving features, rather than assuming users will simply abandon those patterns? For example, if the "challenge platform" is cut, what competitive or social elements from it should be preserved in the remaining core?

5. **Surgical triage vs. architectural restoration** There are two different structural parallels for feature removal: surgical triage (emergency room — save what can be saved, let go of what's too far gone) and architectural restoration (preserve historical integrity while modernizing function). Triage is fast but destructive; restoration is slow but honors the original vision. The 8-month runway creates urgency, but the "jack of all trades" problem suggests vision clarity is needed. Which metaphor better serves this situation — and how does that choice change the feature selection criteria?
