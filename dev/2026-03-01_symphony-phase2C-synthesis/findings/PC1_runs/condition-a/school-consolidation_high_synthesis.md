# Brainstorming Questions: School Consolidation

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Foundational Framing and Alternatives to Consolidation

1. **Is consolidation even the right unit of analysis?** At its most fundamental level, what are the essential educational services these 1,800 students actually need, and is "consolidating three districts into one" the only way to deliver those services? What alternative models — shared services agreements, cooperative purchasing, a "lead district" model, regional high school districts, a confederation with local boards retaining authority, or virtual/functional consolidation of specific departments (transportation, special education, HR) — could capture 60-70% of administrative savings without triggering community identity loss, and how should we rigorously compare partial versus full consolidation before committing to the most disruptive path? [First Principles Thinker, Questioner, Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker]

2. **Reimagining the geographic and institutional assumption.** If we were building educational delivery for a rural county from scratch today — without legacy assumptions about district boundaries, buildings, or attendance zones — what would the optimal structure look like? Would the fundamental unit be the "district" at all, or should it be the educational needs of specific student cohorts, the financial capacity of taxpayers, or the practical constraints of geography? What definitionally different models (learning hubs, hybrid delivery, community-based micro-schools) would work better than the traditional model we're trying to preserve? [First Principles Thinker, Questioner]

3. **The fundamental problem — decline or structure?** Is the core problem that the districts are structured incorrectly (three small districts instead of one large one), or that rural education is inherently incompatible with declining enrollment and fixed infrastructure costs? If consolidation solves the immediate financial crisis, what fundamental challenges remain unaddressed — and does a Shifting-the-Burden dynamic apply, where consolidation offers a short-term fix that erodes capacity to address the root cause (enrollment decline) and leaves a more cumbersome bureaucracy to downsize later? [First Principles Thinker, Systems Thinker, Questioner]

4. **Why assume the state funding formula is fixed?** What if the energy and political capital required for consolidation were redirected toward reforming the state funding formula that penalizes small districts? What other state policies — transportation reimbursement ratios, minimum school size requirements, curriculum mandates, teacher certification rules — are the hidden constraints shaping what's possible, and what state-level policy tweaks would dramatically improve rural district viability without requiring consolidation at all? [Questioner, Systems Thinker]

5. **Is voter approval a feasibility gate or a signal?** We're treating "getting voters to approve this" as a political challenge to solve through messaging and coalition-building. But what if voter rejection isn't a communication failure — it's a signal that the fundamental value proposition is inadequate? What would a consolidation proposal look like if it were so compelling that opposition would be irrational rather than justified? [First Principles Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 02: Strengths, Aspirations, and Peak-Moment Vision

6. **What already makes these districts special, and what must not be lost?** When you walk into any of these seven schools on a day when everything is working beautifully — when students are engaged, parents feel proud, and there's genuine joy — what specifically is happening? What practices, relationships, or traditions create those moments, and how do we identify which are worth preserving at any cost? [Appreciative Inquirer — orphan register]

7. **Peak moments of district collaboration as the foundation for consolidation.** Tell me about a time in the past decade when two or more of these districts worked together on something that succeeded beyond anyone's expectations. What conditions, relationships, and trust were already in place that made that collaboration possible, and how could those relationships become the foundation for consolidation rather than casualties of it? [Appreciative Inquirer — orphan register]

8. **Hidden assets in declining enrollment.** Declining enrollment creates genuine challenges, but what unexpected strengths or opportunities has it already revealed — smaller class sizes that enabled innovation, stronger parent-teacher relationships, more nimble decision-making? What would we want to make sure doesn't get lost in the transition to a larger system? [Appreciative Inquirer — orphan register]

9. **The school experience we want children to remember.** Imagine it's ten years from now and a child who started kindergarten in a newly consolidated district is now graduating, saying "I'm so glad I went through schools in this county." What would they describe about their relationships, learning, and connection to community — and what structures would need to be in place from day one of consolidation to make that experience reliable? [Appreciative Inquirer — orphan register]

10. **Learning from what already works across districts.** Each of the three districts has something it does exceptionally well — perhaps one excels at special education inclusion, another at community partnerships, another at fiscal efficiency under constraint. What if consolidation were designed around systematically amplifying each district's existing strengths across the whole county rather than creating one-size-fits-all uniformity — and what cross-pollination structures would enable that? [Appreciative Inquirer — orphan register]

## Topic Cluster 03: Financial Modeling, Break-Even, and Hidden Costs

11. **What is the true break-even analysis and 5-year cash-flow model for consolidation?** Beyond the $1.2M in annual administrative savings, what are the one-time transition costs — legal fees for district dissolution ($500K-$1.5M range), technology/SIS/financial-software integration, HR record consolidation, union contract buyouts, early-retirement incentives, facility closure and mothballing expenses, asbestos abatement and environmental remediation? If transition costs run $3-5M, consolidation operates at a net loss for 3-4 years; can the districts absorb this period, especially the smallest district with only a 2-year fund balance, and what contingency reserves should be modeled? [Analyst, Accountant, Devil's Advocate, Questioner]

12. **Unit economics at different enrollment decline scenarios and the viability floor.** A 12% per-pupil funding increase is a one-time structural improvement, but enrollment is projected to decline another 15% by 2035 — roughly 270 fewer students and $2.4M in state funding at $8-10K per pupil. Does the $1.2M in administrative savings still leave the consolidated district running a structural deficit within 5-7 years, and what student count is the "floor" for a viable rural district under the state funding formula? What specific contingency scenarios should we develop for 25%, 35%, and 50% enrollment decline? [Accountant, Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

13. **Full lifecycle cost picture for facilities decisions.** Rather than looking only at the $4.2M deferred maintenance backlog, what are the capital replacement schedules for each of seven buildings — which reach end-of-life in 5, 10, or 20 years? What's the net present value of keeping fewer but better-maintained facilities versus spreading resources thinly across aging infrastructure, and should the economic analysis factor in closure costs (asbestos abatement, environmental remediation, demolition) alongside sale proceeds that in these rural towns may be essentially zero? [Analyst, Accountant]

14. **How do debt, bonds, and pension obligations combine across districts?** If District A has $8M in outstanding bonds while District B is debt-free, does debt get pooled proportionally, or do pre-consolidation district voters remain responsible for prior obligations? Are there opportunities for bond refinancing at better rates given the larger, more creditworthy consolidated tax base — and what hidden liabilities (underfunded pensions, capitalized leases, deferred maintenance, insolvent-district burdens) could consume the anticipated savings? A full forensic accounting is needed before proceeding. [Analyst, Accountant, Devil's Advocate]

15. **Property tax equalization and the political-economic consequences.** If District A has a strong commercial tax base and District C relies on residential-agricultural ($50M vs. $120M assessed value per student), consolidation equalizes rates — meaning higher-value districts effectively subsidize lower-value ones. What is the actual tax-rate impact on homeowners in each district, at what point does tax resistance threaten voter approval, and should the plan include phased tax equalization or revenue-neutral transition periods? [Analyst, Accountant]

16. **Alternative funding models beyond the state per-pupil formula.** If enrollment-driven funding is structurally declining (down 22% in a decade, projected down another 15%), the consolidated district remains vulnerable to the same demographic headwinds at larger scale. What supplementary revenue streams could diversify the model — county-wide sales tax earmarks, state consolidation grants, partnerships with community colleges for shared facility use, leasing unused space to municipal or nonprofit partners — and what's the revenue potential and timeline for each? [Accountant]

17. **The case for strategic investment versus across-the-board cuts.** What if the $3-5M in one-time transition costs were structured as investment — energy efficiency retrofits reducing operating costs 15-20%, or consolidation into modernized buildings that lower long-term maintenance with 5-10 year payback periods? Instead of cutting to sustainability, could the consolidated district grow into sustainability through strategic capital deployment? [Accountant]

## Topic Cluster 04: Systemic Dynamics, Feedback Loops, and Archetypes

18. **What self-amplifying feedback loops might consolidation trigger?** If consolidation improves quality through better resources, it could attract families from neighboring districts or private schools, increasing enrollment and funding, which further improves quality. But could the reverse loop dominate — longer bus rides and loss of community identity accelerate out-migration of young families, triggering more cuts, more service degradation, and more flight? Which reinforcing loop is stronger under what conditions, and what determines the tipping point where virtuous cycles flip vicious? [Systems Thinker]

19. **Success-to-the-Successful between remaining and closed-school towns.** If one town keeps its school and another loses theirs, the town with the school becomes more attractive to families, which raises property values and tax base, which funds better school facilities, which attracts more families — a self-reinforcing advantage. Meanwhile the town without its school enters a death spiral: families leave, property values fall, businesses close, tax revenue erodes. How would the consolidated district's resource allocation decisions either mitigate or accelerate this Success-to-the-Successful divergence, and what policies would ensure "losers" in the closure decision don't become permanent losers? [Systems Thinker]

20. **Fixes-that-Fail in facility and capacity decisions.** Closing the most dilapidated buildings saves maintenance immediately but reduces total capacity — meaning the district can't easily absorb enrollment if demographics stabilize or reverse, and would need to rebuild at far higher cost. Conversely, keeping underutilized buildings open "just in case" creates ongoing drain that prevents investment in remaining schools. What scenarios reveal which decision is truly adaptive vs. temporarily convenient, and how do we recognize a Fixes-that-Fail pattern before it locks in? [Systems Thinker]

21. **Eroding Goals and silent standard creep.** If consolidation is sold as "maintaining quality while cutting costs" but post-consolidation reality reveals persistent budget pressure despite administrative savings, the district may gradually redefine "acceptable" — freezing programs, expanding class sizes, reducing course offerings. Each step seems reasonable, but over five years the community's expectations quietly erode. What guardrails would prevent this Eroding Goals dynamic, and what observable early-warning indicators would reveal the system is trending toward decay before delay effects become irreversible? [Systems Thinker, Appreciative Inquirer]

22. **Ripple effects through local economies and social infrastructure.** When a town loses its school, it loses more than education — it loses the venue for community meetings, youth sports, voting, town events; local businesses lose foot traffic; property values decline, reducing tax revenue for town services (fire, road maintenance, police); the town's perceived viability as a place to live deteriorates, affecting employer recruitment. These second- and third-order consequences cascade and compound. What economic-and-social impact assessment framework would capture them rather than just the immediate school budget effects? [Systems Thinker, Audience Advocate, Devil's Advocate]

23. **Balancing pressures and the community "immune response."** Rural communities often have immune responses to perceived threats — parent co-ops, homeschooling networks, charter schools, political activism. If consolidation pushes too hard or too fast, what self-organizing alternatives might emerge, and where are the system's self-correcting forces strongest? How might consolidation work with rather than against these natural balancing pressures to find the realistic bounds of change? [Systems Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 05: Governance, Representation, and Political Feasibility

24. **Optimal governance structure for fair and trust-building representation.** What board composition (weighted geographic representation, at-large seats, allocation formulas for a 9-member board) ensures fair voice across towns with different populations while preventing perpetual outvoting of any single town? What governance innovations — town-level advisory councils with real authority, participatory budgeting, community-school compacts — could make consolidated governance feel MORE locally responsive, not less, and how can facility closure decisions give affected communities genuine input without creating de facto vetoes? [Analyst, Appreciative Inquirer, Politician-adjacent, Questioner]

25. **Coalition-building across three simultaneous voter approvals.** Since all three districts must approve and any one can defeat the entire plan, what is the minimum winning coalition in each district — specific concessions, facility commitments, tax-rate caps, board representation guarantees? Can the proposal be structured so every district sees a net benefit that outweighs its losses, and what sequencing of stakeholder endorsements, trusted local messengers, and targeted polling before the vote would build that coalition? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

26. **Irreducible minimums and "red lines" for stakeholder acceptance.** What specific conditions would cause each stakeholder group — teachers' unions, parents, fiscal conservatives, state officials, town identity advocates — to oppose consolidation? Can those conditions be modeled as red lines any proposal must avoid crossing, and how do we diagnose the nature of resistance in each district (principled disagreement, territorial threat, anxiety about job losses, inertia) so strategies address each type rather than treating opposition as monolithic? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

27. **Who are we not hearing from?** The analysis identifies unions, parents, town advocates, fiscal conservatives, and state officials — but what about students themselves, recent graduates who chose to stay or leave, young families deciding whether to move to the area, and the elderly population who may not have children in school but vote on property taxes? Who holds informal influence (town officials, local business owners, respected long-time families, media voices) that could derail or advance consolidation, and how are these marginalized-stakeholder and documentation-barrier voices structurally brought in rather than collapsed into a generic "inclusion" process? [Questioner, Audience Advocate]

28. **Political backlash, identity resistance, and the "referendum-on-other-grievances" risk.** Rural communities often view schools as symbols of local autonomy, and consolidation may be framed as state overreach or urban imposition. Could the vote become a referendum on unrelated grievances (taxes, state mandates, school performance)? How do we map the political landscape, identify and counter "loss of local control" narratives, and address the documented decline in institutional trust particularly in rural communities? [Devil's Advocate, Futurist]

## Topic Cluster 06: Labor, Staffing, and Human Capital

29. **Union contract reconciliation — cost, leverage, and power dynamics.** If three separate contracts must be harmonized (different salary schedules, benefits, seniority rules), what is the financial exposure in quantified ranges ($500K-$1.5M one-time, $200K-$400K annual), and how might unions leverage consolidation to extract the highest salary schedule or most generous benefits, potentially consuming much of the administrative savings? Beyond the immediate negotiation, how does a larger consolidated union gain more bargaining power vis-à-vis district and state — reshaping power dynamics in ways not visible for 5-10 years? What proactive framework identifies deal-breakers, walk-away points, and sequencing that prevents one union's concessions from becoming another's rejection point? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker, Questioner]

30. **Teacher recruitment, retention, and the reinvented teaching role.** These districts struggle to attract qualified teachers, and rural teacher pipelines are documented as declining. What if consolidation enabled new models — hybrid remote/in-person specialists, community-expert teaching partnerships, differentiated staffing with master teachers designing curriculum while facilitators guide daily learning, regional teacher collaboratives, or visiting-practitioner rotation? What specific evidence shows consolidated districts actually perform better at teacher recruitment, and how do we imagine three years out that this county has become known as a place where teachers WANT to build careers — what daily experience, professional community, autonomy, and resources would they describe? [Appreciative Inquirer, Questioner, Futurist]

## Topic Cluster 07: Student Experience — Daily Life, Belonging, and Emotional Costs

31. **The daily journey and what students give up.** For a student who shifts from a 15-minute bus ride to an hour-plus commute each way, how would the lost 1.5-2 hours daily affect homework, family responsibilities, extracurriculars, sleep, and simply being a kid? What does the ride actually feel like, and at what point do transportation costs (fuel, maintenance, driver wages, winter weather, rural roads) eat into administrative savings? [Audience Advocate, Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

32. **Belonging in transition, and what it feels like to be "the bus kids."** When students from three distinct school communities merge into consolidated buildings, what concrete experiences would help them form a shared identity rather than feeling like "those kids from [other town]"? What emotional costs do kids bear when they're bused 45 minutes to a building where everyone else knows each other — and how do those experiences ripple through friendships, confidence, and sense of place? [Audience Advocate, Empath — emotional-interior register]

33. **Invisible losses and what dies when a school closes.** Beyond test scores and finances, what do students lose when a small school closes that doesn't show up in any metric — the teachers who know their family history, the certainty of making the sports team, the sense that "this place is ours"? Beyond the building itself, what rituals, gatherings, and informal social glue disappear, and what emotional void does that leave for a town already watching its population dwindle? [Audience Advocate, Empath — emotional-interior register]

34. **Equity impacts on vulnerable students and families.** For students with physical disabilities, medical conditions, sensory processing needs, or individualized education programs (IEPs) developed in small districts with specific relationships — how does consolidation affect ride comfort, service delivery, and continuity of accommodations? For families without cars, reliable internet, or jobs that allow midday flexibility, what aspects of consolidation quietly exclude them from full participation, and how do translation services, cultural liaison staff, and language-access infrastructure for ELL families survive the transition? [Audience Advocate — structural-equity dimensions]

35. **Extracurricular, participation, and working-family access.** For students whose families can't drive to 6 a.m. band practice or late sports pickups, or who can't afford participation fees that might increase with consolidation, what opportunities quietly disappear? For parents who rely on older siblings for after-school childcare or on schools for before/after care, how do transportation and schedule changes affect their ability to hold jobs and manage the week? When school board meetings move to a central location 30 miles away, which families simply drop out of participation — who gains voice, and who loses it? [Audience Advocate — structural-equity dimensions]

36. **Student voice in the process itself.** If we asked students themselves what matters most about their school experience, what would they say that adults rarely consider, and how might their priorities differ from the financial and logistical factors dominating consolidation discussions? [Audience Advocate, Questioner]

## Topic Cluster 08: Community Identity, Emotional Reality, and Grief

37. **What "our school" means — and the grief of losing it.** For communities where the school is the only remaining institution that says "we exist as a town," what does taking that away feel like, and how do we acknowledge that grief without treating it as an obstacle to be overcome? What becomes of a town's pride when its name no longer appears on a school building — Friday night lights, the hometown marquee, the rivalries that gave communities their narrative all folded into someone else's identity? [Empath — emotional-interior register, Audience Advocate]

38. **Parent trust, proximity, and the felt experience of distance.** When school administration shifts from someone you might see at the grocery store to a centralized office 45 minutes away, how does that distance affect parents' willingness to advocate for their children, report concerns, or feel heard — and what are they actually grieving when they can no longer drop in for a game, watch practice, or simply see their child's teacher in daily life? [Audience Advocate, Empath — emotional-interior register]

39. **Winners, losers, surrender, and resentment.** If one town keeps the high school and another doesn't, how does that emotional math get calculated across decades of local history — and what does resentment cost a community? Has anyone put a dollar figure on the emotional tax of living in a town that feels it sacrificed its school for someone else's fiscal stability, and how do we distinguish the practical reality of insolvency from the visceral experience of being told you can no longer govern your own school? [Empath — emotional-interior register]

40. **How we talk about loss, and what "being heard" actually feels like.** The consolidation conversation keeps framing this as "gaining efficiency" — but what if we started by naming what people are actually afraid to lose? When the feasibility study, state pressure, and fiscal deadlines all signal consolidation is already decided, what does genuine participation feel like, and what would make people believe their input actually matters? [Empath — emotional-interior register]

41. **Long-standing rivalries and cultural reconciliation.** Sports rivalries, historical tensions, and community identities run deep. How do we distinguish "we're all in this together" from "your emergency became my loss," and what proactive school-culture initiatives acknowledge and respect distinct community identities while building new shared traditions — preventing student conflict, bullying, and factionalism without erasing heritage? [Devil's Advocate, Empath]

## Topic Cluster 09: Facilities, Transportation, and Operational Design

42. **Facility closure decisions as community development rather than extraction.** When school buildings close, communities experience genuine loss — but what if the process of deciding which facilities to repurpose became an opportunity to create early childhood centers, adult education hubs, community spaces, full-service community-school models that co-locate healthcare and adult programming, or revenue-generating partnerships? What transparent facility-decision framework, paired with transition assistance and community-use agreements for losing towns, could turn closure into community-asset creation — preserving option value for future hybrid, micro-school, or regional-service configurations? [Appreciative Inquirer, Devil's Advocate, Futurist, Questioner]

43. **Transportation optimization as a design problem, not just a cost.** With three districts becoming one larger geographic area, what is the route-efficiency analysis — maximum acceptable bus ride time, rural road conditions, winter weather constraints, fuel and maintenance scaling? What thresholds signal that transportation costs reverse the economies of scale, and how do we avoid designing a system that increases per-pupil transportation cost? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

44. **Systems integration complexity we're underestimating.** Beyond visible costs, what hidden integration efforts — student information systems, financial software, special education tracking, staff email, credentialing and HR systems — have a realistic timeline and cost? What "hidden" ongoing costs scale up with unified operations (expanded IT support, salary leveling upward as contracts reconcile, increased central-office overhead), and are there economies of scale that reverse beyond a certain size where larger becomes administratively more expensive rather than less? [Analyst, Accountant]

## Topic Cluster 10: Futures, Adaptability, and Long-Horizon Design

45. **Scenario planning, tripwires, and what happens beyond 2035.** If the consolidated district loses another 15% of students by 2035, what specific tripwire indicators or metrics should trigger further restructure, and how does the district avoid being in this same position a decade from now? What is the sustainable minimum scale for rural education, what contingency strategies exist for further restructuring, and what exit options or de-consolidation mechanisms exist if outcomes disappoint — given that district consolidation is effectively irreversible? What accountability mechanisms and correction triggers (3-year fiscal audit, performance benchmarks) should be baked in before committing? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker]

46. **Positioning for documented trend shifts — remote work, hybrid models, and state incentives.** Remote-work migration has accelerated rural in-migration since 2020 — if 2035 projections assume pre-2020 decline trends, what sensitivity analysis accounts for potential stabilization or reversal? How does consolidation timing align with multiple states actively incentivizing district consolidation through funding formula changes and grants (documented legislative activity since 2020), and with the documented expansion of hybrid learning, virtual charter schools, community-school models, and regional service cooperatives — preserving governance flexibility to participate in these emerging models? [Futurist]

47. **Failure modes, early-warning indicators, and the "what if we're wrong" plan.** If consolidation fails financially, politically, or educationally, what are the specific scenarios — consolidated district insolvency in year 4, voter rejection, mass teacher departures, quality bleed that accelerates enrollment loss — and what early warning indicators would signal we're on those trajectories? At what point does "quality bleed" financially undermine consolidation's fiscal benefits, and what ROI calculation justifies quality-preserving investments (teacher retention bonuses, keeping elementary schools local while consolidating secondary) during the transition? [Devil's Advocate, Analyst, Accountant]

48. **Coalition seeds and experiments that build confidence before the vote.** For consolidation to pass it needs champions in all three districts — who are the natural allies (parents who've experienced successful regional programs, teachers who've collaborated across districts, business leaders, students in cross-district activities), and what existing relationships could be the seed of a broader coalition? Before asking voters to decide on full consolidation, what smaller-scale experiments (shared superintendent, combined professional development, joint extracurricular programs, shared specialty courses) could demonstrate that collaboration works — what counts as evidence of success, and what quick wins are achievable this school year to build momentum? [Appreciative Inquirer — orphan register, Questioner]

## Appended Questions (Perspective, Provocation, and Distinctive-Register Personas)

### Provocateur (Append)

1. **What if "school districts" were the wrong unit of analysis entirely?** Imagine if education funding, governance, and enrollment were organized around something completely different — students traveling to learning rather than schools receiving them, teachers as independent contractors serving multiple micro-communities, or education organized around "learning seasons" rather than geographic boundaries. What would become possible if we stopped assuming consolidated districts are the only alternative to fragmented ones? *The insight: we're debating the architecture of a system whose fundamental unit may be obsolete.*

2. **What if the goal was to make consolidation fail spectacularly?** Imagine designing the consolidation process specifically to maximize community outrage, financial waste, and educational disruption. What would you do? Close all buildings simultaneously? Fire everyone and rehire? Auction off assets to private buyers? Now work backward: what does this reveal about which safeguards, pacing decisions, and transition structures actually matter most? *The insight: sometimes disaster planning reveals what success requires more clearly than optimization does.*

3. **What if every student attended a different "school" every week?** Imagine a system where students rotated through all 7 buildings on a rolling basis, where no town had a "local school" but everyone had access to every facility. Or imagine schools were organized around student interests rather than geography — the STEM building, the arts building, the nature school — and transportation became the primary cost instead of duplication. What would this reveal about what "community school" actually means versus what we assume it means? *The insight: we're protecting a concept of local schooling that may already be fiction for rural students facing long commutes anyway.*

4. **What if the districts deliberately made themselves LESS efficient before consolidating?** Imagine if each district, knowing consolidation was coming, accelerated hiring, raised salaries, took on debt, and expanded programs specifically to maximize the "savings" that consolidation would appear to generate. What if consolidation was structured as a rescue rather than a merger? How would the politics and narrative differ if the new district "saved" failing districts rather than "rationalizing" functional ones? *The insight: we're pretending consolidation is about efficiency when it's really about solvency — what if we named that directly?*

5. **What if declining enrollment was framed as an educational asset rather than a crisis?** Imagine a world where smaller student-teacher ratios, individualized attention, and underutilized facilities were positioned as luxury advantages rather than inefficiencies. What if the districts competed to attract students FROM each other using quality and experience rather than fighting against consolidation? What if the problem wasn't "not enough students" but "too much infrastructure designed for a different era"? *The insight: enrollment decline is only a failure if metrics designed for growth are applied to shrinkage — what if we designed for contraction instead?*

6. **What if we planned for the district to be dissolved in 20 years?** Imagine designing the consolidation with an explicit expiration date — create the unified district with the assumption that it will need to radically restructure or cease to exist in 2035. How would facility decisions, contract negotiations, transportation planning, and community engagement change if this were a transition state rather than a permanent solution? What interim structures would you build differently if you knew they were temporary? *The insight: treating consolidation as "forever" locks in decisions that should remain adaptable as conditions continue changing.*

7. **What if consolidation required every student to change schools, not just some?** Rather than closing buildings and displacing students from closed schools, imagine requiring ALL 1,800 students to reassess and re-enroll under a new system with no guaranteed placement. What if "our school is our town" was disrupted for everyone rather than protected for a few? How would the political dynamics and coalition-building change if no community received special protection? *The insight: we're trying to minimize disruption when sometimes equitable disruption creates different political possibilities.*

### Storyteller (Append)

1. **The Last Day at Maple Grove Elementary** Imagine walking through the halls of Maple Grove Elementary on what might be its final day of operation as a neighborhood school. The walls display photos of seven generations of students who learned to read in these classrooms. The principal's grandmother attended this school, as did her mother, and now her own daughter faces the prospect of being bused 45 minutes away. How might a community ritually honor and preserve 90 years of collective memory when a building stops being a school, and what transitional objects or ceremonies could help families carry their school's identity forward rather than experiencing it as an amputation?

2. **When "Consolidation" Arrives on the Doorstep** Picture the morning when Emma, a single mother working two jobs in the smallest district, receives the consolidation proposal in the mail. Her son Marcus has thrived under the personal attention of teachers who've known him since kindergarten—teachers who may now lose their seniority or be reassigned. Emma has no car and relies on the walking-distance school for before-school care and after-school activities while she works. What happens to families like Emma's when the infrastructure of daily life—care, transportation, trusted relationships—must be rebuilt, and what alternative support ecosystems would need to exist to prevent consolidation from becoming an existential crisis for the most vulnerable families?

3. **The New Kid, Again** Consider the experience from Javier's perspective, an eighth grader who has already changed schools three times due to family circumstances. His current school, though small and resource-constrained, has been the one constant where teachers know his learning needs and he's finally found a friend group. Now he faces the prospect of another transition to a consolidated middle school where he'll be one of 400 students instead of 80. What specific scaffolding and continuity bridges could transform this transition from a source of anxiety into an opportunity for expanded horizons, and how might the consolidation process actively identify and support students for whom stability is the foundation of learning?

4. **A Tale of Two School Years** Imagine following two teachers—Ms. Chen from the smallest district, who has taught science with outdated textbooks and no lab equipment, and Mr. Davis from the largest district, who has a fully-equipped lab but dwindling class sizes—as they navigate their first year in a consolidated district. Both have expertise to share and anxieties about losing autonomy. How might their professional journey unfold in ways that create a richer learning environment than either could provide alone, and what narrative frameworks could help them see themselves as collaborators in building something new rather than competitors in a zero-sum reorganization?

5. **The Day the Bus Route Changed** Picture a Tuesday morning six months after consolidation, when students from three formerly separate communities step off buses at what was once "their rival's" high school. Some arrive excited, others resentful, many anxious. The cafeteria now serves students who grew up hearing each other described as "those people from across the county." What happens in that first week, first semester, first year to weave these separate identities into something that doesn't erase differences but creates new shared experiences—through joint traditions, collaborative projects, spaces that honor all three communities' histories—and what role might students themselves play in authoring this new chapter rather than having it written for them?

6. **Who Gets to Tell the History?** When three districts consolidate, whose version of the past gets preserved in yearbooks, trophy cases, and school names? The current proposal suggests keeping one high school open, converting another to a middle school, and closing a third entirely. Students and alumni from the closed high school will see their championship banners, theater productions, and commencement rituals subsumed under another institution's identity. How might a consolidated district actively honor the distinct histories of all three communities while creating space for new shared stories to emerge, and what processes could involve current students in designing this layered identity rather than having it imposed by adults who made the consolidation decisions?

7. **The Wednesday Board Meeting** Visualize the pivotal school board meeting where the consolidation vote will take place. The room holds teachers worried about their contracts, administrators whose jobs may disappear, parents convinced consolidation will save their children's education, and grandparents certain it will destroy their town's soul. Outside, television trucks gather. The vote requires majority approval in all three districts, meaning any single community can veto the entire plan. What happens in that room—and the community conversations leading up to it—to move people from "defending our territory" to "imagine what we could build together," and what narratives about shared risk and mutual benefit might overcome the instinct to protect what's familiar even when familiar is no longer sustainable?

### Visionary (Append)

1. **What if we eliminated the concept of "school districts" entirely and replaced it with something unprecedented — a learning ecosystem where students, educators, and resources flow fluidly across geographic boundaries based on interest, need, and opportunity rather than fixed attendance zones?** Instead of three districts becoming one larger district, imagine a network model where physical buildings serve as nodes in a county-wide learning fabric, students engage through project-based cohorts that form and dissolve based on inquiry, and "school" becomes an activity rather than a place — what would this look like, what new problems would it solve that consolidation doesn't address, and what resistance would emerge from communities that have never known education without the geographic anchor?
