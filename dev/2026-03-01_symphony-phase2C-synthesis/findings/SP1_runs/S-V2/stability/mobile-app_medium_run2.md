# Brainstorming Questions: Mobile App Simplification

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Defining Core Value and the Product's True Job

1. **What "core value" actually means in this product**: Are we seeking the features most people use, the features creating the most passionate engagement, or the job users are hiring us to do — and how do we operationally distinguish these? What does our product do that no fitness app does, regardless of whether current usage data reflects it? Use a first-principles definitional framing (what IS this fundamentally?) before we let metrics pick winners. [Questioner, Analyst]

2. **Keystone features vs. noise in a Success-to-the-Successful ecosystem**: The 15-feature structure creates a **Success-to-the-Successful** dynamic — popular features attract more investment and grow more popular, while neglected features atrophy. Which features are structurally "keystone" (removing them collapses the ecosystem) versus merely noise, and how do we detect a core that is actually an unexpected combination of 2-3 features working together, which siloed usage metrics will systematically hide? [Systems Thinker, Analyst]

3. **Feature engagement matrix beyond usage counts**: What data points should we examine to determine which features truly drive retention versus which are merely noise — feature stickiness, dependency patterns, correlation with 30-day retention, and cascading structural impact if removed? How do we distinguish "gateway" features from "retention anchor" features per cohort? [Analyst, Systems Thinker]

4. **Feature popularity vs. feature value (the churning-user illusion)**: With 8% retention, most usage data is generated by users on their way out — features used early by churning users look "popular" but may be on-ramps to nowhere. What named risk does this create for feature-selection methodology, and how do we separate retention-driving features from initial-engagement features before we build the simplified product around the wrong signal? [Devil's Advocate, Analyst]

5. **Redefining the north star**: Current metrics (2% DAU/MAU, 47-second sessions, 8% retention) signal PMF failure — what leading indicators should we target instead? How do we avoid the perverse-incentive trap where bloat inflates "engagement" (users wander lost = longer sessions) while a simpler app that lets users accomplish things faster looks "worse" on the very metrics we're optimizing? [Analyst, Systems Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 02: Listening to Users — Research, Equity, and Unarticulated Needs

6. **Gap between stated preferences and revealed behavior**: The 47-second session and 2% DAU/MAU suggest users aren't engaging as intended. What might their actual behavior reveal about which features they genuinely value versus which ones they ignore or actively avoid, and have we asked users directly what problem they thought they were solving when they downloaded? [Audience Advocate, Questioner]

7. **Unarticulated needs users have normalized**: People accept bad UX as "just how apps work." What frustrations or workarounds have users silently normalized — and what does that reveal about what the app's true core value should be, beyond what any survey question would surface? [Audience Advocate]

8. **Self-selection bias in who we hear from**: Users willing to respond to surveys or interviews are likely the most engaged minority — so research risks over-optimizing for power users while ignoring the 92% who churn. What research methods can surface why the silent majority abandons without alienating the few who still care? [Devil's Advocate, Audience Advocate]

9. **Equity-structural barriers in a 15-feature app**: Which features create barriers for users with limited tech literacy, limited English proficiency, assistive-technology reliance, limited time, or limited mental bandwidth (single parent with 5 minutes; someone managing chronic illness)? How might prioritization change if we centered these users rather than the loudest, most-visible feedback — and what power dynamics are embedded in whose voices currently define "core"? [Audience Advocate]

10. **The 30-day survivors as a research population**: What would happen if we interviewed the 8% who stayed past 30 days and asked specifically what they'd stop using if we removed it? Would their answers surprise us — and which of them represent a future core versus a peripheral enthusiasm? [Questioner, Audience Advocate]

11. **Broader-life context beyond in-app behavior**: In-app data shows what users do; it does not show the constraints, motivations, and environments of their real, messy lives. How might understanding that broader context change which features actually matter — and how do we gather it without surveillance-style tracking? [Audience Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 03: Hidden Assets, Passionate Minorities, and Signals of Future Value

12. **Hidden assets inside the 180K-download base**: Even with low retention there are users who found value. Who are they, what specific use case or demographic found this app worthwhile, and what are the 2% daily users doing that keeps them coming back that we could design more of? [Appreciative Inquirer, Audience Advocate]

13. **Peak moments and features that sparked joy**: When has the app worked beautifully? Which single feature (of the 15) elicited the strongest positive response, sustained engagement, or loyalty — and what made it resonate (utility, experience, social connection, something else)? How do we design the entire product to create more of those specific moments? [Appreciative Inquirer]

14. **Small-but-passionate user bases as leading indicators vs. dead ends**: A feature with 2% adoption but 80% retention among its users could be a foothold into an underserved niche that grows — or a dead end. What leading indicators distinguish "early adopters of the future core" from "enthusiastic users of a peripheral feature," and how do we avoid cutting tomorrow's valuable feature based on today's adoption numbers? [Systems Thinker, Appreciative Inquirer, Questioner]

15. **Passionate-minority identification before cuts**: How do we identify features with small but passionate user bases before we cut them, so we don't drive away our most vocal advocates (however few)? What structured listening would find them, and what might their experience teach us about genuine value that aggregate metrics obscure? [Questioner, Appreciative Inquirer]

## Topic Cluster 04: Simplify vs. Relaunch — Strategic Transition Design

16. **Incremental simplification vs. clean relaunch — comparative risk profiles**: What are the comparative outcome distributions for gradual simplification versus clean relaunch, considering user confusion, technical complexity, market perception, and team bandwidth? How do we avoid both the **erosion-of-goals** pattern where incremental cuts never add up to real transformation, and the shock of a relaunch the system cannot recover from? [Analyst, Systems Thinker, Questioner]

17. **The relaunch assumption itself**: A "clean relaunch" assumes users will give us a second chance, but the app store is full of failed "2.0" relaunches where users had already moved on. What evidence suggests our existing base will return, and are we over-indexing on retention of current users versus acquisition of net-new ones who never experienced the bloated version? [Devil's Advocate]

18. **Simplify-into-nothing trap**: If users call the app "overwhelming," what if the real issue is poor information architecture or onboarding rather than feature count? How do we validate that simplification will actually improve retention before we irreversibly remove features, and how do we distinguish "overwhelming because too much" from "overwhelming because poorly designed"? [Devil's Advocate, Questioner]

19. **A/B testing simplification before committing**: Could we ship a version with one feature temporarily disabled and see who complains — or who doesn't notice? What experimental design lets us test feature combinations without alienating users, and what statistical-significance thresholds should we set before committing? [Questioner, Analyst]

20. **Cascading churn and the maximum acceptable loss**: Each feature cut may trigger abandonment from its niche users, and simultaneous cuts could create compounding churn exceeding current attrition — a classic **cascading-failure** risk. What's the maximum acceptable user loss we can sustain, and what contingency plans exist if simplification backfires and accelerates decline? [Devil's Advocate, Analyst]

21. **Post-launch validation dashboard**: Once we relaunch, what early-warning signs and success metrics should we monitor at 30/60/90 days to distinguish "users mourning removed features" from "genuine product-market fit" from "solved the wrong problem"? [Analyst]

## Topic Cluster 05: Financial Runway, Technical Debt, and the Execution Timeline

22. **Unit economics of feature simplification**: What's the cost structure per feature — engineering maintenance, customer support, server costs, opportunity cost — and how do we calculate the ROI of cutting features in terms of resources freed versus revenue/user-impact risk? [Analyst]

23. **Runway optimization under an 8-month constraint**: With 8 months of funding remaining and a team of 8, what's the break-even analysis for different simplification strategies? How much does reducing the feature surface lower burn rate versus extend runway, and at what point does simplification trade long-term viability for short-term survival? [Analyst]

24. **Technical debt quantification and the "excise-carefully" problem**: How do we measure the maintenance cost embedded in each feature, and what's the difference between "delete-and-forget" features versus deeply integrated ones? Could removing a feature cost more engineering than maintaining it — consuming the 8-month runway without delivering a working product? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

25. **Fixes-that-Fail from hidden backend dependencies**: Features that look independent in the UI may share auth, data models, notifications, or analytics pipelines in the backend. Cutting may leave **ghost code** (maintenance burden with no user value) or break features that depend on shared infrastructure — a classic **Fixes-that-Fail** archetype where we "simplify" the product but the technical complexity remains. How do we map dependencies before cutting? [Systems Thinker, Analyst]

26. **8-month runway as a false constraint**: Eight months sounds sufficient, but hidden dependencies, database migrations, or user backlash could burn it. What does a realistic timeline look like with buffer for unknown unknowns, and at what point do we pivot from clean-relaunch to incremental simplification? How do we avoid **Shifting-the-Burden** decisions where time pressure makes us skip user research now and pay for it later? [Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 06: Team, Decision-Making, and Morale

27. **False-consensus and ownership-bias in a 6-engineer team**: A 6-engineer team likely has 6 different opinions about the "core," and each person may unconsciously champion features they built or use. What decision framework could objectively identify core value without letting ownership bias or team politics drive the outcome? [Devil's Advocate]

28. **One-person PM risk**: With a single PM, who determines which user feedback matters, which research methods apply, and ultimately what "core value" means? What guardrails ensure this pivotal decision isn't essentially one person's biases and limited perspective? [Devil's Advocate]

29. **Team's brightest moments and strengths to amplify**: When has this team felt most energized, proud, or effective — perhaps shipping something meaningful or seeing user feedback validate their work? What conditions made that moment possible, and what strengths does each member bring that a leaner product could amplify? [Appreciative Inquirer]

30. **Team-aligned product vision**: Given the team's existing strengths (technical capabilities, design sensibilities, domain expertise), what kind of simplified product would let them do their best work and make the simplification feel like liberation rather than loss? [Appreciative Inquirer]

31. **"Killing darlings" and rebuilding psychological investment**: If engineers have spent months or years building features now being deleted, how will they stay motivated — especially when "core" features may require them to work on code they didn't write? What mechanisms rebuild ownership, and what's the smallest intervention that flips the dynamic from defensive attachment to proactive pruning (addressing the **ownership-resistance-to-maintenance-burden** reinforcing loop)? [Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker, Appreciative Inquirer]

32. **Team-morale reframing as psychological impact**: How do we help engineers and designers feel proud of simplification rather than feeling they "failed" by building features now being cut? What framing, ritual, or recognition turns "we're deleting your work" into "we're focusing your work"? [Questioner, Appreciative Inquirer]

## Topic Cluster 07: User Communication, Trust, and Co-Creation

33. **Communication strategy framework for announcing removals**: What's the optimal sequence and messaging for announcing feature removal — balancing transparency and trust-building against minimizing churn? What metrics tell us if we're getting it right? [Analyst]

34. **Avoiding the Shifting-the-Burden communication trap**: Heavy "we're simplifying for you" messaging and reassurance may cause users to depend on us to frame every change — a **Shifting-the-Burden** pattern where external prompts replace intrinsic motivation and create self-amplifying need for handholding. How do we communicate without creating dependency? [Systems Thinker]

35. **Migration with grace for loyal feature users**: How might we honor the commitment of users whose favorite feature is being cut? What would a graceful transition look like — one that acknowledges loyalty while guiding them toward the core value, turning potential churn into deeper engagement? [Appreciative Inquirer, Audience Advocate]

36. **Simplification as loss vs. simplification as clarity**: The team sees this as "refocusing," but users may experience loss. What would it feel like to be a user who deeply relied on a now-removed feature, and how might we understand and address that experience? Could we frame it as "focusing on what matters most" rather than "taking things away"? [Audience Advocate, Questioner]

37. **Genuine user co-creation in cut decisions**: Rather than communicating decisions *after* they're made, how could the community be brought into the decision-making in a way that respects their agency and acknowledges their investment? [Audience Advocate]

38. **Rebuilding trust with burned-out users**: Users who stuck around despite poor experience may feel betrayed or skeptical. What would it take to demonstrate genuine commitment to their needs rather than business survival — and can the relaunch be an invitation into a new chapter rather than a reset? [Audience Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 08: Emotional Experience and Felt Journey

39. **Emotional arc from app-open to app-close**: Users may describe feeling "overwhelmed" or "lost," but what's the specific emotional journey — from app open to close — where do they feel confusion, frustration, or relief, and at what precise moment do they decide to disengage? How do different segments (25-year-old fitness enthusiast vs. 55-year-old getting healthy) experience the bloat differently? [Audience Advocate]

40. **Notification overload as disproportionate burden**: Users report "too many notifications," but which communities are most impacted? Are we driving away the very users who might benefit most from a simplified experience — and what does that reveal about who the simplified product should serve? [Audience Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 09: Post-Relaunch Vision and Amplification

41. **The app users champion — three years out**: Imagine three years from now and this app has become something users genuinely champion and recommend. What would they say, what specific feeling or outcome would they describe, and how would it fit their daily routine in a way that feels indispensable rather than overwhelming? [Appreciative Inquirer]

42. **The "one thing" evolution**: If the app became known for doing one thing exceptionally well — the thing users think "I can't live without this" about — what would that be, and how would that single focus shape onboarding, daily use, and word-of-mouth? [Appreciative Inquirer]

43. **Amplifying what already works**: Based on features that already show promise, what structures, practices, or product principles would reliably amplify those strengths? If the social-connection features work best, what would the whole app look like if built around that core? [Appreciative Inquirer]

44. **Virtuous cycle from reduced feature surface**: Six engineers maintaining 3 features suddenly have excess capacity — a self-amplifying opportunity where each remaining feature becomes dramatically better, attracting more users, data, resources, and further improvement. But what structural constraints prevent the **slow-return-to-bloat** pattern as scope creep refills the capacity? [Systems Thinker, Appreciative Inquirer]

## Topic Cluster 10: Low-Risk Experiments and First Steps

45. **First experiments this month**: What small, low-risk experiments could the team try this month to test which features truly represent core value — what would you measure, and how would you engage users in ways that make them feel part of the product's evolution rather than subjects of it? [Appreciative Inquirer]

46. **Building from existing assets toward MVP reset**: What resources, capabilities, or assets are already in place — technical infrastructure, user insights, team skills, community relationships, learnings from the current feature set — that could accelerate the move toward a focused product and make this feel like an evolution rather than a restart? [Appreciative Inquirer]

## Appended Questions (Perspective, Provocation, and Distinctive-Register Personas)

### Provocateur (Append)

1. **What if you kept exactly ONE feature and made it absurdly worse?** Imagine you stripped the app down to just workout tracking, but then removed all logging, history, progress visualization, and data—leaving only the immediate act of pressing a button that says "I worked out" before the app closes itself. What would this useless button reveal about what users actually crave from the tracking experience? *The real insight: is the value in the data or in the ritual of acknowledgment?*

2. **What if the app did nothing but DELETE features for the next 8 months?** Every week, one feature disappears permanently with no replacement, no explanation, and no user choice—until month 9 when the app becomes entirely blank. What would user behavior and retention patterns in this controlled demolition reveal about which features create genuine dependence versus which create mere noise? *The real insight: what if feature removal itself—rather than the final simplified state—was the product experience that taught you what matters?*

3. **What if you launched a "competitor" that was intentionally terrible?** Create a clone app with all 15 features, but each one is broken in a specific, maddening way—workout tracking only logs every third workout, meal planning displays recipes you can't actually cook, social feed shows posts from people who don't exist. Market it aggressively to your existing users. What does watching them struggle with the anti-version reveal about which brokenness they'll tolerate and which sends them away forever? *The real insight: what if you could discover core value by studying which imperfections function as dealbreakers?*

4. **What if your 180K downloads were 180K different people who never opened the app twice?** Imagine the retention disaster is actually a feature—your app is designed for single-use transformation, like a ceremonial rite of passage after which users are "graduated" and should never return. How would you redesign the onboarding experience to complete someone's entire fitness journey in 47 seconds? *The real insight: what if the lifetime value model is wrong for this problem and the real opportunity is in perfect, self-contained moments?*

5. **What if you had to explain this app to someone from 1995 who has never seen a mobile phone?** They understand "fitness," "food," "friends," and "shopping" as physical, non-digital experiences. As you describe your app, which features make sense in their world and which become meaningless? What does the alien-ness of "social fitness" or "digital meal planning" reveal about assumptions you've never questioned? *The real insight: which parts of your product exist only because they're possible in software, not because they actually solve human problems?*

### Storyteller (Append)

1. **What would the "first five minutes" experience look like for a returning user who opens the simplified app for the first time, and what emotional state should we be designing toward?** Imagine it's Tuesday morning and Sarah, who previously faced 15 features and 6 navigation tabs, opens the new version. Walk through what she sees, what she feels, and what she can accomplish in those critical first minutes. How do we ensure the transition from confusion to clarity happens within a single session? What does "success" look like in that first interaction—should she feel relief, excitement, curiosity, or something else entirely?

2. **How might the "moment of deletion" unfold for different types of users when features they've incorporated into their routines disappear, and what narrative threads could help them feel excited rather than abandoned?** Consider Marcus, who uses the supplement store feature weekly, versus Priya, who relies on the progress journal as her primary touchpoint. When they open the simplified app and discover these features gone, what happens in the seconds before they either delete the app or give the new version a chance? What story could the app tell them in that moment—and through what channels (push notifications, onboarding, email, in-app messaging)—that would transform a sense of loss into curiosity about the newly focused experience?

3. **What would a "day in the life" of a power user look like if the app successfully delivered on its core promise with just 3-4 features, and how would that differ from their current experience of fragmented engagement across 15 half-baked tools?** Paint the picture of someone who currently spends 47 seconds per session bouncing between features, trying to extract value from a buffet of mediocrity. Now show us what their day becomes when the app provides one or two moments of genuine delight or utility, and how those moments compound into daily habit formation. What does the notification ecosystem look like when you're not fighting for attention across 15 features, and how does the user's relationship with the app fundamentally change?

4. **How might the engineering team's daily work life transform when they shift from maintaining 15 half-built features to perfecting 3-4 world-class experiences, and what "micro-moments" of professional satisfaction will replace the current burnout cycle?** Walk us through the standup meeting six months from now. What language are engineers using to describe their work? How do conversations about quality, testing, and user feedback change when they're not spread thin across a feature graveyard? What moments of pride—seeing a user review, watching usage metrics, shipping a polished experience—replace the current reality of constant bug fixes and technical debt management? How does the team's relationship with the codebase itself evolve when they're not afraid to touch it?

5. **What does the "funeral" scene look like when the team decides which features to cut, and how can we turn a potentially demoralizing process into a moment of clarity and shared purpose?** Imagine the product owner standing at the whiteboard with 15 feature cards, knowing that 11 of them are about to be "killed." How does that conversation unfold? What frameworks or rituals could transform this from a series of painful decisions into a collective act of strategic vision? How do we ensure that every person in that room—whether they built the meditation guide or the friend finder—leaves feeling energized about the new direction rather than mourning their specific contribution? What narrative about the company's future could make sacrificing the present feel like a victory rather than a loss?

### Visionary (Append)

1. **[What if fitness wasn't something you track, but something that emerges naturally through a fundamentally different relationship with your body?]** Instead of asking which features to keep or cut, what if we completely reimagined the category: What would a fitness product look like if it were designed around joy, emergence, and organic behavior change rather than metrics, tracking, and discipline? What if the core value isn't "helping people work out" but something entirely different that doesn't have a current analog in the fitness app space?

2. **[Imagine if the app could disappear entirely as an interface and instead become a presence that anticipates and supports without requiring active engagement?]** What would it mean to invert the relationship between user and app — where the app exists not as something you open, but as something that works on your behalf in the background, creating interventions at the moment they're needed without requiring you to navigate tabs or manage features? What would a zero-UI fitness product look like?

3. **[What if the core value isn't any single feature, but the emotional state users hope to achieve through using the product — and what if we could reverse-engineer from that destination rather than from existing features?]** Rather than asking "what features do people use most," what if we asked "what emotional transformation do people seek when they download this app" and built backward from there? What if the product that delivers that transformation doesn't look like a fitness app at all, but resembles something from a completely different domain (therapy, coaching, entertainment, community)?

4. **[What if user retention isn't about features at all, but about identity transformation — and what if we designed for that psychological shift instead of for engagement?]** Consider that the 2% DAU/MAU ratio might not be a feature problem but an identity problem: users don't see themselves as "people who use this app." What if we completely rethought onboarding, not as a tutorial, but as an identity-forming ritual that fundamentally reshapes how users view themselves? What would an app look like if its primary function was identity construction rather than fitness tracking?

5. **[What if instead of choosing 3-4 core features, we created a product with no features at all — just a single, coherent behavior that users engage in repeatedly?]** Imagine a fitness app with no tracking, no planning, no social features, no content — just one elegant, repeatable action that somehow produces fitness outcomes. What would that one action be? What if the app became a "one-button" product where pressing that button initiates a cascade of value without requiring navigation, configuration, or decisions?

### Connector (Append)

1. **Feature pruning as ecosystem succession** The challenge of cutting features mirrors ecological succession, where crowded ecosystems self-thin to let dominant species thrive. In forest ecology, "self-thinning" creates space for the strongest organisms to flourish. What if we viewed the 15 features as competing species in an ecosystem — how would we identify which features are the "keystone species" whose presence enables the entire ecosystem to function, and which are the invasive species crowding out the core value?

2. **The IKEA effect paradox in user attachment** There's a structural parallel between users attached to niche features and the psychological phenomenon known as the IKEA effect — people overvalue things they've invested effort in building or using. But behavioral economics also shows the "endowment effect" creates irrational attachment to things simply because we own them. How do we distinguish between genuine value-creating features and features users are irrationally attached to due to sunk-cost fallacy — and could we borrow techniques from "loss aversion" research to help users let go without feeling betrayed?

3. **UI complexity as information entropy** Information theory uses entropy to measure uncertainty and complexity in communication systems. The current app's 6 navigation tabs plus hamburger menu represents high interface entropy — users must process too much information before finding value. Claude Shannon's work showed that reducing noise increases signal transmission. If we treated every UI element as adding "noise" to the user's cognitive load, what entropy-reduction principles from information theory could guide which elements are signal (essential) versus noise (eliminable)?

4. **Museum curation as a model for feature preservation** When museums face space constraints, they don't simply throw away lesser works — they rotate collections, loan to other institutions, or create digital archives. The 15 features likely each have some users (even if small). Rather than binary keep/kill decisions, what if we borrowed from museum curation practices: could some features be "rotated out" (temporarily disabled but preserved), "loaned" (spun off as separate apps), or "digitized archived" (export functionality for power users while removing from core experience)?

5. **Surgical triage vs. architectural restoration** There are two different structural parallels for feature removal: surgical triage (emergency room — save what can be saved, let go of what's too far gone) and architectural restoration (preserve historical integrity while modernizing function). Triage is fast but destructive; restoration is slow but honors the original vision. The 8-month runway creates urgency, but the "jack of all trades" problem suggests vision clarity is needed. Which metaphor better serves this situation — and how does that choice change the feature selection criteria?
