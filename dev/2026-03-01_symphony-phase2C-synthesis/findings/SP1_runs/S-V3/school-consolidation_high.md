# Brainstorming Questions: School Consolidation

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Foundational Framing and Problem Definition

1. **Is consolidation the right unit of analysis?** At its most fundamental level, what are the essential educational services these 1,800 students actually need, and is "consolidating three districts into one" the only (or even best) way to deliver them? Could shared services agreements, cooperative purchasing, a "lead district" model, or functional/virtual consolidation address the fiscal pressures without requiring full administrative unification — and why are we treating three separate political entities as the natural starting point rather than student cohorts, taxpayer capacity, or geographic constraints? [First Principles Thinker, Questioner]

2. **Is the core problem decline or structure?** Is the problem that the districts are structured incorrectly (three small districts instead of one) — or that rural education is inherently incompatible with declining enrollment and fixed infrastructure costs? If consolidation solves the immediate financial crisis, what fundamental challenges remain unaddressed, and are we making a permanent structural change based on a temporary state-funding-formula advantage that could be recalibrated once other districts consolidate? [First Principles Thinker, Questioner]

3. **Why is enrollment decline treated as inevitable?** What proactive measures could reverse enrollment trends — attracting families, developing specialty programs that draw students from neighboring districts, becoming destination schools — rather than restructuring around a projected 15% further decline? Could the energy required for consolidation be redirected toward reforming the state funding formula that penalizes small districts in the first place? [Questioner]

4. **Reimagining without legacy assumptions.** If we were building educational delivery for a rural county from scratch today — without district boundaries, fixed buildings, or in-person-only assumptions — what would the optimal structure look like? How much fiscal pressure comes from clinging to a model that assumes all students must be physically present in designated buildings every day, and what does the consolidation calculus look like if we relax that assumption? [First Principles Thinker]

5. **The efficiency vs. accessibility tension.** Any consolidation that improves efficiency (fewer administrators, shared services, optimized transportation) inherently reduces accessibility (longer bus rides, distant decision-makers, less local accountability). This isn't a trade-off to optimize — it's a fundamental conflict. How do we decide which towns bear which costs, and what principle guides that allocation? [First Principles Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 02: Financial Break-Even and Transition Economics

1. **True break-even with transition costs layered in.** Beyond the $1.2M annual administrative savings, what are the one-time transition costs — legal fees for district dissolution, union contract buyouts, IT/SIS integration, facility closure/decommissioning, early-retirement incentives — and how many years until recovery? If transition costs run $3-5M, consolidation operates at a net loss for 3-4 years; can the districts absorb this period, especially the smallest district with only a 2-year fund balance, and what contingency reserves should be modeled? [Accountant, Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

2. **Unit economics under continued enrollment decline.** If enrollment declines another 15% by 2035 (≈270 students ≈ $2.4M in state funding at current per-pupil rates), does the $1.2M in administrative savings still leave the consolidated district running a structural deficit within 5-7 years? What is the "floor" enrollment for a viable rural district under the state formula, and at what crossover point do consolidation savings no longer offset enrollment losses? [Accountant, Analyst]

3. **What costs are being omitted or underestimated?** Beyond visible savings, what about increased transportation distances and fuel, IT/SIS/HR/financial-software integration, curriculum standardization, credentialing reconciliation, expanded central-office overhead, and potentially higher union-negotiated wages if staff gain bargaining power through consolidation? What is the realistic timeline and cost for each "hidden" integration effort? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Questioner]

4. **Hidden liabilities from the insolvent district.** The smallest district's 2-year runway suggests existing distress. If that district carries significant debt, underfunded pensions, or deferred maintenance liabilities, the consolidated district inherits these burdens — potentially consuming much of the anticipated savings. What is the full forensic accounting of all obligations, and do pre-consolidation voters remain responsible for their prior bond obligations or does debt get pooled proportionally? [Devil's Advocate, Analyst, Accountant]

5. **Contingency if projected savings fail to materialize.** What if the projected savings are smaller than anticipated, new costs emerge, or enrollment declines continue even after consolidation? What is the contingency plan if the consolidated district still faces structural deficits five years post-merger, and what accountability mechanisms and correction triggers (fiscal audit after 3 years, performance benchmarks) should exist before committing — given that de-consolidation is effectively irreversible? [Questioner, Devil's Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 03: Taxpayer, Bond, and Revenue Model Design

1. **Property tax equalization across unequal bases.** If District A has $50M assessed value per student and District C has $120M — or if one district has a strong commercial base while another relies on residential-agricultural property — consolidation equalizes this, which means higher-base taxpayers effectively subsidize lower-base communities. What is the actual tax rate impact on homeowners in each district under consolidation, and should the plan include phased tax equalization or revenue-neutral transition periods to prevent sharp tax increases from killing voter approval? [Accountant, Analyst]

2. **Debt and bond obligation mechanics.** Each district likely has different bond obligations, pension liabilities, and lease commitments. Do these obligations combine or are they assumed at different rates post-merger? Are there refinancing opportunities given the larger, more creditworthy consolidated tax base — or hidden liabilities (underfunded pensions, capitalized leases) that don't appear on operating budgets but could become consolidated district obligations? [Accountant, Analyst]

3. **Diversifying beyond the per-pupil formula.** If enrollment-driven state funding is structurally declining (down 22% in a decade, projected down another 15%), the consolidated district remains vulnerable to the same demographic headwinds at a larger scale. What supplementary revenue could diversify the model: county-wide sales tax earmarks, state consolidation grants, community college partnerships for shared facility use, or leasing unused school space to municipal/nonprofit partners? [Accountant]

4. **Investment vs. across-the-board cuts.** What if the $3-5M in one-time transition costs were structured as investment — energy efficiency retrofits that reduce operating costs 15-20%, or modernized facility consolidation with 5-10 year payback periods — rather than cuts? Could the consolidated district grow into sustainability through strategic capital deployment rather than shrink into it? [Accountant]

## Topic Cluster 04: Facilities, Maintenance, and Lifecycle Decisions

1. **Full lifecycle and NPV of facility decisions.** Rather than looking only at the $4.2M deferred maintenance backlog, what are the capital replacement schedules for each building — which facilities reach end-of-life in 5, 10, or 20 years, and how does timing affect whether to consolidate, renovate, or close each? What is the net present value of keeping fewer but better-maintained facilities versus spreading resources thinly across aging infrastructure, and are sale proceeds from closed buildings realistic or essentially zero in these rural markets? [Analyst, Accountant]

2. **Fixes that Fail in facility decisions.** Closing the most dilapidated buildings saves maintenance costs immediately but reduces total capacity, meaning the district can't easily absorb enrollment if demographics stabilize or reverse — they'd need to rebuild at far higher cost. Alternatively, keeping underutilized buildings "just in case" creates ongoing drain that prevents investment in remaining schools. The obvious fix in either direction plants the seeds of its own failure. What scenarios would reveal which decision is truly adaptive vs. temporarily convenient? [Systems Thinker]

3. **New construction as consolidation dividend.** What if consolidating makes it possible to build one modern, energy-efficient facility that replaces multiple aging buildings? Could deferred maintenance costs actually justify new construction as part of consolidation, and what financing mechanisms would make that feasible? [Questioner]

4. **Facilities as community assets, not losses.** When school buildings close, communities experience genuine loss — but what if facility decisions became an opportunity to create new community value: early childhood centers, adult education hubs, community spaces, revenue-generating partnerships, co-located healthcare/library/senior programming? What existing community strengths could those repurposed buildings serve, and what process would make those decisions feel like community development rather than extraction? [Appreciative Inquirer, Devil's Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 05: Governance, Representation, and Local Control

1. **Optimal governance structure and board allocation.** If the unified board has, say, 9 members, what allocation formula ensures fair representation across towns with different populations while preventing any single town from being perpetually outvoted on decisions affecting its schools? What board composition (geographic, at-large, appointed vs. elected) gives each former district sufficient voice without creating veto points and gridlock? [Analyst]

2. **Governance innovations that build trust.** What governance innovations — weighted board representation, town-level advisory councils with real authority, participatory budgeting at the school level, community-school compacts — could make consolidated governance feel MORE locally responsive, not less? Which builds on existing democratic traditions in these towns rather than overriding them? [Appreciative Inquirer]

3. **Alternative governance structures beyond full merger.** Why assume one unified district is the only structure? What about a confederation model where local boards retain authority over certain decisions while coordinating regionally; a "lead district" model providing administrative services to others; or functional consolidation where specific departments merge independently? [Questioner]

4. **Adaptive governance for an evolving education landscape.** The documented expansion of virtual charter schools, state-level online programs, and regional service cooperatives suggests rural education delivery is decentralizing. How might governance structures balance local accountability with the ability to participate in emerging regional partnerships and shared-service models? [Futurist]

5. **Surrender vs. autonomy: the felt experience of governance change.** When consolidation is framed as fiscal necessity but feels like losing control, how do we distinguish between the practical reality of insolvency and the visceral experience of being told you can no longer govern your own school? What does "being heard" feel like in a process that feels predetermined by feasibility studies, state pressure, and fiscal deadlines? [Empath]

## Topic Cluster 06: Political Feasibility and Voter Approval

1. **Mapping feasibility across three simultaneous votes.** Since all three districts must approve, what is the coalition-building strategy — does this require simultaneous wins, or can the proposal be structured so each district sees a net benefit outweighing its losses? What specific concessions (facility commitments, tax rate caps, board representation) are required to win in each district, given that any one district's residents can veto the entire consolidation? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

2. **Irreducible minimums and red lines for each stakeholder group.** What specific conditions would cause each stakeholder group (teachers' unions, parents, fiscal conservatives, state officials) to oppose consolidation — and can those conditions be modeled as "red lines" that any proposal must avoid crossing? What are the specific scenarios where the "no" coalition becomes larger and more organized than proponents, and what early warning signs would indicate that's happening? [Analyst, Questioner]

3. **Voter rejection as signal, not communication failure.** We're treating "getting voters to approve this" as a political challenge to be solved through messaging. But what if voter rejection isn't a communication failure — it's a signal that the fundamental value proposition is inadequate? What would a proposal look like if it were so compelling that opposition would be irrational rather than justified? [First Principles Thinker]

4. **Reframing the narrative and terminology.** Instead of "consolidation" or "merger," what if this was presented as "creating a premier rural education network" or "building a county-wide learning community"? How much of the resistance is to the practical changes versus the terminology and framing of the proposal — and how might the narrative shift from "rural education is deficient" to "rural education is pioneering"? [Questioner]

5. **Referendum risk: unrelated grievances on the ballot.** Communities might use the consolidation vote to express frustration with unrelated issues (taxes, state mandates, school performance, board decisions). This risks defeat even among residents who see the logical case for consolidation. Rural communities often view their schools as symbols of local autonomy, so consolidation may be framed as state overreach or urban imposition, activating broader cultural resistance beyond the specific merits. [Devil's Advocate]

6. **Institutional-trust headwinds.** Trust in institutions, including public schools, has declined measurably over the past decade, particularly in rural communities. Given that consolidation requires voter approval, what strategies address this documented trust deficit and the specific skepticism toward centralization and loss of local control? [Futurist]

## Topic Cluster 07: Labor, Contracts, and Union Dynamics

1. **Union contract reconciliation: cost exposure and leverage.** If three separate contracts must be harmonized (different salary schedules, benefits, seniority rules), what is the financial exposure — quantified in ranges (e.g., $500K-$1.5M one-time, $200K-$400K annual)? Contract reconciliation presents significant leverage: unions could demand that the highest salary schedule apply to all members. What are the non-negotiable demands and walk-away points for each union, and what proactive negotiation framework identifies deal-breakers and trade-offs? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Questioner]

2. **Will consolidation actually improve teacher recruitment?** What if a larger district offers less individualized support and more bureaucracy that actually deters some teachers? What specific evidence shows that consolidated districts perform better than small districts in attracting qualified staff to rural areas, particularly as rural teacher shortages accelerate and certification requirements tighten? [Questioner, Futurist]

3. **Shifting power dynamics of a larger consolidated union.** Beyond the reconciliation itself, a larger consolidated union gains more bargaining power vis-à-vis the district and the state. Future labor conflicts involve higher stakes, more leverage, and potentially more disruptive outcomes — reshaping power dynamics in ways that won't be visible for 5-10 years. How does this long-arc power shift change the consolidation calculus? [Systems Thinker]

4. **The teacher experience that attracts talent.** Imagine three years from now, this county has become known as a place where teachers WANT to work and build careers. What would be different about their daily experience — professional community, support, autonomy, resources? How might consolidation, rather than being a threat to teacher satisfaction, actually create those conditions, including through remote-teaching and shared-specialist models emerging in rural districts nationally? [Appreciative Inquirer, Futurist]

## Topic Cluster 08: Transportation, Student Experience, and Educational Quality

1. **Transportation optimization and the cost-quality trade-off.** With three districts becoming one larger geographic area, what is the route efficiency analysis — maximum acceptable bus ride time, the point at which transportation costs eat into administrative savings, and rural road / winter weather constraints? Could consolidation paradoxically increase per-pupil transportation costs given longer routes, fuel, maintenance, and driver wages? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

2. **Measuring educational quality impacts before and after.** Beyond test scores, what metrics — class size variability, course offerings, teacher retention rates, extracurricular participation, specialist access — should be tracked to determine whether consolidation actually improves or degrades educational outcomes? Longer bus rides, larger class sizes, and fewer extracurricular spots could undermine quality even as efficiency improves. [Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

3. **Learning from what each district already does well.** Each of these three districts does something exceptionally well — perhaps one excels at special education inclusion, another at community partnerships, another at fiscal efficiency despite constraints. What if consolidation were designed around systematically amplifying each district's existing strengths across the whole county rather than creating one-size-fits-all uniformity? [Appreciative Inquirer]

4. **The "bus kids" experience and belonging in transition.** When students from three distinct school communities merge into consolidated buildings, what concrete experiences would help them form a shared identity rather than feeling like "those kids from [other town]"? What happens during lunch, hallway passing, pep rallies, and the informal moments that shape whether students feel they belong — especially for a student bused 45 minutes to a school where everyone else already knows each other? [Audience Advocate, Empath]

5. **Equity access: extracurriculars and participation barriers.** For students whose families can't drive to 6 a.m. band practice or late sports pickups, or who can't afford participation fees that might increase, what opportunities quietly disappear? Who gets to be a "full participant" in school life when transportation shifts, and when school board meetings move 30 miles away and parent-teacher conferences require a half-day off? [Audience Advocate]

6. **Rural-reality participation barriers.** For families without reliable internet, who live on unpaved roads that snowplows treat as low priority, or whose parents work jobs that don't allow midday transportation flexibility, what aspects of consolidation might quietly exclude them from full participation in school life? [Audience Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 09: Protections for Vulnerable Students and Families

1. **Special education continuity and IEP delivery.** When individualized education programs were developed in small districts with specific resources and relationships, how does consolidation affect delivery of those services, the relationships between families and special education staff, and the continuity of accommodations students rely on? [Audience Advocate]

2. **Special needs transportation.** For students with physical disabilities, medical conditions, or sensory processing needs, how would longer bus rides affect their health, comfort, and ability to learn? What would the ride actually feel like for a student who uses a wheelchair, has anxiety about long rides, or needs to access medication during the day? [Audience Advocate]

3. **Language and cultural accessibility.** In districts with varying proportions of English language learner families, how would consolidation affect translation services, cultural liaison staff, and the ability of non-English-speaking parents to advocate for their children's needs? [Audience Advocate]

4. **Working families, childcare, and the daily infrastructure of life.** For parents who rely on older siblings to watch younger ones after school, or who depend on schools for before/after care, how would consolidation's transportation shifts and schedule changes affect their ability to hold jobs, manage childcare, or get through the week? [Audience Advocate]

5. **Parent trust, access, and distant administration.** When the school administration shifts from someone you might see at the grocery store to a centralized office 45 minutes away, how would that distance affect parents' willingness to advocate, report concerns, or feel heard — and how do parents experience the loss of proximity to their children's daily lives when they can no longer drop in for a game, watch practice, or see their child's teacher in the community? [Audience Advocate, Empath]

6. **Student voice in the process.** If we asked students themselves what matters most about their school experience, what would they say that adults rarely consider? How might their priorities differ from the financial and logistical factors dominating consolidation discussions — and who else are we not hearing from (recent graduates, young families considering a move, elderly voters without direct connection to schools)? [Audience Advocate, Questioner]

## Topic Cluster 10: Emotional Landscape, Grief, and Community Identity

1. **What actually dies when a school closes.** Beyond the building itself, what rituals, gatherings, and informal social glue disappear — and what emotional void does that leave for a town that's already watching its population dwindle? For communities where the school is the only remaining institution that says "we exist as a town," what does taking that away feel like, and how do we acknowledge that grief without treating it as an obstacle to be overcome? [Empath, Audience Advocate]

2. **Naming what people are afraid to lose.** The consolidation conversation keeps framing this as "gaining efficiency" and "creating opportunities" — but what if we started by naming what people are actually afraid to lose, then figured out how to carry that forward? How do we talk about losing without making people feel like they've lost? [Empath]

3. **Friday night lights and the hometown marquee.** When a town's pride is tied to its name on a school building — Friday night lights, rivalries, the narrative communities tell about who they are — what happens when that all gets folded into someone else's identity? What does it feel like to drive past an empty building, attend games in someone else's town, and explain to new residents where "your" school went? [Empath, Audience Advocate]

4. **Winners and losers in the emotional math.** If one town keeps the high school and another doesn't, how does that emotional math get calculated across decades of local history, and what does it do to the idea of "one unified district"? What feels like betrayal and what feels like collaboration — and who gets to decide? [Empath]

5. **What kids internalize when their school closes.** When the consolidation narrative is "your district is failing," how does that internalize for an 8-year-old or a 16-year-old who hears "my town couldn't figure it out"? And for an eighth grader already being told their small school is closing, what emotional baggage are they carrying? [Empath]

6. **The dollar figure of resentment.** The financial analysis tallies administrative savings and per-pupil funding gains — but what about the emotional tax of living in a town that feels it sacrificed its school for someone else's fiscal stability? Has anyone put a dollar figure on what resentment costs a community? [Empath]

7. **Long-standing rivalries and social fabric.** What happens to the social fabric when students from long-standing rival communities are combined? Sports rivalries, historical tensions, and community identities run deep in rural areas — what proactive school-culture initiatives acknowledge and respect community identities while building new shared traditions? [Devil's Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 11: Systems Dynamics, Feedback Loops, and Long-Horizon Effects

1. **Self-amplifying patterns and tipping points.** If consolidation improves educational quality through better resources, it could attract families — increasing enrollment, funding, and quality in a virtuous cycle. But the reverse loop may dominate: longer bus rides and loss of community identity accelerate out-migration of young families, worsening enrollment decline, triggering more cuts and more flight. Which loop is stronger under what conditions, and what determines the tipping point where virtuous cycles flip vicious? [Systems Thinker]

2. **Delay effects and intergenerational accountability.** Financial benefits appear immediately (administrative savings, state funding increases), but social costs (community disintegration, student alienation from longer commutes, loss of "third place" community hubs) compound gradually over 5-10 years. By the time these delayed consequences become visible, the decision-makers who approved consolidation are gone, and the causal link is forgotten. What early warning indicators would reveal whether the system is trending toward resilience or decay before the delayed effects become irreversible? [Systems Thinker]

3. **Shifting the Burden dynamics.** Consolidation offers a short-term fix for budget deficits by eliminating administrative overhead, but if the underlying driver is enrollment decline, the relief is temporary. As enrollment continues falling, the consolidated district faces the same structural deficits — only now with less local control, fewer "trimmable" administrative positions, and a more cumbersome bureaucracy. The temporary fix erodes the capacity to address the root cause. What symptoms would indicate the district is relying on consolidation benefits rather than building genuine resilience? [Systems Thinker]

4. **Success to the Successful between towns.** If one town keeps its school and another loses theirs, the town with the school becomes more attractive to families, which increases property values and tax base, which funds better facilities, which attracts more families. Meanwhile, the town without its school enters a death spiral: families leave, property values fall, businesses close, tax revenue erodes. The gap compounds over time. How would resource-allocation decisions either mitigate or accelerate this divergence, and what policies ensure "losers" in the closure decision don't become permanent losers? [Systems Thinker]

5. **Eroding Goals: silent standard creep.** If consolidation is sold as "maintaining quality while cutting costs," but post-consolidation reality reveals persistent budget pressure despite administrative savings, the district may gradually redefine "acceptable" — first by freezing programs, then expanding class sizes, then reducing course offerings. Each step seems reasonable given constraints, but over five years, community expectations quietly erode. What guardrails would prevent this silent standard creep? [Systems Thinker]

6. **Non-obvious leverage points short of full consolidation.** The debate frames consolidation as binary: stay separate or merge completely. But leverage points often produce disproportionate effects from small interventions. Could shared services agreements for transportation, special education, or professional development capture 60-70% of administrative savings without triggering community identity loss? Could a "soft consolidation" — unified purchasing, shared superintendent, joint curriculum development — build trust and demonstrate benefits before any structural merger? [Systems Thinker, Questioner, Devil's Advocate]

7. **School closures rippling through local economies.** When a town loses its school, it loses more than education — it loses the venue for community meetings, youth sports, voting, town events. Local businesses lose daily foot traffic. Property values decline, reducing tax revenue for town services. The town's perceived viability as a place to live and work deteriorates. What economic and social impact assessment would capture these second- and third-order consequences? [Systems Thinker, Devil's Advocate]

8. **State policy as hidden constraint or enabler.** The state funding formula penalizes small districts, creating a structural incentive to consolidate. But what other state policies — transportation reimbursement ratios, minimum school size requirements, curriculum mandates, teacher certification rules — shape what's possible? Often, the highest-leverage intervention is changing the rules at the next system level rather than optimizing within current constraints. How do emerging state consolidation incentives and funding-formula shifts likely to accelerate over 3-5 years affect this calculus? [Systems Thinker, Futurist]

## Topic Cluster 12: Futures, Scenarios, and Reimagination

1. **Scenario planning for continued and accelerated decline.** If the consolidated district loses another 15% of students by 2035 — or if rural areas experience non-linear drops from a major employer closure or demographic shift — what contingency scenarios exist for 25%, 35%, 50% declines? What "tripwire" indicators should trigger another restructure, and how does the district avoid being in this same position a decade from now? What financial planning horizon (5-year, 10-year, worst-case) should the consolidated district use? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Accountant]

2. **Failure modes and early warning indicators.** If consolidation fails financially, politically, or educationally, what are the specific failure scenarios (consolidated district insolvency in year 4, voter rejection, mass teacher departures) and what early warning indicators would signal we're on those trajectories? [Analyst]

3. **Remote work and reverse-migration sensitivity.** The pandemic accelerated documented migration from cities to rural areas as remote work became normalized. If 2035 projections assume continued decline based on pre-2020 trends, what sensitivity analysis accounts for potential remote-work-driven in-migration that could stabilize or reverse enrollment losses, and how might rural economic diversification (tourism, niche manufacturing, remote work) reshape enrollment? [Futurist]

4. **Preserving option value for hybrid and community-school models.** The documented growth of hybrid learning models, micro-schools, and full-service community schools (co-locating health services, adult education, community programming) suggests physical facilities may need to serve multiple functions. How might building closure and repurposing decisions preserve option value for flexible scheduling, community learning hubs, shared regional programs, or multi-use capacity? [Futurist]

5. **What if enrollment actually increases after consolidation?** Demographic projections assume continued decline, but what if economic and educational improvements from consolidation make the county more attractive to families? Does the consolidated model have capacity and flexibility to grow, or will success create new problems? [Questioner]

6. **Hidden curriculum of longer commutes over generations.** Longer bus rides mean less time for after-school activities, part-time jobs, family responsibilities, unstructured play. They shift daily rhythms, change who students interact with, and shift the boundary between school and community life. These effects shape student development, social capital formation, and relationship to place in ways that don't show up in academic metrics but matter profoundly for community vitality over generations. What observational indicators would reveal whether transportation decisions are strengthening or eroding the social fabric? [Systems Thinker]

7. **Alternative models beyond full consolidation.** What alternatives — shared services agreements (joint purchasing, shared special education staff, cooperative transportation), partial consolidation (administrative merger while retaining local schools and boards), phased approaches, pilot consolidation of specific functions, or regional high school districts — might achieve similar benefits without full consolidation? Should a phased approach let two districts consolidate first, demonstrate benefits, and invite the third after seeing results? [Devil's Advocate, Questioner]

8. **Hidden assets in declining enrollment.** Declining enrollment creates genuine challenges, but what unexpected strengths or opportunities has it created or revealed — smaller class sizes enabling innovation, stronger parent-teacher relationships, more nimble decision-making? What would we want to make sure doesn't get lost in the transition to a larger system? [Appreciative Inquirer]

9. **Peak moments of district collaboration.** Tell me about a time in the past decade when two or more of these districts worked together on something that succeeded beyond anyone's expectations. What conditions made that collaboration possible, what relationships were already in place, and how could those relationships become the foundation for consolidation rather than casualties of it? [Appreciative Inquirer]

10. **The school experience we want children to remember.** Imagine it's ten years from now and a child who started kindergarten in a newly consolidated district is graduating. What memories would make them say "I'm so glad I went through schools in this county"? What would they describe about their relationships, learning, connection to community — and what structures would need to be in place from day one to make that experience reliable? [Appreciative Inquirer]

11. **The coalition that already exists, and experiments that build confidence.** For consolidation to pass, it needs champions in all three districts. Who are the natural allies who already see the potential — parents who've experienced successful regional programs, teachers who've collaborated across districts, business leaders, students who've participated in cross-district activities? And before asking voters to decide on full consolidation, what smaller-scale experiments (shared superintendent, combined professional development days, joint extracurricular programs, shared specialty courses) could demonstrate that collaboration works and create momentum? [Appreciative Inquirer]

## Appended Questions (Perspective, Provocation, and Distinctive-Register Personas)

### Provocateur (Append)

1. **What if "school districts" were the wrong unit of analysis entirely?** Imagine if education funding, governance, and enrollment were organized around something completely different — students traveling to learning rather than schools receiving them, teachers as independent contractors serving multiple micro-communities, or education organized around "learning seasons" rather than geographic boundaries. What would become possible if we stopped assuming consolidated districts are the only alternative to fragmented ones? *The insight: we're debating the architecture of a system whose fundamental unit may be obsolete.*

2. **What if the goal was to make consolidation fail spectacularly?** Imagine designing the consolidation process specifically to maximize community outrage, financial waste, and educational disruption. What would you do? Close all buildings simultaneously? Fire everyone and rehire? Auction off assets to private buyers? Now work backward: what does this reveal about which safeguards, pacing decisions, and transition structures actually matter most? *The insight: sometimes disaster planning reveals what success requires more clearly than optimization does.*

3. **What if every student attended a different "school" every week?** Imagine a system where students rotated through all 7 buildings on a rolling basis, where no town had a "local school" but everyone had access to every facility. Or imagine schools were organized around student interests rather than geography — the STEM building, the arts building, the nature school — and transportation became the primary cost instead of duplication. What would this reveal about what "community school" actually means versus what we assume it means? *The insight: we're protecting a concept of local schooling that may already be fiction for rural students facing long commutes anyway.*

4. **What if the districts deliberately made themselves LESS efficient before consolidating?** Imagine if each district, knowing consolidation was coming, accelerated hiring, raised salaries, took on debt, and expanded programs specifically to maximize the "savings" that consolidation would appear to generate. What if consolidation was structured as a rescue rather than a merger? How would the politics and narrative differ if the new district "saved" failing districts rather than "rationalizing" functional ones? *The insight: we're pretending consolidation is about efficiency when it's really about solvency — what if we named that directly?*

5. **What if declining enrollment was framed as an educational asset rather than a crisis?** Imagine a world where smaller student-teacher ratios, individualized attention, and underutilized facilities were positioned as luxury advantages rather than inefficiencies. What if the districts competed to attract students FROM each other using quality and experience rather than fighting against consolidation? What if the problem wasn't "not enough students" but "too much infrastructure designed for a different era"? *The insight: enrollment decline is only a failure if metrics designed for growth are applied to shrinkage — what if we designed for contraction instead?*

6. **What if we planned for the district to be dissolved in 20 years?** Imagine designing the consolidation with an explicit expiration date — create the unified district with the assumption that it will need to radically restructure or cease to exist in 2035. How would facility decisions, contract negotiations, transportation planning, and community engagement change if this were a transition state rather than a permanent solution? What interim structures would you build differently if you knew they were temporary? *The insight: treating consolidation as "forever" locks in decisions that should remain adaptable as conditions continue changing.*

7. **What if consolidation required every student to change schools, not just some?** Rather than closing buildings and displacing students from closed schools, imagine requiring ALL 1,800 students to reassess and re-enroll under a new system with no guaranteed placement. What if "our school is our town" was disrupted for everyone rather than protected for a few? How would the political dynamics and coalition-building change if no community received special protection? *The insight: we're trying to minimize disruption when sometimes equitable disruption creates different political possibilities.*

### Storyteller (Append)

1. **The Last Day at Maple Grove Elementary** Imagine walking through the halls of Maple Grove Elementary on what might be its final day of operation as a neighborhood school. The walls display photos of seven generations of students who learned to read in these classrooms. The principal's grandmother attended this school, as did her mother, and now her own daughter faces the prospect of being bused 45 minutes away. How might a community ritually honor and preserve 90 years of collective memory when a building stops being a school, and what transitional objects or ceremonies could help families carry their school's identity forward rather than experiencing it as an amputation?
