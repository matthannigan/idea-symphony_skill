# Brainstorming Questions: Mobile App Simplification

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Defining Core Value and Differentiator

1. **What "core value" actually means**: What does "core value" mean in practice for this app — features most people use, features that create the most passionate engagement, the "job" users hired us to do when they downloaded, or the true differentiator that no other fitness app offers regardless of current usage data? Have we asked users directly what problem they thought they were solving? [Questioner]
2. **Unified core-value assumption challenged**: What if no unified "core value" exists — what if retention is low not because the app is unfocused but because no single feature is good enough to compete with dedicated alternatives, meaning simplification could leave us with nothing compelling? And are we even in the right category — what if our actual value lies in an adjacent space we've accidentally stumbled into? [Devil's Advocate, Questioner]
3. **The "One Thing" evolution (strengths-framed)**: If this app became known for doing one thing exceptionally well — the thing that makes users think "I can't live without this" — what would that be, how would that single focus shape onboarding, daily use, and word-of-mouth, and what would users accomplish or feel here that they couldn't elsewhere? [Appreciative Inquirer]

## Topic Cluster 02: Retention Signals, Metrics, and Measurement

1. **Decomposing retention and session metrics**: Breaking down the 8% 30-day retention and 2% DAU/MAU ratio with cohort analysis and behavioral sequence analysis, which features show the steepest drop-off points in the onboarding-to-habit pipeline, and are users failing to find what they need, completing tasks too quickly (low depth), or bouncing due to decision paralysis — with results segmented by user type? [Analyst]
2. **Feature popularity vs. retention-driving feature value**: The 8% retention rate makes usage data potentially misleading — features used early by churning users might appear "popular" while actually being on-ramps to nowhere. How do we distinguish features that drive retention from those that merely attract initial engagement before users quit, and how do we map time-to-value so features needing weeks of habit formation aren't killed prematurely? [Devil's Advocate, Analyst]
3. **Stated vs. revealed preferences**: If we analyzed which features users actually engage with versus which they say they value, where would we see mismatches between stated preferences and revealed behaviors, and how do we design an experimental approach to distinguish features users claim to want from features they actually use? [Audience Advocate, Analyst]
4. **Leading indicators for the simplified product (metric-system realignment)**: What leading micro-indicators should replace lagging metrics (downloads, 30-day retention) to predict daily habit formation in the simplified product — and what is the risk of measuring a focused utility with the old "engagement-maximizing" rulers, where a utility that solves a problem quickly might have SHORTER session lengths but HIGHER long-term retention? [Analyst, Systems Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 03: Feature Interdependencies and Removal Dynamics

1. **Feature interdependency mapping and keystone features**: Before cutting any feature, what is the complete causal chain of network effects — which feature pairs or clusters create synergistic value that would be lost if eliminated, which are creating drag through complexity/notification fatigue/cognitive overload, and which are "keystone features" whose removal would cause disproportionate system collapse (data sharing, on-ramps, recommendation power)? [Systems Thinker, Analyst]
2. **Cascading user loss and death-spiral thresholds**: When you remove features with passionate but small user bases, what are the second- and third-order consequences — do departing users take their friends, user-generated content, and community moderation with them, at what threshold does feature-cutting trigger a death spiral, and what is the maximum acceptable user loss we can sustain with contingency plans if simplification backfires? [Systems Thinker, Devil's Advocate]
3. **Technical debt from removal vs. retention**: What is the unit economics of feature removal (engineering cost, cognitive-load reduction, performance gains) weighed against churn risk, and could removing deeply integrated features actually create more bugs, complexity, and hidden database-migration work than maintaining them — potentially consuming the entire 8-month runway? Have we mapped the dependency graph? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Questioner]
4. **User adaptation and compensatory behavior**: How will existing users adapt to feature removal, and what unintended consequences emerge — will users flood the challenge platform with social posts if you cut the feed, or clutter community forums with transaction requests if you eliminate the marketplace, undermining the simplification you were trying to achieve? [Systems Thinker]
5. **Cannibalization and feature cannibalization**: Are features cannibalizing engagement from each other (e.g., does the workout tracker reduce usage of coaching?), and how would we measure whether simplification actually increases total engagement rather than reducing it? [Analyst]

## Topic Cluster 04: Approach — Relaunch vs. Incremental, Test Before Cut

1. **Incremental vs. clean-slate methodology**: How should we structure the decision between incremental simplification and clean relaunch — what's the analytical framework comparing risks of abrupt-change alienation against gradualism's opportunity costs, and why do we assume a clean relaunch is better rather than removing one feature per month and measuring impact each time? [Analyst, Questioner]
2. **Testing removal before committing**: Could we ship a version with one feature temporarily disabled to see who complains or doesn't notice, and could we validate through small low-risk experiments this month — engaging users as participants in the product's evolution rather than subjects — before irreversibly removing features that might be working for some? [Questioner, Appreciative Inquirer, Devil's Advocate]
3. **Relaunch assumption and runway realism**: The "clean relaunch" assumes users will give us a second chance, but app-store graveyards are full of "2.0" failures; and 8 months may become a false constraint if removal uncovers hidden dependencies, database migrations, or user backlash — what evidence suggests our existing base will return (vs. acquiring net-new users), and what does a realistic timeline with buffer for unknown unknowns look like? [Devil's Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 05: Is Simplification Even the Right Diagnosis?

1. **"Too much" vs. "poorly designed"**: How do we distinguish between "overwhelming because too much" and "overwhelming because poorly designed" — when users say the app is "overwhelming," are they referring to feature count, cognitive load, decision paralysis, discoverability, or something else, and how do we diagnose the real problem without asking? What if simplification means better UX design rather than fewer features, and users would actually want these features if they could find and understand them? [Questioner, Audience Advocate]
2. **The "simplify into nothing" trap**: If users are already frustrated with "overwhelming" complexity, what if the true issue isn't feature count but poor information architecture or onboarding — how can we validate that simplification will actually improve retention before we irreversibly remove features that might be working for some users? [Devil's Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 06: User Experience, Equity, and Agency

1. **First-30-seconds and journey mapping**: What does the first 30 seconds feel like for a new user who downloaded expecting a fitness tracker but encounters 15 features (cognitive load, excitement-to-overwhelm emotional arc), and if we mapped the actual journeys of the 8% who stayed past 30 days, what paths did they take and what emotional rewards kept them coming back? [Audience Advocate]
2. **Intent vs. distraction friction**: What do users experience when they open the app for a specific purpose (tracking a workout) but get distracted by notifications, social feeds, and marketplace suggestions — how often do they successfully complete the task they came for versus abandon because the app pulled them in too many directions, and what notification philosophy would align with respecting users' attention rather than demanding it? [Audience Advocate]
3. **Accessibility, archetypes, and who gets left behind**: How does the current 6-tab-plus-hamburger navigation differ for users with varying digital literacy, visual impairments, or cognitive processing differences; which user archetypes (social-butterfly challenger vs. privacy-focused tracker vs. stressed/time-poor user) are served or underserved today; and if we cut to 3-4 features, who are we leaving behind and is that acceptable? [Audience Advocate]
4. **Power dynamics and user voice in the cut decision**: What power dynamics exist when a product team decides which features matter, and how can we involve users in determining the app's core value proposition rather than making top-down assumptions — what mechanisms (surveys, interviews, beta testing, advisory boards) ensure marginalized and everyday user voices shape this decision rather than just the 2% power users? [Audience Advocate]
5. **Self-selection bias in user research**: Users who respond to surveys or agree to interviews are the most engaged minority, potentially leading us to overoptimize for power users while ignoring the 92% who leave within 30 days — what research methods help us understand why the majority abandon the app without alienating the only users who still care, and with only one PM who determines which feedback matters? [Devil's Advocate]
6. **Unarticulated needs and normalized problems**: What unarticulated needs are users currently working around because they've never experienced an alternative — fundamental human needs we're failing to meet that users might not realize could be addressed differently because "all fitness apps are like this"? [Audience Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 07: Transition, Passionate Minorities, and Communication

1. **Passionate minority valuation and graceful migration**: For features with small but passionate user bases (e.g., <5% of users but 80% engagement in that segment), what is the analytical framework for deciding whether they represent a defensible niche worth preserving, a distraction to cut, or an opportunity to spin off — and how do we honor their commitment through a graceful transition that turns potential churn into deeper engagement, identifying these users before we cut? [Analyst, Appreciative Inquirer, Questioner]
2. **Feature-removal communication and honoring investment**: How will existing users who have built routines around specific features experience their removal — what communication approaches (timeline, transparency, support) honor their investment, and could we frame the simplification as "we're focusing on what matters most" rather than "we're taking things away," preserving trust and preventing users from feeling abandoned? [Audience Advocate, Questioner]
3. **Communication-fragmentation feedback loop**: The simplification message itself creates a system — how announcements are made, how early adopters react, how sentiment spreads through review channels and social media, how that sentiment influences fence-sitters, and how resulting retention numbers inform future product decisions. What are the leverage points where small messaging changes could dramatically shift user perception? [Systems Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 08: Team, Decision-Making, and Governance

1. **False consensus and one-PM decision bias**: A 6-engineer team likely has 6 different opinions about which features constitute the "core," each person may unconsciously champion what they built, and with only one PM determining which feedback matters and what "core value" means, what decision framework and guardrails could objectively identify core value without letting ownership bias, team politics, or one person's limited perspective drive the outcome? [Devil's Advocate]
2. **Team morale, pride, and post-"killing-darlings" psychological investment**: If engineers have spent months or years building features now being deleted, how do we help them stay motivated to execute the relaunch — feeling proud of simplification rather than like they "failed" — especially when "core" features require working on code they didn't originally write, and what mechanisms rebuild psychological investment? [Devil's Advocate, Questioner]
3. **Team reimagined and aligned product vision (strengths-based)**: This team of six engineers, one designer, and one PM has clearly been through a lot — recall a time when the team felt most energized, proud, or effective. What conditions made that moment possible, what strengths does each member bring that could be amplified in a leaner environment, and what kind of simplified product would allow them to do their best work, making simplification feel like liberation rather than loss? [Appreciative Inquirer]
4. **Team capacity-reinforcement dynamics and simplification-creep vigilance**: When you eliminate features, do you redeploy engineers to deepen remaining features (potentially creating new bloat) or reduce headcount (undermining iteration capacity)? What system are you creating with team-structure decisions, and what early-warning systems or structural constraints (e.g., a "zero-sum feature budget" where adding anything requires removing something — a balancing loop maintaining simplicity) could naturally resist feature creep over time? [Systems Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 09: Peak Moments, Existing Strengths, and Forward Vision

1. **Peak user experiences and features that sparked joy**: Tell me about a moment when a user genuinely loved this app — a session that went beyond 47 seconds, or a time someone told a friend. What were they doing, what problem did the app solve so well that they felt grateful, and among the 15 features, which elicited the most positive feedback, sustained engagement, or strongest loyalty — and what made it resonate (utility, experience, social connection)? [Appreciative Inquirer]
2. **Hidden assets in the user base**: With 180K downloads, even at low retention, there are users who found value — who are they, what specific use case or demographic found this app worthwhile, and what might we learn from the 2% who show up daily about what keeps them coming back? [Appreciative Inquirer]
3. **The app users champion (3-year vision)**: Imagine it's three years from now and this app has become something users genuinely champion — not just use, but actively recommend. What would users say about it, what specific feeling or outcome would they describe, and how would it fit into daily routine in a way that feels indispensable rather than overwhelming? [Appreciative Inquirer]
4. **Amplifying what works and building from existing assets**: Based on features that already show promise, what structures, practices, or product principles would reliably amplify those strengths, and what resources already in place (technical infrastructure, user insights, team skills, community relationships, learnings from the current feature set) could accelerate the move toward a focused product — making the MVP reset feel like an evolution rather than a restart? [Appreciative Inquirer]

## Appended Questions (Perspective, Provocation, and Distinctive-Register Personas)

### Provocateur (Append)

1. **What if you kept exactly ONE feature and made it absurdly worse?** Imagine you stripped the app down to just workout tracking, but then removed all logging, history, progress visualization, and data—leaving only the immediate act of pressing a button that says "I worked out" before the app closes itself. What would this useless button reveal about what users actually crave from the tracking experience? *The real insight: is the value in the data or in the ritual of acknowledgment?*
2. **What if you launched a "competitor" that was intentionally terrible?** Create a clone app with all 15 features, but each one is broken in a specific, maddening way—workout tracking only logs every third workout, meal planning displays recipes you can't actually cook, social feed shows posts from people who don't exist. Market it aggressively to your existing users. What does watching them struggle with the anti-version reveal about which brokenness they'll tolerate and which sends them away forever? *The real insight: what if you could discover core value by studying which imperfections function as dealbreakers?*
3. **What if the 2% who actually use the app daily are the problem, not the solution?** Imagine your power users are actually tumors—pathological attachments to features that represent the app's worst impulses, and their retention is preventing the product from evolving into something healthy for normal humans. What would happen if you actively tried to drive away your most engaged users? *The real insight: what if your retention metrics are optimized for a tiny minority of freak users while everyone else is correctly recognizing that the product doesn't serve them?*

### Storyteller (Append)

1. **What would the "first five minutes" experience look like for a returning user who opens the simplified app for the first time, and what emotional state should we be designing toward?** Imagine it's Tuesday morning and Sarah, who previously faced 15 features and 6 navigation tabs, opens the new version. Walk through what she sees, what she feels, and what she can accomplish in those critical first minutes. How do we ensure the transition from confusion to clarity happens within a single session? What does "success" look like in that first interaction—should she feel relief, excitement, curiosity, or something else entirely?
2. **How might the engineering team's daily work life transform when they shift from maintaining 15 half-built features to perfecting 3-4 world-class experiences, and what "micro-moments" of professional satisfaction will replace the current burnout cycle?** Walk us through the standup meeting six months from now. What language are engineers using to describe their work? How do conversations about quality, testing, and user feedback change when they're not spread thin across a feature graveyard? What moments of pride—seeing a user review, watching usage metrics, shipping a polished experience—replace the current reality of constant bug fixes and technical debt management? How does the team's relationship with the codebase itself evolve when they're not afraid to touch it?
3. **How might the press release, app store description, and launch announcement tell a story that frames simplification as innovation rather than retreat, and what metaphors or analogies would help users and investors see this as a bold step forward?** Consider the difference between "we're removing features because we couldn't make them work" versus "we've discovered what users actually need, and we're cutting away everything else." What story positions this as the company's bravest decision rather than its most desperate? How do we communicate to new users that this simplified version isn't a downgrade, but rather the first version of the product that actually works? What cultural references or parallels—from other companies, from product design history, from everyday life—could make this pivot feel obvious and inevitable in retrospect?

### Visionary (Append)

1. **[What if fitness wasn't something you track, but something that emerges naturally through a fundamentally different relationship with your body?]** Instead of asking which features to keep or cut, what if we completely reimagined the category: What would a fitness product look like if it were designed around joy, emergence, and organic behavior change rather than metrics, tracking, and discipline? What if the core value isn't "helping people work out" but something entirely different that doesn't have a current analog in the fitness app space?
2. **[What if the core value isn't any single feature, but the emotional state users hope to achieve through using the product — and what if we could reverse-engineer from that destination rather than from existing features?]** Rather than asking "what features do people use most," what if we asked "what emotional transformation do people seek when they download this app" and built backward from there? What if the product that delivers that transformation doesn't look like a fitness app at all, but resembles something from a completely different domain (therapy, coaching, entertainment, community)?
3. **[What if instead of choosing 3-4 core features, we created a product with no features at all — just a single, coherent behavior that users engage in repeatedly?]** Imagine a fitness app with no tracking, no planning, no social features, no content — just one elegant, repeatable action that somehow produces fitness outcomes. What would that one action be? What if the app became a "one-button" product where pressing that button initiates a cascade of value without requiring navigation, configuration, or decisions?

### Connector (Append)

1. **Feature pruning as ecosystem succession** The challenge of cutting features mirrors ecological succession, where crowded ecosystems self-thin to let dominant species thrive. In forest ecology, "self-thinning" creates space for the strongest organisms to flourish. What if we viewed the 15 features as competing species in an ecosystem — how would we identify which features are the "keystone species" whose presence enables the entire ecosystem to function, and which are the invasive species crowding out the core value?
2. **The IKEA effect paradox in user attachment** There's a structural parallel between users attached to niche features and the psychological phenomenon known as the IKEA effect — people overvalue things they've invested effort in building or using. But behavioral economics also shows the "endowment effect" creates irrational attachment to things simply because we own them. How do we distinguish between genuine value-creating features and features users are irrationally attached to due to sunk-cost fallacy — and could we borrow techniques from "loss aversion" research to help users let go without feeling betrayed?
3. **UI complexity as information entropy** Information theory uses entropy to measure uncertainty and complexity in communication systems. The current app's 6 navigation tabs plus hamburger menu represents high interface entropy — users must process too much information before finding value. Claude Shannon's work showed that reducing noise increases signal transmission. If we treated every UI element as adding "noise" to the user's cognitive load, what entropy-reduction principles from information theory could guide which elements are signal (essential) versus noise (eliminable)?
