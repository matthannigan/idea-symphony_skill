# Brainstorming Questions: School Consolidation

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Foundational Framing — What Problem Are We Actually Solving?

1. **Is "consolidating districts" the right unit of analysis at all?** At its most fundamental level, what are the essential educational services these 1,800 students actually need, and is merging three districts into one the only — or even the best — way to deliver them, given alternatives like shared-services agreements, cooperative purchasing, virtual consolidation, a "lead district" administrative model, or a confederation model where local boards retain authority on some decisions while coordinating regionally on others? If we were building rural county education from scratch today without any legacy assumption of district boundaries, what would the optimal structure look like — and could we redirect the political energy toward reforming the state funding formula that penalizes small districts rather than restructuring around a disadvantage we assume is fixed? [First Principles Thinker, Questioner]

2. **Is the core problem structural, demographic, or a deeper category error?** Is the fundamental issue that three small districts are structured incorrectly, or that rural education is inherently incompatible with declining enrollment and fixed infrastructure costs? If consolidation solves the immediate fiscal crisis, what root driver remains unaddressed — and does the proposal merely postpone the reckoning to a larger scale while baking in an irreducible conflict between efficiency (fewer administrators, shared services, optimized transportation) and accessibility (longer bus rides, distant decision-makers, less local accountability) that no amount of optimization can resolve? [First Principles Thinker, Devil's Advocate]

3. **What costs, assumptions, and voices are we not counting?** Beyond the $1.2M administrative savings and 12% per-pupil funding increase, what hidden costs — legal fees for merger implementation, technology/curriculum integration, potentially higher union-negotiated wages, "quality bleed" that accelerates enrollment loss — are systematically under-counted; who are we not hearing from (students themselves, recent graduates, young families deciding whether to move, the elderly property-tax voters without direct school connection); and is voter rejection of consolidation perhaps not a communication failure at all but a signal that the fundamental value proposition is inadequate? [Questioner, First Principles Thinker, Audience Advocate]

4. **What already makes these districts special, and what peak moments of collaboration could be the seed of consolidation?** When you walk into any of these seven schools on a day when everything is working beautifully — students engaged, parents proud, genuine joy in the building — what specifically is happening, and when have two or more of these districts worked together on something that succeeded beyond anyone's expectations? What relationships were already in place that could become the foundation for consolidation rather than casualties of it? [Appreciative Inquirer, AI orphan register]

## Topic Cluster 02: Fiscal Reality — Break-Even, Unit Economics, and Hidden Liabilities

1. **What is the true break-even analysis, and can the smallest district survive the transition period?** Beyond the $1.2M annual administrative savings, what are the one-time transition costs — legal fees for dissolving three districts, HR/technology/systems integration, union contract buyouts ($500K–$1.5M one-time, $200K–$400K annual), facility closure expenses (asbestos abatement, environmental remediation), early-retirement incentives — and how many years would it take to recover them? If transition costs run $3–5M, consolidation operates at a net loss for 3–4 years before realizing savings; can the smallest district with only a 2-year fund balance absorb that gap, and what forensic accounting of insolvent-district debt, underfunded pensions, and deferred-maintenance liabilities must precede any merger decision so those burdens don't consume the anticipated savings? [Analyst, Accountant, Devil's Advocate]

2. **Where is the "floor" for viability as enrollment decline continues?** If enrollment declines another 15% by 2035 (roughly 270 students, or $2.4M in state funding at current per-pupil rates), at what student count does the consolidated district itself become financially unsustainable — and does the $1.2M administrative savings leave it still running a structural deficit within 5–7 years under 25%, 35%, or 50% worst-case decline scenarios? What "revenue shock absorbers" (stabilization fund, multi-year union agreements with flexibility clauses, county-wide sales-tax earmarks, state consolidation grants, community-college partnerships for shared facility use, leasing unused space) and tripwire indicators should be built in from day one? [Analyst, Accountant, Devil's Advocate]

3. **How does debt pooling, bond refinancing, and property-tax equalization actually work across three districts?** If District A has $8M in outstanding bonds while District B is debt-free, do obligations pool proportionally or do pre-consolidation voters remain responsible? Are there bond-refinancing opportunities given a larger, more creditworthy tax base? And with rural districts in one county often having dramatically different tax bases ($50M/student assessed value vs. $120M/student) across agricultural vs. residential vs. commercial assessments, what is the actual tax-rate impact on homeowners in each district, at what point does tax resistance from previously "low-tax" towns threaten voter approval, and should the plan include phased equalization or revenue-neutral transition periods? [Analyst, Accountant]

4. **Is transition money better spent on cost-cutting or strategic capital investment?** The $4.2M deferred-maintenance backlog signals a capital-starved system. What if the $3–5M in one-time transition costs were structured as investment — energy-efficiency retrofits reducing operating costs 15–20%, or consolidation into modernized buildings with lower long-term maintenance? Could the consolidated district grow into sustainability through strategic 5–10-year-payback capital deployment rather than cut its way there? [Accountant]

## Topic Cluster 03: Facilities — Lifecycle, Closure, and Reuse

1. **What is the full lifecycle and NPV picture for the 7-building portfolio — and are we treating this as an efficiency question when it's really a community-anchor question?** Rather than looking only at $4.2M deferred maintenance, what are capital replacement schedules for each building (end-of-life in 5, 10, 20 years), and if the district closes 2–3 buildings, what are avoided future costs vs. one-time closure costs (asbestos abatement, demolition), NPV of fewer but better-maintained facilities vs. aging spread, and realistic sale proceeds in rural markets? At its core, though, this is a question about what role physical school buildings play in rural communities — "which facilities are most efficient" and "which towns deserve to keep their community anchor" are fundamentally different questions, and conflating them may be why consolidation proposals fail. Should deferred-maintenance costs actually justify new construction of one modern, energy-efficient facility as part of consolidation? [Analyst, Accountant, First Principles Thinker, Questioner]

2. **How might the obvious facility fix plant the seeds of its own failure — a Fixes-that-Fail dynamic that could also trigger a Success-to-the-Successful divergence between towns?** Closing the most dilapidated buildings saves maintenance costs immediately but reduces total capacity, so the district can't absorb enrollment if demographics stabilize or reverse — requiring rebuild at far higher cost; alternatively, keeping underutilized buildings open "just in case" creates ongoing drain that prevents investment in remaining schools. Meanwhile, if one town keeps its school and another loses theirs, the town with the school becomes more attractive to families → property values rise → tax base grows → better school funding, while the town without its school enters a death spiral: families leave, property values fall, businesses close, tax revenue erodes. How would resource-allocation decisions mitigate or accelerate this compounding divergence, and what scenarios reveal which closure decision is truly adaptive vs. temporarily convenient? [Systems Thinker]

3. **Closed schools as community assets rather than losses.** When a school building closes, what if the process of deciding which facilities to repurpose became an opportunity to create new community assets — early-childhood centers, adult-education hubs, full-service community schools co-locating health services and adult education, satellite community-college campuses, or revenue-generating partnerships? What existing community strengths, organizations, or needs could repurposed buildings serve, and what process would make those decisions feel like community development rather than extraction? [Appreciative Inquirer, Futurist, AI orphan register]

## Topic Cluster 04: Governance, Representation, and Voter Approval

1. **What is the optimal governance structure for fair representation — and are there non-consolidation alternatives?** If the unified board has 9 members, what allocation formula ensures fair representation across towns of different populations while preventing any single town from being perpetually outvoted, and beyond a unified board, what alternatives (confederation with local boards retaining authority on some decisions, "lead district" models, functional consolidation where specific departments merge independently, phased two-then-three merger) better balance scale economies against local voice — and what governance innovations (weighted board representation, town-level advisory councils with real authority, participatory budgeting, community-school compacts) could make consolidated governance feel MORE locally responsive, not less, while building adaptability for the documented decentralization of rural education delivery (virtual charter schools, state-level online programs, regional service cooperatives)? [Analyst, Questioner, Appreciative Inquirer, Futurist, AI orphan register]

2. **How do we map political feasibility across three simultaneous voter approvals and identify the irreducible minimums for acceptance?** Since all three districts must approve, what is the coalition-building strategy — simultaneous wins in all three, or a structure where each district sees a net benefit outweighing its losses? What specific minimum guarantees (facility commitments, tax-rate caps, board representation) and "red lines" would each stakeholder group (teachers' unions, parents, fiscal conservatives, state officials) require before crossing into opposition — and could the vote become a referendum on unrelated grievances (taxes, state mandates, school performance) even among residents who see the logical case? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

3. **What political backlash could emerge from perceived "loss of local control" in a documented era of declining institutional trust?** Rural communities often view schools as symbols of local autonomy. Consolidation may be framed as state overreach or urban imposition, activating cultural resistance — and trust in institutions, including public schools, has declined measurably over the past decade, particularly in rural communities. What local-messenger and trust-repair strategy responds to this skepticism toward centralization? [Devil's Advocate, Futurist]

## Topic Cluster 05: Labor, Contracts, and Operational Complexity

1. **How do union-contract reconciliation costs — and union leverage — reshape the savings picture, and what power dynamics will not be visible for 5–10 years?** If three separate contracts must be harmonized (different salary schedules, benefits, seniority rules), what is the exposure in quantified ranges ($500K–$1.5M one-time, $200K–$400K annual), and how might the three teachers' unions leverage consolidation to extract better terms — highest salary schedule standardized, most generous benefits applied to all, favorable seniority rules? A larger consolidated union also gains more bargaining power vis-à-vis the district and state, with higher-stakes future labor conflicts. What proactive negotiation framework identifies deal-breakers, trade-offs, and walk-away points, and what morale and attrition effects (teacher departures, resistance to change, skepticism of district leadership) could affect implementation quality? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Questioner, Systems Thinker]

2. **What systems-integration and scale-reversal costs are we underestimating?** Beyond visible costs, what is the realistic analysis of hidden integration — student information systems, financial software, special-education tracking, staff email, credentialing and HR systems — and what scale-up costs (longer bus routes increasing fuel and maintenance, expanded IT support, salary leveling upward, increased central-office overhead) might actually reverse economies of scale, where larger becomes administratively more expensive rather than less? [Analyst, Accountant]

3. **Why do we assume consolidation will improve teacher recruitment — and what teacher experience would actually attract talent?** What if a larger district offers less individualized support and more bureaucracy that actually deters some teachers? What evidence shows consolidated districts perform better at attracting qualified staff to rural areas, what positioning prepares the district for the documented decline in rural teacher pipelines as education graduates increasingly urbanize, and imagine three years from now that this county has become known as a place where teachers WANT to work and build careers — what would be different about their daily experience, professional community, autonomy, and resources, and how might consolidation actually create those conditions? [Questioner, Futurist, Appreciative Inquirer, AI orphan register]

## Topic Cluster 06: Transportation, Student Access, and Daily Experience

1. **What is the transportation optimization problem — and could consolidation paradoxically increase per-pupil transportation costs while altering the hidden curriculum of student experience?** With three districts becoming one larger geographic area, what is the rigorous route-efficiency analysis — maximum acceptable bus ride time, the point where transportation costs eat into administrative savings, rural road conditions, winter weather, fuel, maintenance, driver wages? Could longer routes actually increase total transportation expense, and beyond cost, how would a shift from a 15-minute ride to an hour-plus commute affect student homework, family responsibilities, extracurriculars, sleep, social-capital formation, and the daily rhythm of neighborhood connections vs. cross-town peer relationships? What observational indicators would reveal whether transportation decisions are strengthening or eroding the social fabric? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Audience Advocate, Systems Thinker]

2. **What structural-equity dimensions of transportation and access quietly exclude the most vulnerable families?** For students with physical disabilities, medical conditions, or sensory processing needs (what does the ride actually feel like for a student who uses a wheelchair or needs medication access); for students whose families can't drive them to 6 a.m. band practice or late sports pickups, or who can't afford participation fees; for parents who rely on older siblings for after-school care or depend on schools for before/after care; for school-board meetings relocated 30 miles away or parent-teacher conferences requiring a half-day off work; and for families without reliable internet, on unpaved roads that snowplows treat as low priority, or working jobs without midday flexibility — which families simply drop out of participation, and who gains voice while losing it? [Audience Advocate, AA structural-equity dimensions]

3. **Special-education continuity and language/cultural accessibility across the merger.** When IEPs were developed in small districts with specific resources and relationships, how does consolidation affect service delivery, family-staff relationships, and continuity of accommodations? In districts with varying proportions of English-language-learner families, how does consolidation affect translation services, cultural-liaison staff, and the ability of non-English-speaking parents to advocate for their children's needs? [Audience Advocate, AA structural-equity dimensions]

## Topic Cluster 07: Belonging, Culture, and Student Voice in the Merger

1. **Belonging in transition — when rival communities combine.** When students from three distinct school communities merge into consolidated buildings — often long-standing rivals with sports rivalries, historical tensions, and deep community identities — what concrete experiences help them form a shared identity rather than feeling like "those kids from [other town]"? What happens during lunch, hallway passing, pep rallies, and the informal moments that shape whether students feel they belong, and how do we proactively design school-culture initiatives that acknowledge community identities while building new shared traditions — rather than letting student conflict, bullying, and factionalism undermine the environment? How would the presence of older students in consolidated K-12 or middle/high-shared buildings affect younger students' sense of safety and ability to navigate school as children rather than "mini high schoolers"? [Audience Advocate, Devil's Advocate]

2. **Student voice, invisible losses, and the experience we want children to remember.** If we asked students themselves what matters most about their school experience, what would they say that adults rarely consider, and what invisible losses (teachers who know their family history, certainty of making the sports team, the sense that this place is "theirs") don't show up in test scores or finances but shape daily experience? Imagine ten years from now a child who started kindergarten in a newly consolidated district is graduating — what memories would make them say "I'm so glad I went through schools in this county," and what structures would need to be in place from day one to make that experience reliable? [Audience Advocate, Appreciative Inquirer, AI orphan register]

3. **Governance that builds trust and redefines "our school" without losing community.** Voter approval requires trust that local interests won't be drowned out by a larger system — what governance innovations (weighted board representation, town-level advisory councils with real authority, participatory budgeting at the school level, community-school compacts) could make consolidated governance feel MORE locally responsive, and how might "our school" expand to mean "our county's schools" while still feeling personally ours, with each town developing a genuine stake in the larger district rather than casualties of it? [Appreciative Inquirer, AI orphan register]

## Topic Cluster 08: Community Identity and the Emotional Interior of Loss

1. **What does "our school" mean to people who have nothing else — and what actually dies when a school closes?** For communities where the school is the only remaining institution that says "we exist as a town," beyond the building itself, what rituals, gatherings, and informal social glue disappear, and what emotional void does that leave for a town already watching its population dwindle? When consolidation is framed as fiscal necessity but feels like losing control, how do we distinguish between the practical reality of insolvency and the visceral experience of surrender — and how do we talk about losing without making people feel like they've lost, by starting from what people are afraid to lose rather than from "gaining efficiency"? [Empath, emotional-interior register]

2. **Town identity without a school — Friday night lights, hometown marquee, property values, and the stories people tell about who they are.** When a community's school closes, what happens to the town's sense of itself? What does it feel like to drive past the empty building, attend Friday night games in someone else's town, and explain to new residents where "your" school went? How would homeowners perceive the impact on property values — even if financial reality shows minimal effect — and what emotions and fears drive their behavior at the polls regardless of what the data says, when the hometown marquee and rivalries that gave communities their narrative get folded into someone else's identity? [Audience Advocate, Empath, emotional-interior register]

3. **Parent trust, proximity loss, and what kids carry when told their school isn't good enough.** When the administration shifts from someone at the grocery store to a centralized office 45 minutes away, how does distance affect parents' willingness to advocate, report concerns, or feel heard, and what are parents grieving when they can no longer drop in for a game, watch practice, or see their child's teacher around town? When the consolidation narrative is "your district is failing," how does that internalize for an 8-year-old or 16-year-old who hears "my town couldn't figure it out" — and does genuine participation feel possible when the feasibility study, state pressure, and fiscal deadlines signal the decision is already made? [Audience Advocate, Empath, emotional-interior register]

4. **Redefining "our school" without losing community.** If consolidation succeeded beyond anyone's wildest dreams — meaning towns felt MORE connected to their schools, not less — what new traditions, governance structures, or community-school partnerships could emerge that give each town a genuine stake in the larger district while preserving local identity? How might "our school" expand to mean "our county's schools" while still feeling personally ours, and how much of the resistance is actually to the practical changes versus the terminology and framing (e.g., "creating a premier rural education network" vs. "consolidation")? [Appreciative Inquirer, Questioner, AI orphan register]

## Topic Cluster 09: Measuring Quality and the Quality/Cost Feedback Loop

1. **How do we measure educational quality impacts, and how might Eroding Goals silently shift the standard?** Beyond test scores, what metrics — class size variability, course offerings, teacher retention, extracurricular participation (fewer spots on teams/casts/clubs), weakened teacher-student connections in larger schools — should be tracked? And if consolidation is sold as "maintaining quality while cutting costs" but post-consolidation reality reveals persistent budget pressure despite administrative savings, the district may gradually redefine "acceptable" — first freezing programs, then expanding class sizes, then reducing course offerings. Each step seems reasonable given constraints, but over five years the community's expectations quietly erode. What guardrails prevent this silent standard creep? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker]

2. **What is the economic model for maintaining educational quality during consolidation — and for systematically amplifying each district's existing strengths?** Financial analysis often focuses on operational costs but excludes the economic cost of quality erosion: if consolidation drives families away, each 100-student loss represents $800K–$1M in state funding. What is the ROI on quality-preserving investments (keeping elementary schools in each town while consolidating secondary schools, teacher-retention bonuses to prevent attrition) — and what if consolidation were designed around systematically amplifying each district's existing strengths (one excels at special-ed inclusion, another at community partnerships, another at fiscal efficiency) rather than creating one-size-fits-all uniformity? [Accountant, Appreciative Inquirer, AI orphan register]

## Topic Cluster 10: Systems Dynamics — Loops, Leverage, and Root Causes

1. **What self-amplifying patterns might consolidation trigger, and how do delay effects obscure their true impact?** If consolidation improves educational quality through better resources, it could attract families — increasing enrollment and funding, which further improves quality. But could the reverse loop dominate: longer bus rides and loss of community identity accelerate out-migration of young families, worsening enrollment decline, triggering more cuts and more flight? The financial benefits appear immediately (administrative savings, state funding increases), but the social costs (community disintegration, student alienation, loss of "third place" hubs) compound gradually over 5–10 years — by the time those delayed consequences become visible, decision-makers are gone and the causal link is forgotten. Which loop is stronger under what conditions, and what early warning indicators reveal whether the system is trending toward resilience or decay before effects become irreversible? [Systems Thinker]

2. **Where might Shifting-the-Burden dynamics undermine long-term sustainability, and what natural immune responses will counterbalance consolidation pressures?** Consolidation offers a short-term fix for budget deficits by eliminating administrative overhead, but if the underlying driver is enrollment decline, the relief is temporary — as enrollment continues falling, the consolidated district faces the same structural deficits, only now with less local control, fewer trimmable positions, and a more cumbersome bureaucracy. The temporary fix erodes capacity to address the root cause. Meanwhile, rural communities often have immune responses to perceived threats — parent co-ops, homeschooling networks, charter schools, increased political activism. What symptoms indicate the district is relying on consolidation benefits rather than building genuine resilience, and where are the system's self-correcting forces strongest? [Systems Thinker]

3. **Where are the non-obvious leverage points that don't involve full consolidation, and where is the highest-leverage intervention actually at the state-policy level?** The debate frames consolidation as binary. But could shared-services agreements for transportation, special education, or professional development capture 60–70% of administrative savings without triggering community-identity loss? Could a "soft consolidation" — unified purchasing, shared superintendent, joint curriculum development — build trust before any structural merger? And often the highest-leverage intervention is changing the rules at the next system level: what state-policy tweaks (transportation reimbursement ratios, minimum school size requirements, curriculum mandates, teacher certification rules) would dramatically improve rural district viability without requiring consolidation at all? [Systems Thinker]

4. **Could the consolidated district restructure its relationship to the enrollment-decline driver itself, rather than just managing symptoms?** The core problem is demographic: rural counties face population aging, youth out-migration, and economic transition. School consolidation treats enrollment as a given, not a dynamic to be influenced — but schools themselves affect enrollment: quality attracts families, community engagement builds retention, career-technical education creates local economic opportunity. When a town loses its school, it also loses the venue for community meetings, youth sports, voting, and town events; local businesses lose daily foot traffic; property values decline; the town's perceived viability deteriorates. What feedback loops connect school quality to community vitality, and could the consolidated district become an active driver of demographic reversal rather than a passive victim — and what economic and social impact assessment would capture these second- and third-order consequences? [Systems Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 11: Futures, Contingencies, and Alternatives

1. **What scenario planning, tripwires, and failure modes apply if consolidation doesn't solve the fiscal problem?** If the consolidated district still faces structural deficits five years post-merger (projected savings smaller than anticipated, new costs emerging, enrollment declines continuing), what are the specific failure scenarios (consolidated-district insolvency in year 4, voter rejection, mass teacher departures, union-reconciliation collapse, state funding-formula changes again after consolidation), what early warning indicators would signal those trajectories, and — since district consolidation is effectively irreversible — what accountability mechanisms and correction triggers (fiscal audit after 3 years, performance benchmarks) should be identified before committing? [Devil's Advocate, Analyst, Questioner]

2. **What alternative and phased models might achieve similar benefits without full consolidation?** Shared-services agreements (joint purchasing, shared special-education staff, cooperative transportation), partial consolidation (administrative merger while retaining local schools and boards), regional high-school districts, a phased approach where two districts consolidate first and the third joins later, or a "pilot consolidation" of specific functions (transportation, special education, HR) preceding full merger — all could capture efficiency gains while preserving local autonomy. What rigorous comparison precedes committing to the most disruptive path, and what smaller-scale experiments (shared superintendent, combined professional-development days, joint extracurricular programs, shared specialty courses) could demonstrate that collaboration works before asking voters to decide? [Devil's Advocate, Questioner, Appreciative Inquirer, AI orphan register]

3. **How might documented 2020s trends — remote-work migration, hybrid/micro-schools, aging demographics, rural economic diversification, and state-level consolidation incentives — reshape the 2035 picture?** The pandemic accelerated rural in-migration as remote work normalized; rural populations are aging faster than urban counterparts (with school funding dependent on property taxes and voters without school-age children); rural economies are shifting toward remote work, tourism, and niche manufacturing; and multiple states are actively incentivizing district consolidation through funding-formula changes since 2020. What sensitivity analysis accounts for potential in-migration stabilizing enrollment, how should facility closure and repurposing preserve option value for hybrid learning and community learning hubs, and how does consolidation timing align with the likelihood that the state funding environment will shift further toward consolidated districts within 3–5 years? [Futurist]

4. **How much of the fiscal pressure comes from clinging to bricks-and-mortar delivery?** If we relaxed the assumption that all students must be physically present in designated buildings every day — which the pandemic already proved possible — what does the "consolidation" calculus look like, and what simplest possible structure delivers on every child's access to quality education, every teacher's fair compensation, and every community's finite resources? [First Principles Thinker]

## Appended Questions (Perspective, Provocation, and Distinctive-Register Personas)

### Provocateur (Append)

1. **What if "school districts" were the wrong unit of analysis entirely?** Imagine if education funding, governance, and enrollment were organized around something completely different — students traveling to learning rather than schools receiving them, teachers as independent contractors serving multiple micro-communities, or education organized around "learning seasons" rather than geographic boundaries. What would become possible if we stopped assuming consolidated districts are the only alternative to fragmented ones? *The insight: we're debating the architecture of a system whose fundamental unit may be obsolete.*

2. **What if every student attended a different "school" every week?** Imagine a system where students rotated through all 7 buildings on a rolling basis, where no town had a "local school" but everyone had access to every facility. Or imagine schools were organized around student interests rather than geography — the STEM building, the arts building, the nature school — and transportation became the primary cost instead of duplication. What would this reveal about what "community school" actually means versus what we assume it means? *The insight: we're protecting a concept of local schooling that may already be fiction for rural students facing long commutes anyway.*

3. **What if declining enrollment was framed as an educational asset rather than a crisis?** Imagine a world where smaller student-teacher ratios, individualized attention, and underutilized facilities were positioned as luxury advantages rather than inefficiencies. What if the districts competed to attract students FROM each other using quality and experience rather than fighting against consolidation? What if the problem wasn't "not enough students" but "too much infrastructure designed for a different era"? *The insight: enrollment decline is only a failure if metrics designed for growth are applied to shrinkage — what if we designed for contraction instead?*

4. **What if we planned for the district to be dissolved in 20 years?** Imagine designing the consolidation with an explicit expiration date — create the unified district with the assumption that it will need to radically restructure or cease to exist in 2035. How would facility decisions, contract negotiations, transportation planning, and community engagement change if this were a transition state rather than a permanent solution? What interim structures would you build differently if you knew they were temporary? *The insight: treating consolidation as "forever" locks in decisions that should remain adaptable as conditions continue changing.*

5. **What if consolidation required every student to change schools, not just some?** Rather than closing buildings and displacing students from closed schools, imagine requiring ALL 1,800 students to reassess and re-enroll under a new system with no guaranteed placement. What if "our school is our town" was disrupted for everyone rather than protected for a few? How would the political dynamics and coalition-building change if no community received special protection? *The insight: we're trying to minimize disruption when sometimes equitable disruption creates different political possibilities.*

6. **What if the goal was to make consolidation fail spectacularly?** Imagine designing the consolidation process specifically to maximize community outrage, financial waste, and educational disruption. What would you do? Close all buildings simultaneously? Fire everyone and rehire? Auction off assets to private buyers? Now work backward: what does this reveal about which safeguards, pacing decisions, and transition structures actually matter most? *The insight: sometimes disaster planning reveals what success requires more clearly than optimization does.*

7. **What if the districts deliberately made themselves LESS efficient before consolidating?** Imagine if each district, knowing consolidation was coming, accelerated hiring, raised salaries, took on debt, and expanded programs specifically to maximize the "savings" that consolidation would appear to generate. What if consolidation was structured as a rescue rather than a merger? How would the politics and narrative differ if the new district "saved" failing districts rather than "rationalizing" functional ones? *The insight: we're pretending consolidation is about efficiency when it's really about solvency — what if we named that directly?*

### Storyteller (Append)

1. **The Last Day at Maple Grove Elementary** Imagine walking through the halls of Maple Grove Elementary on what might be its final day of operation as a neighborhood school. The walls display photos of seven generations of students who learned to read in these classrooms. The principal's grandmother attended this school, as did her mother, and now her own daughter faces the prospect of being bused 45 minutes away. How might a community ritually honor and preserve 90 years of collective memory when a building stops being a school, and what transitional objects or ceremonies could help families carry their school's identity forward rather than experiencing it as an amputation?

2. **The New Kid, Again** Consider the experience from Javier's perspective, an eighth grader who has already changed schools three times due to family circumstances. His current school, though small and resource-constrained, has been the one constant where teachers know his learning needs and he's finally found a friend group. Now he faces the prospect of another transition to a consolidated middle school where he'll be one of 400 students instead of 80. What specific scaffolding and continuity bridges could transform this transition from a source of anxiety into an opportunity for expanded horizons, and how might the consolidation process actively identify and support students for whom stability is the foundation of learning?

3. **The Day the Bus Route Changed** Picture a Tuesday morning six months after consolidation, when students from three formerly separate communities step off buses at what was once "their rival's" high school. Some arrive excited, others resentful, many anxious. The cafeteria now serves students who grew up hearing each other described as "those people from across the county." What happens in that first week, first semester, first year to weave these separate identities into something that doesn't erase differences but creates new shared experiences—through joint traditions, collaborative projects, spaces that honor all three communities' histories—and what role might students themselves play in authoring this new chapter rather than having it written for them?

4. **When "Consolidation" Arrives on the Doorstep** Picture the morning when Emma, a single mother working two jobs in the smallest district, receives the consolidation proposal in the mail. Her son Marcus has thrived under the personal attention of teachers who've known him since kindergarten—teachers who may now lose their seniority or be reassigned. Emma has no car and relies on the walking-distance school for before-school care and after-school activities while she works. What happens to families like Emma's when the infrastructure of daily life—care, transportation, trusted relationships—must be rebuilt, and what alternative support ecosystems would need to exist to prevent consolidation from becoming an existential crisis for the most vulnerable families?

5. **A Tale of Two School Years** Imagine following two teachers—Ms. Chen from the smallest district, who has taught science with outdated textbooks and no lab equipment, and Mr. Davis from the largest district, who has a fully-equipped lab but dwindling class sizes—as they navigate their first year in a consolidated district. Both have expertise to share and anxieties about losing autonomy. How might their professional journey unfold in ways that create a richer learning environment than either could provide alone, and what narrative frameworks could help them see themselves as collaborators in building something new rather than competitors in a zero-sum reorganization?

6. **Who Gets to Tell the History?** When three districts consolidate, whose version of the past gets preserved in yearbooks, trophy cases, and school names? The current proposal suggests keeping one high school open, converting another to a middle school, and closing a third entirely. Students and alumni from the closed high school will see their championship banners, theater productions, and commencement rituals subsumed under another institution's identity. How might a consolidated district actively honor the distinct histories of all three communities while creating space for new shared stories to emerge, and what processes could involve current students in designing this layered identity rather than having it imposed by adults who made the consolidation decisions?
