# Brainstorming Questions: Mobile App Simplification

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Defining Core Value and the "One Thing"

1. **What "core value" actually means in practice**: Are we looking for the features most people use, or the features that create the most passionate engagement — and what if those aren't the same thing? What if the problem isn't which features to keep, but which job users hired us to do when they downloaded the app, and have we asked them directly? [Questioner]

2. **Differentiator test without usage data**: How would we identify our true differentiator if we couldn't see any usage data? What does our product do that no other fitness app does, regardless of whether users currently prioritize it — and why do we assume we need to be in the fitness app category at all versus an adjacent space we've accidentally stumbled into? [Questioner]

3. **The "One Thing" evolution and emotional transformation users seek**: If this app became known for doing one thing exceptionally well — the thing that makes users think "I can't live without this" — what would that be, how would that single focus shape every aspect from onboarding to word-of-mouth, and what specific feeling or outcome would a three-years-from-now champion describe? [Appreciative Inquirer]

4. **What "essential" means from the user's lived context**: The team frames "essential" from a product strategy perspective — but what does essential mean to a single parent with 5 minutes, or someone managing chronic illness needing simple tracking without cognitive overload? What might we learn about users' broader lives — constraints, motivations, environments — that would inform which features actually matter in their real, messy lives? [Audience Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 02: Distinguishing Signal from Noise in Usage Data

1. **Feature Engagement Matrix vs. keystone features and on-ramps-to-nowhere**: What data points determine which features truly drive retention versus which are merely noise — examining feature stickiness, dependency patterns, and correlation with 30-day retention — and how do we distinguish features that drive retention from those that merely attract initial engagement before users quit (given 8% retention makes usage data potentially misleading)? How do we identify "keystone features" whose removal would disproportionately harm the ecosystem, recognizing that the current Success-to-the-Successful dynamic may be amplifying the wrong things? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker]

2. **Cohort segmentation and the gap between stated and actual behavior**: What distinct user cohorts exist (social users, solo trackers, content consumers), which features serve as "gateway drugs" versus "retention anchors" for each, and what gaps exist between what users say they want and what they actually do daily (given the 47-second session and 2% DAU/MAU)? [Analyst, Audience Advocate]

3. **Interviewing the 8% who stayed and the niche passionate bases**: What would happen if we interviewed the 8% who stayed past 30 days and asked specifically what they'd stop using if we removed it, and how do we identify features with small but passionate user bases before we cut them — recognizing a feature with 2% adoption but 80% retention among those users could be a foothold into an underserved niche or a dead-end? What leading indicators would help us distinguish "early adopters of the future core" from "enthusiastic users of a peripheral feature," and could these micro-communities reveal the app's true north? [Questioner, Systems Thinker, Audience Advocate]

4. **Self-selection bias and unarticulated needs of the 92%**: Users who respond to surveys are likely the most engaged minority, potentially leading us to overoptimize for power users while ignoring the 92% who leave within 30 days. What research methods help us understand why the majority abandon without alienating the ones who still care, and what frustrations or workarounds have users normalized that could illuminate the true core value? [Devil's Advocate, Audience Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 03: Feature Popularity vs. Genuine Value (Unified Core Assumption)

1. **Does a unified "core value" even exist, and is the real problem execution quality or discoverability?** What if retention is low not because the app is unfocused but because no single feature is good enough to compete with dedicated alternatives, or because users might actually want these features if they could find and understand them? How do we distinguish between "overwhelming because too much" and "overwhelming because poorly designed" — and what if simplification means better UX design rather than fewer features? [Devil's Advocate, Questioner]

2. **False consensus in team decision-making and one-PM research limits**: A 6-engineer team likely has 6 opinions about the "core," and each may champion features they built. With only one PM, who determines which feedback matters and what "core value" means? What decision framework could objectively identify core value without letting ownership bias, team politics, or one person's biases drive the outcome? [Devil's Advocate]

3. **Combinatorial core — when 2-3 features together are the value**: How can we distinguish between features that are central to the value proposition versus those that merely surround it, recognizing that if the core is an unexpected combination of 2-3 features working together, usage data that treats each feature as an independent silo will never detect it? [Systems Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 04: Unit Economics, Runway, and Technical Debt

1. **Unit economics, ROI of simplification, and runway trade-offs**: What does the cost structure look like per feature — engineering maintenance, customer support burden, server costs, opportunity cost — and how do we calculate ROI of cutting features in terms of freed resources versus revenue/user impact risk? With 8 months of funding and a team of 8, how much does reducing feature surface lower burn versus extend runway, and at what point does simplification trade long-term viability for short-term survival? [Analyst]

2. **Technical debt quantification, hidden dependencies, and the Fixes-that-Fail risk**: How do we measure maintenance cost of technical debt embedded in each feature, and what's the difference between "delete-and-forget" features and "excise-carefully" ones with deep system integration? Features that look independent in the UI may share authentication, data models, notification systems, or analytics pipelines — cutting them could leave ghost code or break dependencies, creating a "Fixes that Fail" pattern where we cut features to simplify but complexity remains. How can we assess whether engineering cost of removal might consume the entire 8-month runway without delivering a working product, and have we mapped the dependency graph before assuming removal is simpler than maintenance? [Analyst, Systems Thinker, Devil's Advocate, Questioner]

3. **The 8-month runway as a false constraint with unknown unknowns**: Eight months sounds sufficient, but if feature removal uncovers hidden dependencies, requires database migrations, or triggers user backlash, we could run out of funding before launching anything viable. There's a structural time pressure: we won't know if the simplified product works until 3-6 months after launch but have only 8 months of runway — moving too fast risks cutting the wrong features, moving too slow risks running out of money. What's the minimum viable timeline, what buffer do we need for unknown unknowns, and at what point do we pivot to incremental simplification? [Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 05: Churn Risk, User Segments, and Who We Design For

1. **Churn risk model and cascading user loss from cuts**: When we remove features, what's the expected churn from affected segments — how do we identify "feature-dependent" versus "feature-agnostic" users, and what's the quantifiable trade-off between simplification and user loss in LTV impact? Each cut might trigger abandonment from passionate niches, and cutting multiple simultaneously could cause compounding churn — what's the maximum acceptable user loss, and what contingency plans exist if simplification backfires? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

2. **Designing for the users we want vs. the users we have**: The 180K downloads with 8% retention means ~165K have churned — our current base is the tail end of a leaky funnel, and the most engaged users are likely using a tiny subset of features while the confused majority left entirely. Why do we assume our existing users are the right audience for the simplified product, rather than fundamentally pivoting to a different market? What would it look like to design for the users we want, and whose needs might be systematically overlooked when feature prioritization reflects loudest voices and most data visibility? [Systems Thinker, Questioner, Audience Advocate]

3. **Accessibility, literacy, and tolerance for complexity across segments**: A 25-year-old fitness enthusiast and a 55-year-old trying to get healthy have vastly different tolerance for complexity. Which features create barriers for users with different tech literacy, language proficiency, or accessibility needs, and how might feature prioritization change if we centered users with limited digital literacy, non-English speakers, or those relying on assistive technologies? Which communities are most impacted by notification and feature overload — are we driving away the users who might benefit most? [Audience Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 06: Transition Strategy — Incremental vs. Clean Relaunch

1. **Incremental vs. big-bang with the erosion-of-goals trap**: What are the comparative risk profiles of gradual simplification versus clean relaunch — user confusion, technical complexity, market perception, team bandwidth? Why do we assume a clean relaunch is better than removing one feature per month and measuring impact each time? Incremental simplification feels safer but creates a self-reinforcing pattern of compromise (erosion of goals) where each round has to justify itself to existing users, so we keep "just one more feature" and the product never becomes truly focused — while a clean relaunch risks alienating all 180K users at once. [Analyst, Questioner, Systems Thinker]

2. **Testing feature removal before committing, and the "simplify into nothing" trap**: Could we ship a version with one feature temporarily disabled and see who complains — or who doesn't notice? What experimental design allows us to test feature combinations without alienating users, and what statistical significance thresholds should we set before committing? If users are already frustrated with "overwhelming" complexity, what if the true issue isn't feature count but poor information architecture — how do we validate that simplification will actually improve retention before irreversibly removing features that might be working for some users? [Questioner, Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

3. **The relaunch assumption itself — will users give us a second chance?** A "clean relaunch" assumes users will return after we've burned them, but app stores are littered with failed "2.0" relaunches where users had already moved on. What evidence suggests our existing base will return, and are we over-indexing on retention versus acquisition of net-new users? [Devil's Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 07: Metrics, Validation, and Redefining Success

1. **Redefining the North Star beyond engagement**: Current metrics (2% DAU/MAU, 47-second sessions, 8% retention) signal PMF failure — what leading indicators should we target instead, and how do we distinguish "better engagement" from "fewer features masking the same problems"? Engagement isn't value delivered: a simpler app might have shorter sessions because users accomplish what they need faster, which would look "worse" on metrics but represent better design. The more bloated the app, the longer users wander lost, the "better" engagement looks — what success metric would actually capture core value delivery? [Analyst, Systems Thinker]

2. **Post-launch validation dashboard and distinguishing outcomes**: Once we relaunch, what early warning signs and success metrics should we monitor at 30, 60, and 90 days — how do we distinguish "users mourning removed features" from "genuine product-market fit" from "solved the wrong problem"? [Analyst]

3. **First experiments this month**: What small, low-risk experiments could the team try this month to test which features truly represent the app's core value? What would you measure, and how would you engage users in a way that makes them feel part of the product's evolution rather than subjects of it? [Appreciative Inquirer]

## Topic Cluster 08: Communication, Trust Repair, and User Co-Creation

1. **Communication strategy that avoids Shifting-the-Burden and loss framing**: What's the optimal sequence and messaging for announcing feature removal — balancing transparency and trust against minimizing churn — and what metrics tell us if we're getting it right? Could we frame simplification as "we're focusing on what matters most" rather than "we're taking things away," recognizing users might experience reduction as loss rather than simplification? If we rely heavily on "we're simplifying for you" reassurance, users may become dependent on us to frame the change — this is a Shifting-the-Burden dynamic where external prompts replace intrinsic motivation, eroding users' capacity to self-motivate and creating a self-amplifying need for handholding. How do we communicate without creating dependency? [Analyst, Questioner, Audience Advocate, Systems Thinker]

2. **Rebuilding trust and co-creating with burned users**: Users who stuck around despite poor experience may feel betrayed. What would it take to demonstrate genuine commitment to their needs rather than just business survival, and what would genuine user co-creation look like in determining which features survive — bringing the community into the decision-making process rather than communicating decisions after they're made? How might the relaunch be an opportunity to repair relationships and design a product with the community rather than for them? [Audience Advocate]

3. **Migration with grace and acknowledging feature loss**: Considering small but passionate user bases, how might we honor their commitment while simplifying — what would a graceful transition look like that acknowledges loyalty while guiding them toward the core value, and how might we understand and address the experience of loss for users who deeply relied on a now-removed feature? [Appreciative Inquirer, Audience Advocate]

## Topic Cluster 09: Team Morale, Capacity, and the Psychology of Killing Darlings

1. **Team morale and the defensive-attachment dynamic**: If engineers spent months or years building features now being deleted, how will they stay motivated — especially when "core" features require working on code they didn't write? There's a self-reinforcing dynamic: if people feel ownership over "their" features, they resist cuts, which maintains bloat, which increases maintenance burden, lowering morale further — conversely, experiencing the relief of a focused product creates momentum. What's the smallest intervention that could flip this from defensive attachment to proactive pruning, and what mechanisms rebuild psychological investment after "killing darlings"? Are we underestimating the psychological impact — how do we help engineers and designers feel proud of simplification rather than feeling they "failed"? [Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker, Questioner]

2. **Team reimagined and aligned with strengths**: Picture the team feeling energized, creative, and proud — no longer burned out maintaining 15 half-built features. What would their day-to-day look like, what product culture would they be building, and what kind of simplified product would align with their existing strengths (technical capabilities, design sensibilities, domain expertise) so simplification feels like liberation rather than loss? [Appreciative Inquirer]

3. **Post-simplification capacity and the slow return to bloat**: Six engineers maintaining 15 features are stretched thin, but six maintaining 3 suddenly have excess capacity — creating a self-amplifying opportunity where each remaining feature can become dramatically better, attracting more users and enabling further improvements. But excess capacity can lead to scope creep, slowly adding features back until bloated again. What structural constraints or processes would prevent the "slow return to bloat" pattern? [Systems Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 10: User Experience, Emotional Journey, and What's Already Working

1. **Peak user experiences and features that sparked joy**: Tell me about moments when a user genuinely loved this app — a session beyond 47 seconds, a time someone told a friend. What were they doing, what problem was solved so well they felt grateful, and among the 15 features which elicited the most enthusiasm, sustained engagement, or strongest loyalty — was it utility, experience, social connection, or something else? How might we design the entire product to create more of those specific moments? [Appreciative Inquirer]

2. **Emotional journey from app-open to app-close**: What emotional states do users experience opening the app, and what determines whether they stay or leave? Beyond "overwhelmed" or "lost," what's the specific emotional journey from app-open to app-close — where do they feel confusion, frustration, or relief, and at what precise moments do they decide to disengage? [Audience Advocate]

3. **The post-removal "withdrawal" period and which loop dominates**: What happens during the 3-6 month period after feature removal when habits formed around removed features persist but the features are gone? Users who built routines around meal planner, habit tracker, or meditation guide will reach for features that no longer exist — a balancing loop where frustration reduces engagement, which makes the app feel less valuable. But if the simplified experience is genuinely better, frustration may transition to relief, creating a virtuous cycle. What determines which loop dominates? [Systems Thinker]

4. **Hidden assets in the user base and building from existing strengths**: With 180K downloads, even at low retention, there are users who found value — who are they, what use case or demographic found it worthwhile, and what might we learn from the 2% who show up daily? What resources, capabilities, or assets are already in place — technical infrastructure, user insights, team skills, community relationships, learnings from the current feature set — that could accelerate the move toward a focused product and make the reset feel like evolution rather than restart? [Appreciative Inquirer]

## Appended Questions (Perspective, Provocation, and Distinctive-Register Personas)

### Provocateur (Append)

1. **What if you kept exactly ONE feature and made it absurdly worse?** Imagine you stripped the app down to just workout tracking, but then removed all logging, history, progress visualization, and data—leaving only the immediate act of pressing a button that says "I worked out" before the app closes itself. What would this useless button reveal about what users actually crave from the tracking experience? *The real insight: is the value in the data or in the ritual of acknowledgment?*

2. **What if the app did nothing but DELETE features for the next 8 months?** Every week, one feature disappears permanently with no replacement, no explanation, and no user choice—until month 9 when the app becomes entirely blank. What would user behavior and retention patterns in this controlled demolition reveal about which features create genuine dependence versus which create mere noise? *The real insight: what if feature removal itself—rather than the final simplified state—was the product experience that taught you what matters?*

3. **What if your 180K downloads were 180K different people who never opened the app twice?** Imagine the retention disaster is actually a feature—your app is designed for single-use transformation, like a ceremonial rite of passage after which users are "graduated" and should never return. How would you redesign the onboarding experience to complete someone's entire fitness journey in 47 seconds? *The real insight: what if the lifetime value model is wrong for this problem and the real opportunity is in perfect, self-contained moments?*

4. **What if you had to explain this app to someone from 1995 who has never seen a mobile phone?** They understand "fitness," "food," "friends," and "shopping" as physical, non-digital experiences. As you describe your app, which features make sense in their world and which become meaningless? What does the alien-ness of "social fitness" or "digital meal planning" reveal about assumptions you've never questioned? *The real insight: which parts of your product exist only because they're possible in software, not because they actually solve human problems?*

5. **What if the 2% who actually use the app daily are the problem, not the solution?** Imagine your power users are actually tumors—pathological attachments to features that represent the app's worst impulses, and their retention is preventing the product from evolving into something healthy for normal humans. What would happen if you actively tried to drive away your most engaged users? *The real insight: what if your retention metrics are optimized for a tiny minority of freak users while everyone else is correctly recognizing that the product doesn't serve them?*

### Storyteller (Append)

1. **What would the "first five minutes" experience look like for a returning user who opens the simplified app for the first time, and what emotional state should we be designing toward?** Imagine it's Tuesday morning and Sarah, who previously faced 15 features and 6 navigation tabs, opens the new version. Walk through what she sees, what she feels, and what she can accomplish in those critical first minutes. How do we ensure the transition from confusion to clarity happens within a single session? What does "success" look like in that first interaction—should she feel relief, excitement, curiosity, or something else entirely?

2. **How might the "moment of deletion" unfold for different types of users when features they've incorporated into their routines disappear, and what narrative threads could help them feel excited rather than abandoned?** Consider Marcus, who uses the supplement store feature weekly, versus Priya, who relies on the progress journal as her primary touchpoint. When they open the simplified app and discover these features gone, what happens in the seconds before they either delete the app or give the new version a chance? What story could the app tell them in that moment—and through what channels (push notifications, onboarding, email, in-app messaging)—that would transform a sense of loss into curiosity about the newly focused experience?

3. **How might the engineering team's daily work life transform when they shift from maintaining 15 half-built features to perfecting 3-4 world-class experiences, and what "micro-moments" of professional satisfaction will replace the current burnout cycle?** Walk us through the standup meeting six months from now. What language are engineers using to describe their work? How do conversations about quality, testing, and user feedback change when they're not spread thin across a feature graveyard? What moments of pride—seeing a user review, watching usage metrics, shipping a polished experience—replace the current reality of constant bug fixes and technical debt management? How does the team's relationship with the codebase itself evolve when they're not afraid to touch it?

4. **What does the "funeral" scene look like when the team decides which features to cut, and how can we turn a potentially demoralizing process into a moment of clarity and shared purpose?** Imagine the product owner standing at the whiteboard with 15 feature cards, knowing that 11 of them are about to be "killed." How does that conversation unfold? What frameworks or rituals could transform this from a series of painful decisions into a collective act of strategic vision? How do we ensure that every person in that room—whether they built the meditation guide or the friend finder—leaves feeling energized about the new direction rather than mourning their specific contribution? What narrative about the company's future could make sacrificing the present feel like a victory rather than a loss?

5. **What would the "six-months-later" user testimonial sound like if this simplification gamble paid off, and how would that story contrast with the current user experience of overwhelm and disengagement?** Write the narrative of a user who stuck with the app through the transition and is now an evangelist. What specific moments do they reference when describing why the new version works? How do they talk about what the app used to be, and what does that comparison reveal about the transformation? If we're successful, what story will users tell each other about this app—the "why did it take them so long to figure this out?" story versus the "I can't believe they killed my favorite feature" story? How do we design the transition and the communication strategy to make the former inevitable?

### Visionary (Append)

1. **[What if fitness wasn't something you track, but something that emerges naturally through a fundamentally different relationship with your body?]** Instead of asking which features to keep or cut, what if we completely reimagined the category: What would a fitness product look like if it were designed around joy, emergence, and organic behavior change rather than metrics, tracking, and discipline? What if the core value isn't "helping people work out" but something entirely different that doesn't have a current analog in the fitness app space?

2. **[Imagine if the app could disappear entirely as an interface and instead become a presence that anticipates and supports without requiring active engagement?]** What would it mean to invert the relationship between user and app — where the app exists not as something you open, but as something that works on your behalf in the background, creating interventions at the moment they're needed without requiring you to navigate tabs or manage features? What would a zero-UI fitness product look like?

3. **[What if user retention isn't about features at all, but about identity transformation — and what if we designed for that psychological shift instead of for engagement?]** Consider that the 2% DAU/MAU ratio might not be a feature problem but an identity problem: users don't see themselves as "people who use this app." What if we completely rethought onboarding, not as a tutorial, but as an identity-forming ritual that fundamentally reshapes how users view themselves? What would an app look like if its primary function was identity construction rather than fitness tracking?

4. **[What if instead of choosing 3-4 core features, we created a product with no features at all — just a single, coherent behavior that users engage in repeatedly?]** Imagine a fitness app with no tracking, no planning, no social features, no content — just one elegant, repeatable action that somehow produces fitness outcomes. What would that one action be? What if the app became a "one-button" product where pressing that button initiates a cascade of value without requiring navigation, configuration, or decisions?

5. **[What if the 47-second average session isn't a problem to be solved, but the actual product — and what if we leaned into that rather than trying to extend engagement?]** What would it mean to embrace the micro-session pattern and design something that delivers maximum value in under a minute? Could this become a fundamentally new product category — the "instant fitness intervention" that exists in the interstitial moments of daily life? What if the app's role isn't to occupy attention but to punctuate it with brief, transformative experiences?

### Connector (Append)

1. **Feature pruning as ecosystem succession** The challenge of cutting features mirrors ecological succession, where crowded ecosystems self-thin to let dominant species thrive. In forest ecology, "self-thinning" creates space for the strongest organisms to flourish. What if we viewed the 15 features as competing species in an ecosystem — how would we identify which features are the "keystone species" whose presence enables the entire ecosystem to function, and which are the invasive species crowding out the core value?

2. **Museum curation as a model for feature preservation** When museums face space constraints, they don't simply throw away lesser works — they rotate collections, loan to other institutions, or create digital archives. The 15 features likely each have some users (even if small). Rather than binary keep/kill decisions, what if we borrowed from museum curation practices: could some features be "rotated out" (temporarily disabled but preserved), "loaned" (spun off as separate apps), or "digitized archived" (export functionality for power users while removing from core experience)?

3. **Language death and feature migration** Linguistics studies how dying languages pass useful vocabulary to surviving languages before disappearing — structural elements get adopted, idioms get translated, but the language itself fades. This is different from simple deletion. What if we treated each feature like a dying language: what "vocabulary" (individual interactions, data structures, user habits) from cut features should be "migrated" into the surviving features, rather than assuming users will simply abandon those patterns? For example, if the "challenge platform" is cut, what competitive or social elements from it should be preserved in the remaining core?

4. **The retention-burnout feedback loop** These seem like separate problems: user retention is 8% and the team is burned out. But there's a structural parallel here — both users and the team are experiencing cognitive overload from the same source: the 15-feature sprawl. This suggests that solving the user retention problem (simplifying the experience) might simultaneously solve the team burnout problem (simplifying the codebase and maintenance burden). If we view these as one problem manifesting in two stakeholder groups, what does that reveal about prioritization — should features be evaluated based on their compound cost (user confusion + team maintenance) rather than user value alone?

5. **Notification architecture as feature dependency mapping** The user complaint about "too many notifications" reveals something deeper: notifications are a symptom of feature interdependencies. Each of the 15 features likely generates its own notification types, creating notification sprawl. But there's a parallel here with software dependency graphs — you can't remove a module without breaking everything that depends on it. What if the notification architecture is actually a dependency map in disguise? Could analyzing which notifications users actually engage with reveal which features are truly "core" versus peripheral — because features that don't generate meaningful notifications may be features users don't actually value?
