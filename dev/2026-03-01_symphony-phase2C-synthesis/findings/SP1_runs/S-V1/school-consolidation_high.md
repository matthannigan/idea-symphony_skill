# Brainstorming Questions: School District Consolidation

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Fundamental Framing and Alternatives

1. **Is consolidation the right question?**: At its most fundamental level, what are the essential educational services these 1,800 students actually need, and is "consolidating three districts into one" the only (or even best) way to deliver those services? What alternatives — shared services agreements, cooperative purchasing, administrative consolidation preserving local boards — could address the financial pressures without requiring full unification? [First Principles Thinker, Questioner, Devil's Advocate]

2. **Reimagining from scratch**: If we were building educational delivery for a rural county from scratch today, without any legacy assumptions about district boundaries, school buildings, or transportation models, what would the optimal structure look like? Are there fundamentally different ways to organize education (learning hubs, hybrid models, community-based micro-schools) that would work better than the traditional model we are trying to preserve? [First Principles Thinker, Questioner]

3. **Decline vs. structure — which is the real problem?**: Is the core problem that the districts are structured incorrectly (three small districts instead of one large one), or is the problem that rural education is inherently incompatible with declining enrollment and fixed infrastructure costs? If consolidation solves the immediate financial crisis, what fundamental challenges remain unaddressed? [First Principles Thinker, Systems Thinker, Devil's Advocate]

4. **Why is the state funding formula treated as fixed?**: What if the consolidation effort included a parallel advocacy campaign to reform the state funding formula that penalizes small districts? Could the energy and political capital required for consolidation be redirected toward changing the systemic disadvantage rather than restructuring around it? [Questioner, Systems Thinker]

5. **Why must all three districts merge simultaneously?**: What if a phased approach allowed two districts to consolidate first, demonstrate benefits, and then the third joins after seeing results? Or what if a "pilot consolidation" of specific functions (transportation, special education, HR) preceded full merger? [Questioner, Systems Thinker]

6. **Relaxing the bricks-and-mortar assumption**: How much of the fiscal pressure comes from clinging to a model of education that assumes all students must be physically present in designated buildings every day? If we relaxed that assumption — which the pandemic already proved possible — what does the consolidation calculus look like? [First Principles Thinker, Futurist]

## Topic Cluster 02: Financial Modeling and Break-Even

7. **True break-even timeline and transition costs**: Beyond $1.2M in administrative savings, what are the one-time transition costs (legal fees for dissolving three districts, union contract buyouts, integrated IT systems migration, facility closure costs, severance, early-retirement incentives) — and how many years of operations would it take to recover those costs? If transition costs run $3-5M, can the districts absorb the 3-4 year net-loss period, especially the smallest district with only a 2-year fund balance? [Accountant, Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Questioner]

8. **Unit economics at different enrollment scenarios**: A 12% per-pupil funding increase is a one-time structural improvement, but enrollment is projected to decline another 15% by 2035. At what student count does the consolidated district itself become financially unsustainable? Where is the crossover point at which consolidation savings no longer offset enrollment losses, and what is the "floor" for a viable rural district under the state funding formula? [Accountant, Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

9. **Full lifecycle facility economics**: Rather than looking only at annual maintenance deferred ($4.2M), what are the capital replacement schedules for each building — which facilities reach end-of-life in 5, 10, or 20 years? What are the avoided future costs (maintenance, utilities, insurance) versus one-time closure costs (asbestos abatement, environmental remediation, mothballing, demolition), and what is the NPV of keeping fewer but better-maintained facilities? Should sale proceeds or new construction of one modern building be part of the model? [Accountant, Analyst, Questioner]

10. **Debt, bonds, and obligation consolidation**: If District A has $8M in outstanding bonds while District B is debt-free, how does consolidation affect taxpayers — does debt get pooled proportionally, or do pre-consolidation voters remain responsible? Are there hidden liabilities (underfunded pensions, capitalized leases) that do not appear on operating budgets? What is the true debt service capacity of the unified tax base, and are there refinancing opportunities? [Analyst, Accountant]

11. **Property tax equalization and taxpayer impact**: If one district has a strong commercial or high-value tax base ($120M per student) while another relies on residential agricultural property ($50M per student), how does consolidation change tax rates for each community? At what point does tax resistance from previously "low-tax" towns threaten voter approval, and should the plan include phased equalization or revenue-neutral transition periods? [Analyst, Accountant]

12. **Hidden ongoing costs that scale with size**: What costs may scale up or remain hidden in transition — longer bus routes increasing fuel and maintenance, expanded IT support for a unified system, salary leveling upward as three contracts reconcile to the highest scale, and central office overhead? Are there diseconomies of scale that reverse beyond a certain size? [Accountant, Devil's Advocate, Questioner]

13. **Diversified funding beyond per-pupil formulas**: If enrollment-driven state funding is structurally declining, the consolidated district remains vulnerable to the same demographic headwinds at larger scale. What supplementary revenue streams could diversify the funding model — county-wide sales tax earmarks, state consolidation grants, partnerships with community colleges for shared facility use, leasing unused school space to municipal or nonprofit partners? [Accountant, Futurist]

## Topic Cluster 03: Governance, Representation, and Decision-Making

14. **Unified board representation structure**: If the unified board has, say, 9 members, what allocation formula ensures fair representation across towns with different populations while preventing any single town from being perpetually outvoted on decisions affecting its schools? What mix of geographic seats, at-large seats, and appointed vs. elected members gives each former district sufficient voice without creating veto points and gridlock? [Analyst, Politician]

15. **Governance innovations that build trust**: Voter approval requires trust that local interests will not be drowned out by a larger system. What governance innovations — weighted board representation, town-level advisory councils with real authority, participatory budgeting at the school level, community-school compacts — could make consolidated governance feel more locally responsive, not less? What structures from watershed districts, conservation easements, or regional transportation authorities balance local voice with resource efficiency? [Appreciative Inquirer, Connector, Politician]

16. **Alternative governance structures beyond one unified district**: What alternatives exist beyond a single unified district: a confederation model where local school boards retain authority over certain decisions while coordinating regionally; a "lead district" model where one district provides administrative services to others; or functional consolidation where specific departments merge independently? [Questioner, Systems Thinker]

17. **Facility closure decision-making governance**: How can the facility closure decision-making process be structured to give affected communities genuine input and influence over the outcome without creating a de facto veto that would prevent necessary consolidation-related closures? What decision framework should prioritize competing objectives when they conflict (e.g., closing the oldest building saves money but devastates town identity)? [Politician, Analyst]

18. **Genuine vs. predetermined participation**: How do people experience "being heard" in a process that feels predetermined — where the feasibility study, state pressure, and fiscal deadlines signal consolidation is already decided? What would make residents believe their input actually matters, and what would a proposal so compelling that opposition would be irrational (rather than a communication failure) look like? [Empath, First Principles Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 04: Political Feasibility and Coalition-Building

19. **Mapping political feasibility across three simultaneous votes**: Since all three districts must approve, what is the coalition-building strategy? Does this require simultaneous wins in all three districts, or can the proposal be structured so each district sees a net benefit that outweighs its losses? What are the minimum vote thresholds and swing-voter compositions needed in each district? [Analyst, Politician]

20. **Red lines and concessions for each stakeholder**: What specific conditions would cause each stakeholder group (teachers' unions, parents, fiscal conservatives, state officials) to oppose consolidation, and can those conditions be modeled as red lines that any proposal must avoid crossing? What minimum guarantees (facility commitments, tax rate caps, board representation) are needed to build a winning coalition in each district? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Politician]

21. **Sequencing endorsements and tailoring messages**: Which stakeholders should be approached first for public endorsement to create momentum, and how does their support unlock other groups? How should the consolidation case be framed differently for fiscal conservatives, parents, teachers, and town-identity advocates, and what specific fears must each message address? [Politician]

22. **Informal power centers and trusted messengers**: Beyond the official school board and district administration, who holds informal influence in each community (town officials, local business owners, respected long-time families, media voices) that could derail or advance consolidation? Who are the natural allies — parents who have experienced successful regional programs, teachers who have collaborated across districts, students who have participated in cross-district activities — who could anchor a broader coalition? [Appreciative Inquirer, Politician]

23. **Anti-outsider sentiment and unrelated grievances**: Rural communities often view their schools as symbols of local autonomy, and consolidation may be framed as state overreach or urban imposition. The vote could also become a referendum on unrelated issues (taxes, state mandates, board decisions). How do we map the political landscape to pre-empt anti-outsider sentiment and assess community sentiment through targeted polling and focus groups before a vote? [Devil's Advocate, Politician]

24. **Scenarios where opposition coalitions win**: What would need to be true for consolidation to lose rather than gain votes? What specific scenarios — identity backlash, union mobilization, fiscal-conservative revolt — produce a "no" coalition larger and more organized than the consolidation proponents, and what early warning signs would indicate this trajectory? [Questioner, Devil's Advocate]

25. **Reframing the narrative**: Instead of "consolidation" or "merger," what if this was presented as "creating a premier rural education network" or "building a county-wide learning community"? How much of the resistance is to the practical changes versus the terminology and framing of the proposal? [Questioner]

## Topic Cluster 05: Community Identity, Grief, and Emotional Experience

26. **What actually dies when a school closes**: Beyond the building itself, what rituals, gatherings, and informal social glue disappear — and what emotional void does that leave for a town that is already watching its population dwindle? For communities where the school is the only remaining institution that says "we exist as a town," what does taking that away feel like? [Empath, Audience Advocate]

27. **Naming loss before selling gain**: The consolidation conversation keeps framing this as "gaining efficiency" and "creating opportunities," but what if we started by naming what people are actually afraid to lose, then figured out how to carry that forward? How do we acknowledge grief without treating it as an obstacle to be overcome? [Empath]

28. **Town pride, marquee identity, and Friday-night rituals**: Friday night lights, the hometown marquee, the rivalries that gave communities their narrative — when that all gets folded into someone else's identity, what happens to the stories people tell about who they are? What does it feel like to drive past the empty building, attend games in someone else's town, and explain to new residents where "your" school went? [Empath, Audience Advocate]

29. **Winners, losers, and the emotional math across decades**: If one town keeps the high school and another does not, how does that emotional calculus get calculated across decades of local history, and what does that do to the idea of "one unified district"? How might the consolidated district's resource allocation decisions either mitigate or accelerate divergence between "winner" and "loser" towns? [Empath, Systems Thinker, Audience Advocate]

30. **Honoring three histories without erasing any**: Whose version of the past gets preserved in yearbooks, trophy cases, and school names when three districts consolidate? How might a consolidated district actively honor the distinct histories of all three communities while creating space for new shared stories to emerge, and what process makes those decisions feel like community development rather than extraction? [Appreciative Inquirer, Audience Advocate]

31. **Surrender vs. practical necessity**: When consolidation is framed as fiscal necessity but feels like losing control, how do we distinguish between the practical reality of insolvency and the visceral experience of being told you can no longer govern your own school? [Empath]

## Topic Cluster 06: Student Experience, Belonging, and Equity

32. **Daily journey and the 1.5-2 lost hours**: For a student who would shift from a 15-minute bus ride to an hour-plus commute each way, how would the lost 1.5-2 hours daily affect their time for homework, family responsibilities, extracurriculars, sleep, and simply being a kid? What would they give up, and what might they gain? [Audience Advocate, Devil's Advocate]

33. **Belonging across merged student bodies**: When students from three distinct school communities merge into consolidated buildings, what concrete experiences would help them form a shared identity rather than feeling like "those kids from [other town]"? What happens during lunch, hallway passing, pep rallies, and those informal moments that shape whether students feel they belong? [Audience Advocate, Devil's Advocate]

34. **Invisible student losses not captured by metrics**: Beyond test scores and finances, what do students lose when a small school closes that does not show up in any metric but profoundly shapes their daily experience — teachers who know their family history, the certainty of making the sports team, the sense that this place is "theirs"? [Audience Advocate]

35. **Special education continuity and IEP delivery**: When IEPs were developed in small districts with specific resources and relationships, how does consolidation affect the delivery of those services, the relationships between families and special education staff, and the continuity of accommodations students rely on? How do longer bus rides affect students with physical disabilities, medical conditions, or sensory needs? [Audience Advocate]

36. **Extracurricular and equity access**: For students whose families cannot drive them to 6 a.m. band practice or late sports pickups, or who cannot afford participation fees that might increase with consolidation, what opportunities quietly disappear? Who gets to be a "full participant" in school life? [Audience Advocate]

37. **Rural realities and quiet exclusion**: For families without reliable internet, who live on unpaved roads that snowplows treat as low priority, or whose parents work jobs that do not allow midday transportation flexibility, what aspects of consolidation might quietly exclude them from full participation in school life? How does consolidation affect language access, translation services, and cultural liaison staff for ELL families? [Audience Advocate]

38. **Student voice in the process**: If we asked students themselves what matters most about their school experience, what would they say that adults rarely consider? How might their priorities differ from the financial and logistical factors dominating consolidation discussions? [Audience Advocate, Questioner]

## Topic Cluster 07: Educational Quality and Staffing

39. **Measuring educational quality before and after**: Beyond test scores, what metrics — class-size variability, course offerings, teacher retention rates, extracurricular participation — should be tracked to determine whether consolidation actually improves or degrades educational outcomes? [Analyst]

40. **Teacher recruitment assumption**: What if a larger district offers less individualized support and more bureaucracy that actually deters some teachers? What specific evidence shows that consolidated districts perform better than small districts in attracting qualified staff to rural areas, and what makes these districts a place where teachers want to build careers? [Questioner, Appreciative Inquirer]

41. **Union contract reconciliation exposure**: If three separate contracts must be harmonized (different salary schedules, benefits, seniority rules), what is the financial exposure — and can we quantify this in ranges (e.g., $500K-$1.5M one-time, $200K-$400K annual)? What are each union's walk-away points and non-negotiable demands, and what leverage do they hold to demand that the highest salary schedule apply to all? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate, Questioner]

42. **Amplifying each district's existing strengths**: Each district has something it does exceptionally well — perhaps one excels at special education inclusion, another at community partnerships, another at fiscal efficiency. What if consolidation were designed around systematically amplifying each district's existing strengths across the whole county? What structures would enable cross-pollination rather than one-size-fits-all uniformity? [Appreciative Inquirer]

## Topic Cluster 08: Facilities, Transportation, and Operations

43. **Transportation optimization problem**: With three districts becoming one larger geographic area, what is the route efficiency analysis — what is the maximum acceptable bus ride time, at what point do transportation costs eat into administrative savings, and how do we factor in rural road conditions and winter weather? Could consolidation paradoxically increase per-pupil transportation costs? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

44. **Systems integration complexity**: Beyond the visible costs, what is the analysis of hidden integration efforts — student information systems, financial software, special education tracking, staff email, credentialing and HR systems — and what is the realistic timeline and cost for each? [Analyst]

45. **Repurposing closed schools as community assets**: When school buildings close, what if the process of deciding which facilities to repurpose became an opportunity to create new community assets — early childhood centers, adult education hubs, co-working spaces, community spaces, revenue-generating partnerships? What would make these decisions feel like community development, not extraction? What if all communities retained "their" building but each specialized (K-3 in one location, 4-6 in another, 7-8 in a third)? [Appreciative Inquirer, Questioner]

46. **Strategic investment vs. across-the-board cuts**: What if the $3-5M in one-time transition costs were structured as investment — energy-efficiency retrofits that reduce operating costs 15-20%, or facility consolidation into modernized buildings that lower long-term maintenance? Could the consolidated district grow into sustainability through strategic capital deployment with 5-10 year payback periods rather than cutting to sustainability? [Accountant]

## Topic Cluster 09: Systems Dynamics and Second-Order Effects

47. **Virtuous vs. vicious feedback loops**: If consolidation improves quality through better resources, it could attract families and further improve quality. But the reverse loop — longer bus rides and loss of identity accelerating out-migration, worsening enrollment decline — could dominate. Which loop is stronger under what conditions, and what determines the tipping point where virtuous cycles flip vicious? [Systems Thinker]

48. **Delayed consequences and early warning indicators**: Financial benefits appear immediately; social costs (community disintegration, student alienation, loss of third-place hubs) compound gradually over 5-10 years. What early warning indicators would reveal whether the system is trending toward resilience or decay before delayed effects become irreversible? [Systems Thinker, Analyst]

49. **Success-to-the-successful divergence between towns**: If one town keeps its school and another does not, the town with the school becomes more attractive, increasing property values and tax base, funding better facilities, attracting more families. Meanwhile the town without its school enters a death spiral. How would the consolidated district's decisions either mitigate or accelerate this compounding divergence? [Systems Thinker]

50. **Cascading economic and social impacts on towns that lose schools**: When a town loses its school, it loses more than education — it loses venues for community meetings, youth sports, voting, town events. Local businesses lose foot traffic; property values decline; tax revenue for town services erodes. What second- and third-order impact assessment would capture these cascades rather than just immediate school-budget effects? [Systems Thinker, Devil's Advocate]

51. **Shifting-the-burden and eroding-goals dynamics**: Consolidation offers a short-term fix, but if the underlying driver is enrollment decline, the relief is temporary — the consolidated district faces the same deficits with less local control and fewer trimmable positions. Meanwhile, "maintaining quality while cutting costs" can quietly erode standards over five years (program freezes, larger classes, fewer course offerings). What guardrails prevent silent standard creep and reliance on consolidation benefits rather than genuine resilience? [Systems Thinker]

52. **Leverage points that fall short of full consolidation**: Systems with leverage points often have small interventions with disproportionate effects. Could shared services for transportation, special education, or professional development capture 60-70% of administrative savings without triggering identity loss? Could "soft consolidation" — unified purchasing, shared superintendent, joint curriculum development — build trust before structural merger? [Systems Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 10: Risk, Reversibility, and Failure Modes

53. **Failure modes and their probabilities**: If consolidation fails financially, politically, or educationally, what are the specific failure scenarios (consolidated district insolvency in year 4, voter rejection, mass teacher departures) and what early warning indicators would signal we are on those trajectories? [Analyst]

54. **Irreversibility and exit options**: District consolidation is effectively irreversible — the legal, political, and financial costs of de-consolidating would be prohibitive. What accountability mechanisms and correction triggers (fiscal audit after 3 years, performance benchmarks, tripwire indicators) should exist before committing? [Devil's Advocate, Analyst]

55. **What if the smallest district's timeline is shorter than projected?**: If the 2-year fund balance could evaporate faster due to unexpected expenses (facility failures, special-ed costs, litigation), does that change the approach from consideration to emergency action? What contingency plans exist if one district fails before consolidation can be implemented? [Questioner]

56. **What if consolidation does not solve the financial problem?**: What if projected savings are smaller than anticipated, new costs emerge, or enrollment declines continue even after consolidation? What is the contingency plan if the consolidated district still faces structural deficits five years post-merger, and how should the district model financial resilience to enrollment volatility beyond 2035 (5-year, 10-year, or 25%-decline worst-case)? [Questioner, Accountant, Systems Thinker]

57. **Transition trap: first-year math vs. decade outcomes**: Most analyses focus on first-year savings and costs, but consolidation is a bet that decade-three economics justify transition pain. What happens when we explicitly model the first two years being worse in every dimension (budget, community trust, educational continuity), and does the fundamental math still work? [First Principles Thinker]

## Topic Cluster 11: Building Confidence Through Experiments

58. **Small-scale experiments that build confidence**: Before asking voters to decide on full consolidation, what smaller-scale experiments — a shared superintendent, combined professional development days, joint extracurricular programs, shared specialty courses — could demonstrate that collaboration works and creates benefits? What would count as evidence of success, and what quick wins could be achieved within the current school year to build momentum? [Appreciative Inquirer]

59. **Peak moments of prior district collaboration**: Tell me about a time in the past decade when two or more of these districts worked together on something that succeeded beyond expectations. What conditions made that collaboration possible, what relationships were already in place, and how could those become the foundation for consolidation rather than casualties of it? [Appreciative Inquirer]

60. **Phasing consolidation to build momentum**: Should consolidation be implemented all at once or in phases (administrative consolidation first, facilities closure second, contract unification third)? Which sequence creates early wins that build stakeholder confidence rather than early disruptions that fuel opposition? Could a federated model — like successful hospital mergers — preserve local branding while centralizing back-office functions invisibly? [Politician, Connector]

## Topic Cluster 12: Long-Horizon and External Forces

61. **Scenario planning for continued enrollment decline beyond 2035**: If the consolidated district loses another 15% (or 25%, 35%, 50%) of students by 2035, what tripwire indicators should trigger another restructure, and how can the district avoid being in this same position a decade from now? What is the long-range plan identifying sustainable minimum scale for rural education? [Analyst, Devil's Advocate]

62. **Remote-work migration reshaping enrollment**: The pandemic accelerated migration from cities to rural areas. If 2035 projections assume continued decline based on pre-2020 trends, what sensitivity analysis accounts for potential remote-work-driven in-migration that could stabilize or reverse enrollment losses? What if enrollment actually increases after consolidation, and does the model have capacity to grow? [Futurist, Questioner]

63. **Aging-population and property-tax dynamics**: Rural populations are aging faster than urban counterparts, and school funding depends heavily on property taxes and voter support from households without school-age children. How might the consolidated district's fiscal position evolve as this demographic shift accelerates through 2035? [Futurist]

64. **State policy as hidden constraint or enabler**: What state policies beyond the funding formula — transportation reimbursement ratios, minimum school-size requirements, curriculum mandates, teacher certification rules, consolidation incentives — shape what is possible? What state-level tweaks would dramatically improve rural district viability without requiring consolidation, and what happens if state formulas change again after consolidation locks in a structural decision based on a temporary policy advantage? [Systems Thinker, Futurist, Questioner]

65. **Positioning for decentralized education delivery**: The documented expansion of virtual charter schools, state online programs, hybrid models, community schools, and regional service cooperatives suggests rural education delivery is decentralizing. How might facility decisions and governance structures preserve option value for flexible scheduling, community learning hubs, and regional partnerships — balancing local accountability with the ability to participate in emerging shared-service models? [Futurist]

66. **Trust deficit and messaging for a skeptical era**: Trust in institutions, including public schools, has declined measurably over the past decade, particularly in rural communities. Given that consolidation requires voter approval, what strategies address this documented trust deficit and the specific skepticism toward centralization and loss of local control? [Futurist]

## Appended Questions (Perspective Personas)

### Provocateur (Append)

1. **What if "school districts" were the wrong unit of analysis entirely?** Imagine if education funding, governance, and enrollment were organized around something completely different — students traveling to learning rather than schools receiving them, teachers as independent contractors serving multiple micro-communities, or education organized around "learning seasons" rather than geographic boundaries. What would become possible if we stopped assuming consolidated districts are the only alternative to fragmented ones? *The insight: we're debating the architecture of a system whose fundamental unit may be obsolete.*

2. **What if the goal was to make consolidation fail spectacularly?** Imagine designing the consolidation process specifically to maximize community outrage, financial waste, and educational disruption. What would you do? Close all buildings simultaneously? Fire everyone and rehire? Auction off assets to private buyers? Now work backward: what does this reveal about which safeguards, pacing decisions, and transition structures actually matter most? *The insight: sometimes disaster planning reveals what success requires more clearly than optimization does.*

3. **What if every student attended a different "school" every week?** Imagine a system where students rotated through all 7 buildings on a rolling basis, where no town had a "local school" but everyone had access to every facility. Or imagine schools were organized around student interests rather than geography — the STEM building, the arts building, the nature school — and transportation became the primary cost instead of duplication. What would this reveal about what "community school" actually means versus what we assume it means? *The insight: we're protecting a concept of local schooling that may already be fiction for rural students facing long commutes anyway.*

4. **What if the districts deliberately made themselves LESS efficient before consolidating?** Imagine if each district, knowing consolidation was coming, accelerated hiring, raised salaries, took on debt, and expanded programs specifically to maximize the "savings" that consolidation would appear to generate. What if consolidation was structured as a rescue rather than a merger? How would the politics and narrative differ if the new district "saved" failing districts rather than "rationalizing" functional ones? *The insight: we're pretending consolidation is about efficiency when it's really about solvency — what if we named that directly?*

5. **What if declining enrollment was framed as an educational asset rather than a crisis?** Imagine a world where smaller student-teacher ratios, individualized attention, and underutilized facilities were positioned as luxury advantages rather than inefficiencies. What if the districts competed to attract students FROM each other using quality and experience rather than fighting against consolidation? What if the problem wasn't "not enough students" but "too much infrastructure designed for a different era"? *The insight: enrollment decline is only a failure if metrics designed for growth are applied to shrinkage — what if we designed for contraction instead?*

6. **What if we planned for the district to be dissolved in 20 years?** Imagine designing the consolidation with an explicit expiration date — create the unified district with the assumption that it will need to radically restructure or cease to exist in 2035. How would facility decisions, contract negotiations, transportation planning, and community engagement change if this were a transition state rather than a permanent solution? What interim structures would you build differently if you knew they were temporary? *The insight: treating consolidation as "forever" locks in decisions that should remain adaptable as conditions continue changing.*

7. **What if consolidation required every student to change schools, not just some?** Rather than closing buildings and displacing students from closed schools, imagine requiring ALL 1,800 students to reassess and re-enroll under a new system with no guaranteed placement. What if "our school is our town" was disrupted for everyone rather than protected for a few? How would the political dynamics and coalition-building change if no community received special protection? *The insight: we're trying to minimize disruption when sometimes equitable disruption creates different political possibilities.*

### Storyteller (Append)

1. **The Last Day at Maple Grove Elementary** Imagine walking through the halls of Maple Grove Elementary on what might be its final day of operation as a neighborhood school. The walls display photos of seven generations of students who learned to read in these classrooms. The principal's grandmother attended this school, as did her mother, and now her own daughter faces the prospect of being bused 45 minutes away. How might a community ritually honor and preserve 90 years of collective memory when a building stops being a school, and what transitional objects or ceremonies could help families carry their school's identity forward rather than experiencing it as an amputation?

2. **When "Consolidation" Arrives on the Doorstep** Picture the morning when Emma, a single mother working two jobs in the smallest district, receives the consolidation proposal in the mail. Her son Marcus has thrived under the personal attention of teachers who've known him since kindergarten—teachers who may now lose their seniority or be reassigned. Emma has no car and relies on the walking-distance school for before-school care and after-school activities while she works. What happens to families like Emma's when the infrastructure of daily life—care, transportation, trusted relationships—must be rebuilt, and what alternative support ecosystems would need to exist to prevent consolidation from becoming an existential crisis for the most vulnerable families?

3. **The New Kid, Again** Consider the experience from Javier's perspective, an eighth grader who has already changed schools three times due to family circumstances. His current school, though small and resource-constrained, has been the one constant where teachers know his learning needs and he's finally found a friend group. Now he faces the prospect of another transition to a consolidated middle school where he'll be one of 400 students instead of 80. What specific scaffolding and continuity bridges could transform this transition from a source of anxiety into an opportunity for expanded horizons, and how might the consolidation process actively identify and support students for whom stability is the foundation of learning?

4. **A Tale of Two School Years** Imagine following two teachers—Ms. Chen from the smallest district, who has taught science with outdated textbooks and no lab equipment, and Mr. Davis from the largest district, who has a fully-equipped lab but dwindling class sizes—as they navigate their first year in a consolidated district. Both have expertise to share and anxieties about losing autonomy. How might their professional journey unfold in ways that create a richer learning environment than either could provide alone, and what narrative frameworks could help them see themselves as collaborators in building something new rather than competitors in a zero-sum reorganization?

5. **The Day the Bus Route Changed** Picture a Tuesday morning six months after consolidation, when students from three formerly separate communities step off buses at what was once "their rival's" high school. Some arrive excited, others resentful, many anxious. The cafeteria now serves students who grew up hearing each other described as "those people from across the county." What happens in that first week, first semester, first year to weave these separate identities into something that doesn't erase differences but creates new shared experiences—through joint traditions, collaborative projects, spaces that honor all three communities' histories—and what role might students themselves play in authoring this new chapter rather than having it written for them?

6. **Who Gets to Tell the History?** When three districts consolidate, whose version of the past gets preserved in yearbooks, trophy cases, and school names? The current proposal suggests keeping one high school open, converting another to a middle school, and closing a third entirely. Students and alumni from the closed high school will see their championship banners, theater productions, and commencement rituals subsumed under another institution's identity. How might a consolidated district actively honor the distinct histories of all three communities while creating space for new shared stories to emerge, and what processes could involve current students in designing this layered identity rather than having it imposed by adults who made the consolidation decisions?

7. **The Wednesday Board Meeting** Visualize the pivotal school board meeting where the consolidation vote will take place. The room holds teachers worried about their contracts, administrators whose jobs may disappear, parents convinced consolidation will save their children's education, and grandparents certain it will destroy their town's soul. Outside, television trucks gather. The vote requires majority approval in all three districts, meaning any single community can veto the entire plan. What happens in that room—and the community conversations leading up to it—to move people from "defending our territory" to "imagine what we could build together," and what narratives about shared risk and mutual benefit might overcome the instinct to protect what's familiar even when familiar is no longer sustainable?

### Visionary (Append)
