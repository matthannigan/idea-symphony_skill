# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Short-Term Rental Property Management Company

## Cluster 1: The Long-Term Renter's Experience

**Who is getting squeezed out, and can you see them?**: The community context describes "long-term renters concerned about housing costs" as opponents of STR expansion. From these residents' perspectives, a professionally managed STR operation may feel indistinguishable from the absentee landlords they already resent — it just makes the displacement machine run more efficiently. What does the company's model actually look like to a renter who got priced out of a neighborhood where your managed properties operate? Is there any part of the business design that acknowledges this harm, let alone addresses it?

**What does a displaced neighbor actually experience in the moments that matter?**: Empathy mapping this: a long-term tenant learns their building's owner is converting to STR. They feel anxiety, powerlessness, maybe anger. They encounter a professionally branded property management company as the intermediary between them and that decision. What do they think, feel, say, and do at that moment? Does the company's "good neighbor" positioning engage with this person's reality at all, or does it primarily address the concerns of quieter neighbors worried about parking?

**Are we designing for the community or with it?**: The plan mentions positioning as a "good neighbor" operation. But who defines "good neighbor" — the company, or the people affected by its existence? What meaningful mechanisms would allow long-term residents and housing advocates to shape how this company operates in their neighborhoods, rather than simply managing their complaints after the fact?

## Cluster 2: The Guest's Actual Journey

**What does a first-time guest experience when something goes wrong at 11pm?**: The service promise is full-service management including guest communication. But map the emotional journey of a guest who arrives to a broken A/C in July, or a cleaning crew that didn't finish, or a lockbox that won't open. They feel frustrated, then anxious, then increasingly helpless if the local contact is slow to respond. Where exactly in this journey does the 30-minute response requirement actually kick in — and does the company's operational model reliably deliver in the moments guests need it most?

**Who is the guest that the model is not designed for?**: Full-service STR management optimizes for the "ideal" guest — tech-literate, credit-card-holding, English-speaking, able to navigate Airbnb's interface and the property's smart-home entry system. What about guests who are elderly, have disabilities that make self-check-in difficult, don't have smartphones, or need communication in languages other than English? Are these guests systematically excluded from the properties this company manages, and if so, is that a deliberate choice or an unconsidered default?

**What do guests say they want versus what they actually struggle with?**: Guest reviews on Airbnb surface complaints guests were willing to articulate — but what about the friction they silently worked around? The guest who couldn't figure out the thermostat and just suffered, the one who didn't know they could request a late checkout, the one who felt uncomfortable asking about the neighborhood because they didn't know whom to ask. What would a systematic effort to surface unarticulated guest friction reveal about the actual experience the company is delivering?

## Cluster 3: The Property Owner as Client and Power Holder

**What does a property owner experience when trust breaks down?**: Property owners are entrusting their investment — often a significant portion of their net worth — to a company they don't yet know. Map the emotional journey: excitement at the revenue promise, anxiety at the first guest complaint, then the moment they suspect the management company isn't performing. What do they think, feel, say, and do? What does the contract design communicate to an owner who is already worried about handing over control?

**Who among property owners is this model not built for?**: A full-service management model at a percentage of revenue works well for owners with multiple investment properties or high-value listings. But what about the owner whose STR is their primary income during illness, or the retiree managing a single property they can't afford to lose to a bad month, or the owner whose property sits in a neighborhood zone where permit limits leave their income uncertain? Are the contract terms and fee structures accessible and fair to the most financially vulnerable property owners, or only to those who can absorb volatility?

**What power do property owners actually have when they disagree?**: Contract design includes termination clauses and liability allocation. From the property owner's perspective: if they have a dispute with the management company, what leverage do they realistically have? Do they understand — before signing — what they are agreeing to regarding liability, indemnification, and their ability to exit? Or does the information asymmetry between a professional management company and an individual property owner mean the contract relationship is structurally one-sided in ways owners only discover when something goes wrong?

## Cluster 4: The Surrounding Community and Its Unheard Voices

**Which neighbors are most affected and least represented?**: STR community opposition tends to be dominated by organized homeowners with time and resources to attend city council meetings. But the community also includes renters in adjacent units who bear the noise and parking impacts directly, workers in the gig economy who clean these properties, and service workers whose housing options shrink as STR density grows. Are any of these less-organized voices part of how the company thinks about its community relationships, or is "community engagement" primarily about managing the homeowners and HOA boards who have formal power?

**What does the local cleaning and maintenance workforce actually experience?**: The service model depends on a cleaning and maintenance workforce that is often low-wage, frequently gig-economy or contractor-classified, and disproportionately composed of immigrants and people of color. What is their experience of working for this company? Do they have reliable income, safe working conditions, access to benefits, and a way to raise concerns without fear of losing work? The "good neighbor" brand is built on their labor — does it extend to them?

**What is the experience of a neighbor who just wants a quiet block?**: Beyond the organized opposition, there are neighbors who simply want to know: who is accountable when something goes wrong next door? They experience a parade of strangers, noise on weekend nights, and uncertainty about who to call. The 30-minute local contact requirement addresses liability and regulation — but does the company have a way for an ordinary neighbor to reach a real person who will actually solve the problem? What does that neighbor's experience look like at 1am on a Saturday?

## Cluster 5: Power, Voice, and Long-Term Accountability

**Who has the least voice in how this company operates, and what would it take to change that?**: The company will interact with at least five distinct groups: property owners, guests, long-term residents, the cleaning/maintenance workforce, and local government. Each has different levels of formal power and informal leverage. Which group can least afford a bad outcome and has the fewest mechanisms to hold the company accountable? Does the business design include any structures — grievance processes, community advisory input, public transparency — that give these groups meaningful voice beyond filing a complaint with the city?

**What happens to the communities this company operates in if it succeeds at scale?**: The three-year target is 30-50 properties in a market of ~800 active STRs. At that scale, this company could represent a meaningful share of the city's STR inventory. The people we serve — guests, owners — benefit directly from that growth. But the people affected — long-term residents, housing-cost-burdened renters — may experience it as acceleration of a trend that is already harming them. Is there a version of success that the company could articulate that takes seriously the interests of the people it does not directly serve?
