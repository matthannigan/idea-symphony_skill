# Topic Cluster 99: Additional Questions

## Questions

1. **Imagine this company succeeds beyond anyone's wildest expectations, and then becomes the thing everyone hates — what went wrong?**: By year five you have 300 properties and are the dominant operator in the city. Walk backward from that moment. What did success require that planted the seeds of backlash? The insight: growth trajectories that look like wins at 30 properties may become liabilities at 300 — the company needs to design for the version of itself it doesn't want to become.
2. **Operating like a two-sided marketplace that must satisfy both sides simultaneously**: Airbnb itself solved (imperfectly) the problem of earning simultaneous trust from hosts and guests who have adversarial interests in some disputes. This company faces a similar double-trust problem with property owners (who want revenue maximization) and neighbors/regulators (who want noise minimization and housing preservation). Which platform design patterns — transparent review systems, verifiable commitments, third-party audits — could the company borrow to make its "good neighbor" positioning credible to skeptics rather than just asserted in marketing copy?
3. **Acting like a guild protecting a shared commons from overexploitation**: Medieval craft guilds enforced quality standards not just for competitive reasons but because one bad practitioner's output degraded the reputation of the entire trade and invited external regulation. The STR industry in this city is experiencing exactly this dynamic — a few badly managed properties are driving the ordinance. How could the company actively participate in raising minimum standards across the local STR ecosystem (by working with the city on best-practice frameworks, training other operators, or helping draft reasonable regulations) in ways that simultaneously serve the public interest and cement the company's position as the credible, preferred operator?
