# Liability, Insurance, and Contract Design - The Lawyer

---

## Liability Shield and Entity Structure

* **Separate property-specific LLCs with hold-harmless agreements.** Create a distinct LLC for the management company itself, but more importantly, require each owner to maintain title in their own entity (even a single-property LLC) and require that owner-entity to indemnify the management company for on-property incidents. This creates two barriers: the management company is never the property owner (reducing direct negligence claims), and the owner's indemnification obligation makes it financially painful for them to ignore maintenance or safety. The operating agreement with owners should explicitly state that owner entities remain responsible for structural defects, code violations existing before the management relationship, and any violations the owner knowingly overrides against company recommendation.

* **General liability policy naming the management company as additional insured, with express carve-outs for owner negligence.** A single management-company LLC limits personal liability for founders, but the real protection comes from the insurance architecture (see next question). The entity structure merely prevents piercing arguments; the policy language does the heavy lifting. Run this by a broker who writes STR management coverage, because standard GL policies often exclude short-term rental activity or include sub-limits for rental properties.

* **Indemnification structured as "we reimburse owner for claims we caused, owner reimburses us for claims they caused."** Mutual indemnification (rather than one-way owner indemnity) signals partnership and is more enforceable in court — it shows reasonable risk allocation rather than a liability dump. Specify the triggers: management company indemnifies for contractor negligence during scheduled maintenance or company-arranged cleaning; owner indemnifies for pre-existing code violations, guest incidents arising from owner-specified (and overridden) maintenance deferrals, or damage caused by owner's personal use of the property.

* **Carve-out for catastrophic loss where neither party bears full cost.** A guest seriously injured in a fall due to owner-deferred stair repair, when no insurance covers it, will result in a judgment that indemnification cannot satisfy. Cap mutual indemnification at the insurance policy limits; beyond that, both parties absorb proportional loss. This incentivizes the owner to buy adequate insurance rather than betting that liability won't materialize.

## Insurance Architecture Across Three Concurrent Risk Layers

* **The largest uninsured gap is standard homeowner policy exclusion of STR activity.** Most homeowner policies require the property to be occupied by the owner or long-term tenant; STR activity constitutes a material change in use that voids coverage. A coastal-city slip-and-fall ($50K–$150K typical defense + damages) is often uninsured unless the owner has explicitly obtained STR-specific dwelling coverage. Require owners to provide proof of an STR-endorsement homeowner/dwelling policy naming the management company as additional insured before the first booking, with annual renewal verification. If the owner refuses, the company must decide: increase the fee to cover the uninsured gap, decline the property, or require the owner to self-insure.

* **Host guarantee programs (Airbnb Host Guarantee, VRBO protection) cover guest incidents up to ~$1M but exclude contractual liability and certain negligence claims.** The coverage also only applies to incidents hosted through that platform; a guest booked directly and injured on-property is outside coverage. The management company should document which platform each property is listed on, confirm that the owner's profile qualifies for the guarantee, and clarify that neither the guarantee nor the company's GL policy covers indemnification disputes between management company and owner. The gap: when an owner's own negligence (failure to fix a known stair hazard) combines with the guest incident, the platform guarantee may deny coverage based on "known hazard" exclusions.

* **Management-company errors-and-omissions coverage covers missed maintenance, improper pricing, communication failures, and regulatory missteps — but not bodily injury.** Standard GL covers bodily injury; E&O covers professional liability. For an STR management company, both are essential. E&O will cover situations where the company promised to arrange an inspection but failed to document it, or misapplied occupancy tax, or failed to notice that a property fell out of compliance with the new city ordinance. Minimum should be $1M E&O. Require owners to maintain at least $500K owner-level GL; recommend $1M if the property has any guest-safety exposure (pools, hot tubs, steep stairs, waterfront access).

* **Chain-of-coverage audit as quarterly compliance ritual.** The company should maintain a matrix: each property, listed platforms, owner's insurance policy details, expiration dates, additional-insured status, deductibles, and any exclusions. Three months before expiration, remind the owner. If renewal documents are not provided 30 days before expiration, suspend new bookings for that property until proof is received. This is a hygiene cost (20 minutes per property per year) but prevents the catastrophic discovery post-claim that an owner's policy lapsed.

## Termination, Cure, Regulatory-Change Clauses, and Small-Portfolio Runway Risk

* **Regulatory-change termination trigger tied to specific permit/licensing events, not general "regulation tightens."** Define trigger as: (a) owner's property loses STR-use permit due to zone change, neighborhood cap reached, or owner permit revoked for non-compliance; or (b) city prohibition of STR activity in owner's neighborhood where owner had a valid permit as of agreement date. Do not include vague triggers like "STR regulations become unprofitable for owner." This prevents owners from exiting the moment their neighborhood hits the permit cap, while still giving the company and owner a clean exit if the property actually becomes illegal to operate. If state preemption passes and legalizes STR statewide (eliminating local bans), that does not trigger early termination — it's a regulatory *improvement*.

* **Minimum 18–24 month initial term with one-year automatic renewal unless either party provides 90-day notice.** For a 5–10 property portfolio with $80K runway, losing 10–20% of properties unexpectedly is a liquidity crisis. The initial term creates stable projected revenue for year one. The 90-day notice provides owners time to find alternate management or re-manage themselves, and gives the company time to find replacement properties. The auto-renewal is silent — neither party has to "re-agree," reducing friction for relationships that are working.

* **Cure period (30 days) for specific breaches, but not for regulatory disqualification.** If the company misses a maintenance appointment or fails to respond to a guest complaint within the SLA, the owner gets 30 days to notify the company and allow cure before termination. If the owner fails to provide proof of required insurance or commits a material breach (interferes with guest, overrides critical safety recommendations), the company has grounds for immediate termination. This structures incentives: poor performance is curable (shows you take the relationship seriously); broken fundamentals (missing insurance, regulatory disqualification) are not.

* **Onboarding fee of 10–20% of monthly management revenue, non-refundable after first month.** For a $2K/month rental generating $200–400/month in management fees, an onboarding fee of $200–400 backstops the company's cost to integrate the property, negotiate with platforms, and set up compliance. If the owner exits after 4 months, the company has recouped early-out friction. If the owner exits at month 18, the fee is negligible. This is standard in property management (often called "setup fee" or "onboarding fee") and does not poison the relationship if termination is triggered by the owner's regulatory disqualification or material breach.

## What Owner-Partner Agreements Look Like When They Reflect Shared Values

* **Explicit transparency commitments on pricing, guest selection, and incident response.** Rather than burying performance metrics in an appendix, make them the core of the relationship: monthly financial statements showing revenue, management fees, expenses, and net owner payout; quarterly guest review summaries (sentiment analysis, incident reports, feedback themes); semi-annual compliance checkpoints (permit status, occupancy-tax filings, insurance renewal status). A "partner" management company shares this data proactively, not defensively. This also serves legal purposes: documented good faith disclosures and evidence-based incident response protect the company from later claims that it hid information.

* **Communication protocol that signals responsiveness and trust.** Define response-time SLAs (guest inquiry within 2 hours, maintenance request within 24 hours, owner question within 4 business hours) and publish actual compliance monthly. Hold monthly owner calls (even brief 15-minute check-ins) rather than quarterly reviews. Create a shared property dashboard where owners see real-time occupancy, income, and upcoming bookings. These are operational commitments, but they're also relationship signals — they show the owner that the company views the property as the owner's asset, not a revenue line.

* **Owner input on major decisions with documented reasoning if the company overrides owner preference.** When an owner says "I want to block this category of guest" or "I want to reduce pricing in this season," the company should explain the impact on occupancy and revenue, but ultimately honor the owner's choice unless it violates regulatory requirements or creates material liability. If the company overrides the owner (e.g., "we recommend against guest type X due to incident patterns we've observed"), document the recommendation, explain the reasoning, and if an incident occurs, show that the company flagged the risk. This is defensive, but it also demonstrates respect for owner autonomy — the opposite of a vendor relationship.

* **Annual owner-company true-up agreement.** Once per year, owner and company sit down (or exchange detailed written responses) to discuss: What worked? What didn't? Are the fee structure and terms still aligned with both parties' interests? This is not a renegotiation trigger, but an explicit recognition that the relationship evolves and that both parties' values need to stay aligned. If an owner consistently wants more hands-off management and is comfortable with lower occupancy, the company might adjust service levels. If the company realizes a property's profile doesn't fit its risk tolerance, that comes out now, not at renewal.

## Negotiating Owner Control Like a Franchise Agreement

* **Management company owns pricing and guest-vetting decisions; owner retains override rights with documented consequences.** The franchisor model works because the brand controls consistency. The management company should own the dynamic pricing algorithm (adjusting for seasonal demand, competitor rates, occupancy patterns) and guest screening (background checks, review history, cancellation patterns). The owner can request price adjustments (seasonal caps, discounts for long-term guests) and can veto a specific guest, but the company documents each override, tracks its financial impact, and surfaces that impact in the monthly report. If the owner's pricing constraints reduce occupancy by 15%, that's data the owner should see and own responsibility for.

* **Company owns operational standards (cleaning, maintenance, guest communication protocols); owner owns property rules and decor policies.** The company determines how often units are cleaned between guests, what maintenance gets deferred vs. prioritized, and what the response time for guest requests should be. The owner determines occupancy restrictions (no parties, pets allowed/not allowed, quiet hours), house rules, and aesthetic preferences (color palette, furnishings, wifi password). This division creates scalability: the company can enforce consistent operational standards across dozens of properties, while owners retain autonomy over their property's personality.

* **Poor review or guest incident resulting from owner override triggers a "decision log" entry, not a contract breach.** If an owner insists on accepting a guest with a poor review history, and that guest causes damage or issues, the company logs the owner's override decision, the company's recommendation, the actual outcome, and fee implications (owner may owe for excess cleaning or damage). This is not punishment, but accountability — it creates clear visibility that the owner made a discretionary choice with known risks. Over time, patterns emerge: some owners make good overrides; others consistently make poor ones. This data informs renewal conversations.

* **Escalation path for owner-company disagreement without contract blow-up.** Define three tiers: (1) operational questions (guest vetting, pricing) are decided by company, owner gets monthly transparency; (2) strategic questions (seasonal strategy, marketing positioning) are decided jointly, with escalation to a written decision memo if they disagree; (3) values/regulatory questions (can we market to party groups, should we include pet fees) are flagged for joint resolution with a 15-day discussion window. This prevents the owner from experiencing the company as unilaterally controlling, while preventing the company from being held hostage by owner micro-decisions.
