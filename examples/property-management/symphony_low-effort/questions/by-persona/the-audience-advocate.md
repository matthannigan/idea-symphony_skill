# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Short-Term Rental Property Management Company

## Cluster 1: The Guest Experience — Who Is Actually Staying Here?

**Who are the guests this company will actually serve, and how do their needs shape every operational decision?**: Short-term rental guests are not a monolithic audience — they include families with young children, travelers with disabilities, non-English speakers, digital nomads, and last-minute bookers. From the community's perspective of guests, what does a genuinely accessible, welcoming experience look like across all of these profiles, and how does the company design its communication, listing content, and in-stay support to meet people where they are rather than where it's convenient?

**What do guests experience emotionally when something goes wrong — and how does the company's response design reflect that?**: A guest who arrives to find a broken A/C in August or a dirty bathroom has already made decisions about transportation, childcare, and work leave. The people being served might experience this as a crisis, not an inconvenience. How does the company map the emotional arc of a guest problem — from discovery to resolution — and what concrete commitments (response time, remedies, communication) reflect genuine accountability rather than damage control?

**How are guests with accessibility needs considered in property onboarding and listing design?**: Properties managed under this company may never have been audited for accessibility. If a guest who uses a wheelchair or has a visual impairment books a stay based on listing descriptions that don't reflect physical realities, the experience gap is not just disappointing — it can be harmful. What process does the company use to accurately represent accessibility features (or limitations) in every listing, and how does it handle inquiries from guests with specific access needs?

## Cluster 2: Property Owners as Clients — Whose Trust Is Actually Being Built?

**What does "building trust with property owners" actually mean from their perspective, not the company's?**: The founders frame owner trust as a goal, but trust is earned through the owner's experience — not the company's intention. What are the moments in the owner relationship where trust is most fragile (first maintenance decision, first guest complaint, first revenue shortfall)? How does the company design those moments to genuinely serve owners' interests, including owners who may be less financially sophisticated or less familiar with the STR market?

**How does the company serve property owners who depend on rental income as a primary financial lifeline, not just supplemental income?**: Not every owner is an investor with diversification. Some may be relying on rental income to cover a mortgage or care for a family member. The people being served in this case need reliability and transparency more than optimization. What structures — reporting cadence, payment timing, variance explanations — does the company put in place to respect the financial vulnerability of owners who are deeply dependent on consistent performance?

**What happens when the company's interests and a property owner's interests diverge — and who decides?**: Revenue optimization, pricing algorithms, and booking velocity serve the company's scale goals, but a specific owner may prefer fewer, longer-term guests, or may want to limit bookings during certain periods. From the audience's perspective, how does the company honor individual owner preferences when they conflict with portfolio-wide management logic, and what recourse does an owner have if they feel their priorities are being overridden?

## Cluster 3: The Surrounding Community — Neighbors, Long-Term Renters, and Local Residents

**How does the company design a "good neighbor" operation in practice, not just as a brand position?**: From the community's perspective, the difference between a responsible STR operator and an absentee one is felt in small, daily experiences — noise after 10pm, parking blocked in driveways, trash bins left out. What specific, accountable operational practices (noise monitoring response protocols, guest conduct briefings, neighborhood contact follow-through) translate the "good neighbor" framing from marketing language into lived experience for people who share streets with managed properties?

**What is the company's responsibility to the long-term renters who are losing housing options due to STR conversion, and how does it reckon with that tension?**: The people most harmed by STR expansion in coastal cities are often renters who face higher rents or displacement as properties shift from long-term to short-term use. The company will directly manage units that might otherwise be available to long-term tenants. How does the company understand its role in this dynamic — does it have any obligations to the community beyond legal compliance, and what, if anything, does it do differently because of that?

**How does the company create channels for neighbor feedback that go beyond complaint lines — to genuine participation in how properties are managed?**: Asking neighbors to "call if there's a problem" puts the burden entirely on the affected party and only captures the most acute friction. The people most impacted by nearby STRs rarely feel heard until a crisis escalates. What proactive mechanisms — neighborhood liaison relationships, regular check-ins, HOA board engagement — does the company build to surface concerns before they become conflicts, and what authority do those neighbors actually have in shaping how properties operate?

## Cluster 4: Regulatory Compliance as a Community Relationship, Not Just a Legal Checkbox

**How does the company communicate to property owners and guests about the local ordinance in ways that are actually understood, not just legally disclosed?**: Permit requirements, occupancy tax obligations, noise monitoring systems, and local contact rules are complex. Property owners signing management agreements may not fully grasp what they're agreeing to comply with. What does the company do to ensure genuine informed consent — not just signature — from owners, and how does it explain guest conduct rules in ways that land with people across literacy levels and languages?

**When the regulatory landscape shifts — as it likely will with the upcoming city council election — how does the company protect the stakeholders most vulnerable to sudden rule changes?**: Property owners in neighborhoods with permit caps could lose the right to operate. Guests with existing bookings could find their reservations canceled. Long-term renters in the area could face further market disruption. How does the company scenario-plan across these possibilities, and what commitments does it make to different audiences — owners, guests, neighbors — to minimize harm when the rules change in ways nobody controls?
