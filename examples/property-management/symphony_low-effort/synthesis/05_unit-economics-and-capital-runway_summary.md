# Summary: Unit Economics and Capital Runway

## Executive Summary

The cluster's organizing tension runs through all three questions: the percentage-of-revenue model transfers occupancy risk entirely onto the founders, who absorb every fixed cost through a shoulder season that earns them nothing, while property owners bear no downside. A feasibility-oriented case argues that a hybrid structure (a $150–200/property/month base retainer plus a reduced 15% performance fee) is viable and actively sellable if framed as "skin in the game from both sides." It can be piloted on the first three-owner cohort before being adopted as the full market position. A risk-oriented counterpoint flags that this logic assumes the company is operating in a market where price is not the primary decision factor. That assumption has not yet been tested against actual owner interviews.

Both lenses agree on the arithmetic underneath: pre-revenue fixed costs are likely to land between $30,000–40,000 of the $80K capital base before a single booking closes, permit processing timelines in a newly regulated city may run longer than 90 days, and break-even requires more properties than founders typically assume. Where they diverge is on what to do about it. A feasibility-oriented view maps a clear sequencing: front-load legal entity, insurance, and local-contact coverage at roughly $15K, then deploy the remaining $65K against owner acquisition rather than brand building, and hold a three-month fixed-cost reserve as a hard constraint. A risk-oriented view presses on the structural assumptions underneath that plan. Founders' unpaid labor has a survival threshold. Permit denials can vaporize cohort revenue before it starts. Concentration risk at early scale is not just a phase the company passes through; it's a structural feature that re-emerges even at 30 properties if high-value owners are also the most likely to comparison-shop competitors.

On churn, both lenses converge on the core mechanism: proactive check-ins surface dissatisfaction before it becomes a public grievance. They diverge sharply on non-disparagement clauses. A feasibility-oriented view treats mutual non-disparagement as a standard professional protection to include in contracts. A risk-oriented counterpoint identifies this as potentially unenforceable in many jurisdictions and argues that an unenforceable clause signals the company is more concerned with managing its reputation than improving its service.

## Key Themes

### Break-Even Is Higher and Further Away Than the Plan Assumes

Both lenses converge on this: effective per-property revenue contribution after channel fees, credit card processing, and owner accounting overhead is closer to $350–500/month than the headline $600 that a 20% fee on $3,000 in gross bookings implies. Fixed costs at maturity require 13–33 properties to cover depending on the fee structure and occupancy rate modeled. This is a convergent finding with high confidence — the range reflects different assumptions, not a genuine disagreement between the lenses.

### The Revenue Model Is Also a Risk-Allocation Decision

The percentage-of-revenue model is not merely a pricing choice; it decides who absorbs occupancy risk. Both lenses agree this matters, but from opposite directions: a feasibility-oriented view frames the hybrid as a practical stabilizer that owners can be sold on, while a risk-oriented view frames the pure revenue-share model as structurally equivalent to running a restaurant where the chef only gets paid when tables are full. The inverted framing is the cluster's most load-bearing tension. For one lens, the fee structure is the problem; for the other, it's the solution vector.

### Churn Arithmetic Must Be Built Into the Growth Model

Both lenses agree that a growth target of 30–50 properties in three years embeds an unstated churn rate assumption. A risk-oriented view makes the math explicit: at 20% annual churn, reaching 40 properties requires signing 60–70 owners over three years, which is a substantially larger sales motion than two founders-as-operators can probably execute alongside day-to-day management. A feasibility-oriented view agrees on the sales-and-retention simultaneity but focuses on the operational precondition — playbooks for owner onboarding must be written at property five, not property twenty.

### Service Metrics That Predict Renewal Are Not the Ones Being Tracked

Both lenses identify owner communication quality — inquiry response time, statement accuracy, how the first difficult guest incident is handled — as the actual churn driver, distinct from occupancy rate and revenue per property. This is a convergent point: founders who instrument around revenue metrics while neglecting owner-experience metrics will accumulate churn they don't understand until it's already compounded.

### Regulatory Calendar Collides With Seasonal Cash Trough

A risk-oriented view uniquely surfaces that permit renewal cycles, noise-monitoring maintenance, and occupancy tax remittances cluster at the same calendar moments as seasonal revenue lows. January renewals coincide with January low season. This operational timing hazard is absent from the feasibility-oriented analysis, which focuses on vendor payment terms (net-30 with cleaning contractors) as the float mitigation lever.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)

- Before signing the first owner, obtain two insurance quotes for GL and E&O coverage and build a line-item pre-revenue budget (not a narrative estimate) that sequences the $80K against entity formation (~$15K), insurance (~$8–15K), PMS software, and operating reserve. Stress-test at two permit-processing scenarios: 90 days and 150 days to first revenue. [convergent]
- Set a cash-reserve floor — operating account must hold at least three months of fixed overhead (roughly $13,500–16,500 at projected burn rates) at all times, with a documented spend-freeze trigger if the account drops below that threshold. [convergent]
- Model break-even at three churn scenarios (10%, 20%, 30% annual) to derive the annual new-owner acquisition rate required at each — then pressure-test whether two founders can execute that sales motion alongside operations. [unique: risk]

### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Propose the hybrid fee structure (base retainer of $150–200/property/month plus 15% performance fee) to the first three owners explicitly as a pilot, collecting data on owner receptivity before adopting it as the company's full market position. [trade-off: feasibility case that this is sellable if framed as mutual skin-in-the-game; risk-oriented counterpoint that owner price sensitivity must be established empirically before the hybrid is locked in]
- Dedicate shoulder season deliverables (proactive maintenance inspections, permit renewal tracking, listing refresh) so owners have tangible evidence of retainer value in low-occupancy months. [unique: feasibility]
- Map the regulatory compliance calendar (permit renewal dates, noise-monitoring maintenance windows, occupancy tax remittance deadlines) against the monthly occupancy forecast, and identify months where cash outflows and administrative labor peak simultaneously. [unique: risk]
- Negotiate net-30 payment terms with cleaning contractors from contract launch; this single term adjustment can extend effective runway by $8–12K in a slow month without changing the fee structure. [unique: feasibility]

### Long-term (1+ years)

- At 30 properties, audit portfolio concentration: if the top three owners by revenue represent more than 20–25% of total fees, structure new acquisition to reduce that concentration rather than chasing the highest-revenue properties disproportionately. [unique: risk]
- Instrument owner retention around three leading indicators from launch — owner revenue versus pre-engagement baseline, response time to owner inquiries, and maintenance issue close rate — and deliver a quarterly owner report built around those metrics. Survey departing owners systematically to validate or revise these as actual churn predictors. [convergent]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- Effective per-property contribution at realistic coastal occupancy is $350–500/month, not the $600 implied by the 20% headline fee — break-even requires 13+ properties, not the 9–10 often assumed.
- Proactive 30-day and 90-day owner check-ins, and a formal 90-day-before-renewal listening session, are the most cost-effective churn-prevention mechanism available at early scale.
- Owner churn drivers are owner-experience metrics (communication speed, statement accuracy, first-incident handling), not occupancy rate or revenue per property.
- The growth target of 30–50 properties requires simultaneous owner acquisition and retention with documented, repeatable systems by year two.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility case that a hybrid fee structure is viable and sellable ("we both have skin in the game") if piloted with the first cohort before committing; a risk-oriented counterpoint that owner price sensitivity in this specific coastal market is an untested premise that could make any floor fee uncompetitive and must be validated through structured interviews with 10–15 owners before structuring the offer.
- A feasibility case that mutual non-disparagement clauses are standard professional protection and should be included in owner contracts; a risk-oriented counterpoint that such clauses are difficult to enforce in many jurisdictions, create false security, and signal to owners that the company prioritizes reputation management over service improvement. Contract drafting energy is better spent on performance standards, cure periods, and dispute escalation mechanisms.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- Regulatory calendar collision: permit renewals, noise-monitoring maintenance, and occupancy tax remittances cluster in the same low-revenue months. January is both the slowest revenue period and the heaviest administrative-compliance month. [unique: risk]
- Cleaning vendor float gap: platforms hold funds for 30+ days while vendors expect net-15. This cash bridge can extend or compress the effective runway by $8–12K/month. Negotiating net-30 from launch is a high-leverage, low-cost fix. [unique: feasibility]
- Founder survival threshold: if either founder returns to employment at month seven to cover personal expenses, operational capacity halves at the moment the business most needs execution bandwidth. This must be explicitly modeled as a hard milestone, not an afterthought. [unique: risk]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- Equity/access/distributional: neither lens considered how the fee structure interacts with the types of property owners the company will attract. A base retainer structurally favors wealthier owners who can absorb a flat monthly cost regardless of bookings. This may mean the company skews toward higher-value properties and away from the owner demographic most likely to be price-sensitive or most impacted by the new regulatory compliance burden.
- Political-economy/regulatory/institutional: the upcoming city council election in six months, with STR regulation as a key issue, could materially alter the permit cap by zone or the annual renewal process. Neither lens treated the regulatory environment as a dynamic variable rather than a static constraint when modeling capital runway.
