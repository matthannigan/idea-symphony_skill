# Tax Compliance and Insurance Coverage - The Pragmatist

## Q1: Three-Regime Tax Burden and Statutory Remittance Liability

* **Assign liability in the management agreement, not in practice.** The practical starting point is a clear contractual clause making the company the designated collection and remittance agent for occupancy tax, with the owner retaining ultimate statutory liability as permit holder. This is how established property managers operate — the company handles the mechanics, the agreement specifies who bears penalties if remittance fails. Draft this in the initial contract template so it isn't renegotiated property-by-property.

* **Use purpose-built software from day one.** Platforms like Avalara MyLodgeTax or OwnerRez handle multi-jurisdiction occupancy tax calculation and remittance for $20–$50/property/month. At 10 properties this costs $200–500/month — far less than the staff hours required to track filing deadlines across city, county, and state regimes manually. A practical approach: onboard the software before signing the first property, not after compliance problems emerge.

* **Estimate the staffing inflection point at 25–30 properties.** Below that threshold, a part-time bookkeeper plus the tax software handles compliance. Above it, the variance in individual owner tax situations (some have Schedule E losses, some have passive activity rules) creates enough complexity that a dedicated role — or a fractional CPA at $1,500–2,500/month — becomes cost-justified. Model this threshold into year-two projections, not as a surprise.

* **Create a per-property tax compliance checklist at intake.** When onboarding a new property, require the owner to confirm their city registration number, current occupancy tax account status, and whether prior remittances are current. This surfaces inherited liability before you take management responsibility. Practically, a one-page intake form with three checkboxes closes the most common gap.

* **Collect and hold a tax compliance reserve from each property's revenue.** A practical approach: withhold 2–3% of gross booking revenue into a separate escrow-style account per property before distributing owner proceeds. This funds remittance without the cash flow gap that creates late-filing risk during slow months. It also signals to owners that you treat tax compliance as a real operational cost, not an afterthought.

## Q2: Federal Tax Character and 1099 Obligations

* **Understand the 14-day rule before pitching the service.** Under IRC § 280A(g), rental income is excluded from Schedule E — and from deductible expenses — if the property is rented fewer than 15 days per year. That scenario rarely applies to a full-service STR client who wants to maximize occupancy, but the practical risk is an owner who wants to use their unit personally 340+ days a year and rent it out just a little. Be clear in sales conversations: full-service management assumes rental income that will appear on Schedule E.

* **Issue 1099-NEC to contractors, 1099-MISC to owners above $600.** As the entity collecting and disbursing rental proceeds, the company has 1099 obligations for owner distributions that may meet the reportable threshold. A practical approach is to set up payee records in your accounting software (QuickBooks, Wave) with TIN collection at onboarding — treat it as a standard vendor setup step rather than a year-end scramble.

* **Clarify whether the company's management triggers self-employment tax for owners.** If a management company provides substantial services to guests (cleaning, check-in, concierge), the IRS may treat owner income as active business income rather than passive rental income, which means SE tax exposure. Owners need to know this upfront. Your management agreement should include a disclaimer that owners should consult their own tax advisor — this limits your professional liability without requiring you to become a tax practitioner.

* **Distinguish your company's tax situation from the owner's.** The management fee the company earns is ordinary business income subject to SE tax regardless of owner treatment. This is straightforward — the complexity is all on the owner side. To make this feasible, keep these two tax tracks clearly separated in client communications so owners don't conflate their situation with yours.

* **Create a standard year-end tax packet for each owner.** A practical deliverable: a one-page summary per property showing total gross bookings, management fees withheld, occupancy taxes remitted, and net proceeds paid. Owners hand this to their accountants. This positions the company as professionally organized, reduces inbound questions in February, and creates documentation if any tax authority questions the owner's filing.

## Q3: Coverage Gap Between Platform Protection and Company E&O

* **Do not treat AirCover or VRBO host protection as insurance.** These are contractual indemnification programs the platforms administer at their discretion — they have no obligation to defend the company in litigation, and claims are frequently denied or capped below actual loss. A practical approach: document in your owner agreements and internal SOPs that platform protection is supplemental, not primary, so no one is operating under the mistaken assumption that Airbnb covers the company.

* **Build the coverage stack in a specific sequence.** Start with: (1) a commercial general liability policy with STR management listed as a covered business activity — $1M per occurrence, $2M aggregate — ($1,500–2,500/year at 10 properties), then (2) a professional liability / E&O policy covering your management decisions ($1M limit, ~$2,000–3,500/year), then (3) require each owner to maintain their own homeowner's or dwelling policy with an STR endorsement naming the company as additional insured. Umbrella coverage ($1M–2M) adds a practical fourth layer once the company crosses 20+ properties.

* **Audit each insurer's STR management exclusion language before binding.** Many standard GL policies contain professional services exclusions broad enough to exclude STR management decisions. The fix is to either get explicit coverage confirmation in writing or switch to a carrier with STR-specific commercial policies (Proper Insurance, Steadily). Practically, get three quotes before year one and include STR management as a named activity in every policy discussion.

* **Carry E&O from the first managed property, not after the first claim.** Errors and omissions coverage protects against management decisions that cause financial harm to owners — wrong pricing, missed maintenance that causes tenant injury, booking calendar errors leading to double-booking. The premium is modest relative to the exposure, and being named in a co-defendant suit without E&O coverage would exhaust the $80K startup capital quickly. This is a non-negotiable startup cost.

* **Document the coverage stack for each property in a single reference sheet.** A practical approach: maintain a coverage summary per property — owner's policy carrier, limits, STR endorsement status, company additional-insured status, expiration date. Review annually at lease renewal time. This makes coverage gaps visible rather than discovered at claim time.

## Q4: Insurance Requirements as Contract Conditions

* **Make STR endorsement a contract condition precedent, not a nice-to-have.** The management agreement should state clearly: company will not begin management until it receives a certificate of insurance showing a homeowner's or landlord's policy with an STR endorsement, minimum $300K–500K liability, naming the company as additional insured with a 30-day cancellation notice. Practically, this is a one-paragraph addition to the owner agreement and a checkbox on the onboarding intake form.

* **Require waiver of subrogation to protect against owner's insurer suing the company.** Without a waiver of subrogation clause, an owner's insurance carrier that pays a property damage claim can then sue the management company to recover its payout — even if the company was acting within its contractual authority. This protection costs little to request and is standard in commercial property management agreements. Include it in the initial contract template.

* **Build a suspension-of-management trigger tied to lapsed coverage.** The management agreement should include a clause that if owner coverage lapses and is not reinstated within 10 business days of written notice, the company may suspend new bookings and guest check-ins without liability for lost revenue. This gives the company a practical enforcement mechanism without requiring immediate contract termination. Draft the notice template in advance so it can be sent the same day a lapse is discovered.

* **Track owner insurance expiration dates in a shared calendar or CRM.** A practical approach: at onboarding, record the policy expiration date and set an automated reminder 60 days out. Send owners a renewal reminder asking for an updated certificate. At 10 properties this takes 10 minutes per year per property; at 40 properties, systematizing it in a CRM with automated email sequences (HubSpot, Buildium) prevents it from falling through the cracks.

* **Conduct an annual insurance audit with owners.** Once per year — tied to the management agreement renewal — review each owner's coverage, confirm limits remain adequate for current market value, and confirm the company is still listed as additional insured. This keeps coverage current as property values and guest volumes change, and it positions the company as a sophisticated manager rather than one that forgets insurance exists between onboarding and a claim.
