# Unit Economics and Capital Runway - The Pragmatist

## Q1: The $80K Runway Versus Realistic Ramp

* **Front-load the non-negotiables, defer everything else.** Before signing your first owner, you need the LLC, a general liability policy, and your local contact coverage — budget roughly $15K for entity formation, legal review of your management agreement template, and the first year's insurance premiums. That leaves $65K as operating runway. Do not spend on branded vehicle wraps, a physical office, or custom software integrations until you have five properties generating fees.

* **Model the burn rate explicitly before month one.** Fixed monthly costs at launch — insurance installments, PMS software (Guesty or Hostaway runs $200–400/month), a part-time bookkeeper, and your 30-minute contact phone stipend — will likely land between $3,500–5,500/month before you pay yourselves anything. At $4,500/month burn and a 90-day lag to first fee revenue, you spend roughly $13,500 before seeing a dollar. Map that cash flow week by week, not quarter by quarter.

* **Break-even math is simple; commit to the numbers.** A practical approach: assume $2,500 average monthly revenue per property, 20% management fee, yields $500/property/month. At $4,500 fixed overhead, you need nine properties at full occupancy to cover costs. At realistic 70% annual occupancy (coastal seasonal), effective per-property contribution is closer to $350/month — so break-even shifts to 13 properties. That's your north star for year one planning.

* **Sequence capital deployment around owner acquisition, not brand building.** The highest-ROI spend in months 1–6 is anything that closes your first five owners: professional photography for a demo property, a sharp one-page pitch deck showing fee structure and comparable revenue outcomes, and a warm referral network built through one partner's real estate contacts. Skip paid digital advertising until you have case studies to show.

* **Build a 90-day cash reserve as a hard constraint.** A practical approach: once the business account drops below three months of fixed overhead ($13,500 at the numbers above), that triggers a freeze on any discretionary spend — contractor onboarding bonuses, new software trials, conference travel. Discipline around this threshold prevents the slow cash bleed that kills early-stage service businesses before they realize it's happening.

## Q2: Percentage-of-Revenue Model Versus Seasonal Cash Flow

* **Model the worst-case shoulder season before you sign your first contract.** Coastal markets commonly see January–March occupancy drop to 30–40%. On a 10-property portfolio at 35% occupancy averaging $200/night, gross booking revenue is roughly $21,000/month; your 20% fee is $4,200 — barely covering overhead. Run this scenario explicitly in a spreadsheet and confirm you can survive it on $80K before committing to a pure revenue-share model.

* **A hybrid fee structure is more feasible than it sounds to sell.** A practical modification: a $150–200/property/month base retainer (covering the always-on costs — local contact, software seat, regulatory compliance monitoring) plus a 15% performance fee on booking revenue instead of 20% flat. Total owner cost in peak season is similar or lower than competitors; in shoulder season, the owner pays a modest floor rather than zero. Frame it as "we both have skin in the game, but the lights stay on."

* **Test the hybrid model with your first three owners as a pilot.** Rather than restructuring your entire business model before launch, propose the hybrid to your first cohort explicitly as a pilot: "We're testing a structure that aligns our incentives differently — you get a lower performance fee in exchange for a small base." This gives you real data on owner receptivity before you commit to it as your market position.

* **Create a shoulder-season service tier to justify the retainer.** If you're asking owners to pay a base fee during low-occupancy months, they need something for it. A practical approach: dedicate shoulder season to proactive maintenance inspections, permit renewal tracking, and listing refresh (updated photos, revised pricing strategy). Owners see tangible deliverables; you're not just collecting a fee for answering no calls.

* **Negotiate payment timing with your cleaning vendors to match your cash flow.** The biggest variable cost — cleaning — is directly tied to bookings, so it scales with revenue. But if vendors expect net-15 while platforms hold funds for 30+ days, you're bridging a float gap. Negotiate net-30 with cleaning contractors from the start; this single term adjustment can extend your effective runway by $8–12K in a slow month without changing your fee structure at all.

## Q3: Revenue Concentration and Client Churn at Early Scale

* **Acknowledge the concentration risk explicitly; don't model around it.** At five properties, losing one owner is a 20% revenue cut. A practical approach to this reality: target your first five owners across at least three distinct referral sources (one partner's real estate network, one from HOA-adjacent channels, one cold-inbound). Diversifying acquisition channels limits the social-graph scenario where one dissatisfied owner talks to two others in the same building.

* **Build the contract to slow departure, not prevent it.** A 60-day written notice requirement plus a 30-day cure window gives you time to fix a service failure before it becomes a termination. More important: a mutual non-disparagement clause and a structured off-boarding checklist (transferring listing credentials, final settlement of cleaning costs) reduce the chance that a departure becomes a public-facing conflict. Frame these as professional standards, not defensive clauses, when presenting to owners.

* **Identify the three metrics that predict renewal before month six.** Rather than tracking everything, focus on: (1) owner revenue versus pre-engagement baseline — are they earning more? (2) response time to owner inquiries — do they feel heard? (3) maintenance issue close rate — are problems resolved, not just logged? If you can show an owner a quarterly report demonstrating improvement on these three, you've built the case for renewal before they're even thinking about leaving.

* **Build a simple retention check-in into your operating calendar.** A practical approach: 90 days before each owner's annual contract renewal, schedule a 30-minute call specifically to review performance data and ask what's working. This is not a sales call — it's a listening session. Owners who feel proactively managed are far less likely to quietly shop competitors. The cost is a calendar event and 30 minutes; the benefit is early warning on any owner who's drifting.

* **Growth to 30–50 properties requires acquisition and retention working simultaneously.** To reach 50 properties in year three from a year-one base of 10, you need to add roughly 13–15 properties per year while holding churn to under 15% annually. That means your systems — onboarding, reporting, maintenance dispatch — must be documented and repeatable by year two, not improvised property by property. Start writing your internal playbook for owner onboarding at property five, not property twenty.
