# Brainstorming Questions from The Provocateur: Short-Term Rental Property Management Company

---

## The Assumption That You Are Solving the Right Problem

1. **What if the company's real product is political cover, not property management?** What if property owners don't actually want clean sheets and dynamic pricing — they want someone else to be the face when the city council comes calling, and everything else is theater? If that's the hidden transaction, what would a company designed explicitly around blame-absorption and regulatory shield-bearing look like, and what would change about how you price it?

2. **What if compliance is the growth ceiling, not the unlock?** Every instinct in this plan treats regulatory compliance as the thing that lets you operate. But imagine the opposite were true: what if rigorous compliance signals to the city that STR management is a legitimate profession worth regulating more heavily, accelerating restrictions that shrink the addressable market? If getting it right is what triggers the crackdown, what does that change about how visible you make your compliance?

---

## Inverting the Trust Architecture

3. **What if you made it deliberately harder for property owners to hire you?** Right now the model assumes you compete for owners. But here's something absurd — what if a ritualistic, high-barrier onboarding process (property audits, neighbor consent forms, HOA board introductions before signing) made owners feel they'd earned something prestigious rather than just hired a vendor? The real insight: scarcity and selectivity might generate more trust than availability, and a waitlist might be a more powerful marketing tool than a sales call.

4. **What if guests were your actual clients and property owners were the product?** Reverse every assumption about who serves whom. If your loyalty ran to guests — guest satisfaction scores, guest veto power on property standards, guests who could "rate the owner" — how would that flip the power dynamic in ways that actually reduced your liability exposure and produced better properties? The absurd version is a guest-governed platform; the real insight is that centering guest experience as the primary fiduciary duty might be a structural differentiator no competitor has tried.

---

## Absurd Extremes That Reveal Hidden Assumptions About Scale and Permanence

5. **What if you designed the company to be acquired in 18 months and worked backward from that exit?** Not as a goal, but as a thought experiment: what would you build differently if the entire purpose was to make the operation legible, transferable, and irresistible to a regional hotel chain or a national STR platform? The insight: building for acquisition-readiness might produce better documentation, better systems, and better property standards than building for owner satisfaction — and those things compound even if you never sell.

6. **What if every property in your portfolio had a public-facing "community score" visible to neighbors, not just guests?** Imagine the opposite of Airbnb's hidden review system: radical transparency about noise incidents, parking complaints, late-night arrivals, and resolution times — published by address, visible to the block. Absurd and probably legally fraught. But the real insight: proactive neighborhood accountability might be the one move that neutralizes the "absentee management" accusation before the city council election, not because it's good PR, but because it structurally changes your relationship with non-guests.

---

## Collisions Between Unrelated Frames

7. **What if you managed these properties the way a hospital manages infection control — with outbreak protocols, contact tracing, and mandatory incident reporting?** Imagine applying public-health logic to guest turnover: every guest is a vector, every checkout is a sterilization window, every noise complaint is a sentinel event that triggers a formal review. The absurd version is guests signing infection-control waivers. The real insight: the liability exposure in STR management — guest injuries, contractor negligence, property damage — maps almost perfectly onto hospital risk architecture, and the systems hospitals use to document, report, and insulate themselves from liability might be directly transplantable to a property management context that currently treats risk as an afterthought.
