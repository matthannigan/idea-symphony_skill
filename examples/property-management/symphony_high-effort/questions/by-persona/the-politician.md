# Brainstorming Questions from The Politician: Short-Term Rental Property Management Company

---

## Stakeholder Power Map and Council Dynamics

1. **Six-month clock**: With a city council election six months away and STR regulation as a key issue, which incumbent or challenger positions on STR policy are genuinely moveable — and what does your company need to demonstrate before election day to remain a viable operator regardless of who wins?

2. **Blocking and enabling coalitions**: The city has roughly 800 active STR listings, meaning hundreds of property owners have a direct financial stake in the regulatory outcome. How do you build a coalition with organized property owners, tourism businesses, and hospitality employers that gives you political weight at council hearings — without becoming so visibly affiliated with the "STR advocate" camp that you alienate the neighbors and long-term renters whose opposition shapes council votes?

3. **Informal gatekeepers**: Beyond elected officials, who are the informal power brokers in each neighborhood zone — planning commission members, neighborhood association presidents, block club leaders — whose early endorsement or public silence shapes how new STR applications are received, and how do you cultivate those relationships before you need a permit approval?

---

## Regulatory Positioning and Legislative Risk

4. **State preemption as political leverage**: The state legislature is considering preempting local STR bans. How does your company position itself relative to this uncertainty — do you publicly back preemption (risking local council backlash), oppose it (risking alignment with restrictionist neighbors), or develop a third stance that lets you survive either outcome and builds credibility with both tiers of government?

5. **HOA board access strategy**: Several target neighborhoods have HOA boards with their own STR restrictions layered on top of city ordinance. HOA boards are elected bodies with their own politics — board composition, competing factions, upcoming annual meetings. What is the sequence for gaining access to those boards as a legitimizing ally rather than being treated as an adversary trying to circumvent community rules?

6. **Permit scarcity as political risk**: Neighborhood zone permit limits mean that permit slots are a finite, politically allocated resource. When the city issues new permits, who has informal influence over how slots are distributed — staff planners, council members, neighborhood liaisons — and how does early relationship-building with those decision-makers change your odds of securing permits for the properties you want to add?

---

## Community Opposition and Legitimacy Building

7. **Converting opponents, not just neutralizing them**: Long-term renters and noise-affected neighbors are not a monolithic bloc — some are principled opponents of STRs on housing-affordability grounds, some are reacting to specific bad actors, and some are persuadable if they see evidence of a professionally managed, accountable operation. Which segment of the opposition is actually moveable, what do they need to see from your company to shift from active opposition to tolerant neutrality, and how do you create visible proof points before your first permit renewal cycle?

8. **"Good neighbor" as a political product, not just a brand claim**: City ordinance already requires noise monitoring and a local contact within 30 minutes. How do you go beyond minimum compliance to create a track record — documented response times, noise incident logs, guest conduct policies — that functions as political evidence you can present to council members, neighborhood associations, and media when STR regulation heats up?

---

## Coalition Sequencing and Long-Term Political Architecture

9. **Sequencing buy-in for scale**: You are starting with 5–10 properties and targeting 30–50 by year three. Political credibility is cumulative but also fragile — one high-profile incident early on can close doors that took months to open. What is the coalition-building sequence that uses early properties in politically favorable zones to establish a track record, earns the endorsements that unlock harder zones, and positions you as the operator the city wants to partner with rather than regulate against?
