# Foundational Value Proposition and Fee Architecture - The Pragmatist

---

## What Owners Are Actually Buying

* **Regulatory compliance as the irreducible core.** With the new city ordinance demanding 30-minute response times and permit compliance, many owners can't manage this themselves and can't delegate to distant property managers. Focus your positioning on the compliance guarantee — specifically, "we hold the permits, we answer the phone, we keep you legal." Bundle other services, but lead with the thing that creates liability if you get it wrong. Test this with your first five properties: track what actually drove their decision, and you'll find it's not "maximized revenue" but "I can't manage this alone."

* **Peace of mind with financial transparency.** Owners want to know their property is generating the revenue it could generate, not because they want management to vanish, but because they need to trust that fees aren't inflated and money isn't being left on the table. A practical approach: provide monthly dashboards that show (1) actual occupancy rate vs. neighborhood baseline, (2) pricing tier justification (why your price, not higher/lower), and (3) revenue after fees. That transparency becomes your differentiator, and owners see management as a calculator they control, not a black box.

* **Unbundled service as a revenue door.** Some owners may want only compliance + guest communication, not cleaning coordination. Rather than one 20% all-in package, test a modular stack: 8% for compliance/permitting, 5% for revenue optimization/pricing, 3% for guest management, 4% for maintenance coordination. This lets you capture owners at different price points while actually optimizing your own unit economics — you'll see quickly which services have the best margins and which are money-losing obligations.

* **Asset protection, not just revenue.** Inland 40%-occupied properties don't care about 2% more revenue; they care that their asset doesn't deteriorate during slow seasons. Position management as "protecting your investment's condition and resale value," not "maximizing this month's booking rate." This reframes seasonal maintenance spending as essential, not discretionary, and aligns incentives around asset quality.

## Fee Structure As Incentive Design

* **Flat retainer + performance tier for high-volume operators.** A percentage of revenue fails catastrophically at 40% occupancy. A practical redesign: charge a fixed monthly retainer (say, $400–600 per property for compliance + baseline guest communication), then add a 12–15% share of revenue *above* occupancy threshold tiers. For an 85%-occupied beachfront unit, maybe everything counts. For a 40%-occupied inland property, only revenue above 35% occupancy is shared. This keeps you solvent on low performers while staying incentivized on their real upside, and it's transparent enough owners can model it.

* **Revenue-share with occupancy caps and expense guardrails.** The problem with pure percentage-of-revenue is that both parties optimize for short-term bookings at the expense of asset life. A hybrid approach: 18% of revenue (full-service), but capped at 25% year-over-year revenue growth in any property (forces you to optimize pricing, not just turnover), and a separate line for "owner-approved maintenance over $500" that gets flagged and justified. Owners see your incentive is aligned with theirs because you're sharing the cap constraint.

* **Three-tier fee structure based on occupancy distribution.** Properties at 70%+ occupancy get 20% revenue share (you have pricing power, owners are getting revenue-stable). Properties at 50–69% get 15% revenue share plus a small monthly stipend ($300/month) because you're doing more heavy lifting managing idle periods. Properties under 50% get 10% revenue share and you get a $500/month base fee because you're primarily maintaining the asset, not generating turnover. This is implementable now, doesn't require tracking complex metrics, and naturally adjusts your workload allocation.

* **Performance-based variance around a 15% base.** Charge 15% of revenue as your base. Then each quarter, if a property's occupancy improved 5%+ from the previous quarter and revenue grew despite seasonal patterns, you earn an extra 2% that quarter. If occupancy declined despite favorable market conditions, you drop to 12%. This ties your earnings directly to the work you're actually doing and gives owners a real incentive to stay because they see quarters where the market made your life harder, and compensation adjusted fairly.

## Beyond Stewardship

* **Cooperative ownership structure with outcome-sharing.** Rather than charging a percentage, what if owners bought a "share" in the management company itself — $5K buy-in per property, plus you take 50% of net profit after expenses? You own the operation, they own fractional equity. This removes the perverse incentive to stack properties regardless of quality and forces you to care about your reputation because equity holders are long-term partners. You can't afford a bad guest experience because it tanks the co-op's brand and their asset value. Start with your first five properties as a cooperative pilot, document what's actually required (legal, accounting, governance), and you'll have a replicable model.

* **Guest and community co-governance in a three-party trust.** A practical analog: the property is held in a triple-trust relationship where the owner, the management company, and the neighborhood (represented by a local liaison or community council) each hold specific veto rights. Owner controls pricing and property-level decisions. Management controls operational standards and guest screening. Community liaison can veto bookings that violate noise covenants or occupancy rules. This creates accountability beyond just owner-manager contracts and directly addresses the "good neighbor" positioning. Implement this at two properties as a test; it requires more governance overhead, but it neutralizes regulatory risk and community opposition.

* **Outcome-sharing with embedded social metrics.** What if management fees varied not just on revenue but on community impact metrics: guest complaints to the city, neighbor satisfaction scores (surveyed quarterly), local tax contributions, and property appearance? Track these alongside occupancy and revenue, and tie compensation to a weighted score: 40% revenue, 30% guest/neighbor satisfaction, 30% regulatory compliance. This isn't charity; it's risk management. Properties flagged by the city cost you money in regulatory attention. Properties with happy neighbors are less likely to be targeted in the next election cycle. The owner wins because their property's long-term viability improves; you win because you're running a sustainable operation.

* **Pooled insurance and profit-share across portfolio.** Instead of per-property fees, pool all properties and charge owners a fixed annual fee (say, $1,200 per property annually) plus you take 25% of aggregate net profit across the whole portfolio. This creates incentive alignment across your portfolio: you profit more by managing a stable, diverse book than by chasing high-turnover gambles. It also gives you capital to invest in shared resources — a cleaning team you own, a maintenance contractor on retainer — that scale better than per-property outsourcing. Start with five properties to test the model; scale it to thirty.
