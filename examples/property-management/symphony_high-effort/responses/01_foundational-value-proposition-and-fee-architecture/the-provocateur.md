# Foundational Value Proposition and Fee Architecture - The Provocateur

---

## What Owners Are Actually Buying

* **Absurd premise: owners are buying permission to ignore their property.** What if the irreducible thing owners pay for is the right to treat their investment as a passive financial asset without any moral, operational, or reputational responsibility? They want the revenue stream with plausible deniability. The real insight: most owners do want peace of mind, but peace of mind from *what exactly*? Not revenue maximization—they'd verify that weekly. Not compliance—insurance handles liability. What owners actually crave is permission to say "I hired someone, so the neighborhood noise isn't my problem" and have that hold water socially and legally. The full-service bundling exists because it creates exactly that wall. A modular menu might open the market but breaks this psychological contract—owners need to feel they've delegated the *moral weight*, not just the tasks.

* **The real transaction is insurance against guilt.** Owners aren't paying for revenue optimization; they're paying to never see guest reviews that say "the neighbor didn't care about our experience." If you separate cleaning from community stewardship, you're not opening a market—you're forcing owners to choose between feeling profitable and feeling decent. The full-service model works because it buries that choice. Strategic insight: reframe your fee not as percentage of revenue but as a luxury tax on moral disengagement. Owners with conscience pay more because they want that moral burden transferred and verified.

* **What if you're solving the wrong buyer problem?** You've assumed the buyer is the owner, but what if the real paying customer is the municipal government? Cities are suffocating under STR externalities. A property management company could position itself as a *regulatory instrument*—the city pays you per property to handle compliance, neighbor relations, and community integration so tightly that you eliminate the political energy spent fighting STRs. The owner gets a lower fee; the city gets social stability. The insight: owners aren't your primary customer; community stability is. Build that into your fee structure—charge the owner less and invoice local government for externality management.

* **Buyers want time arbitrage, not revenue.** Owners didn't become owners to become property managers—they became owners to have made a decision once and then not think about it for 10 years. Every question you ask them, every decision you need them to make, every problem you escalate to them, you're breaking the contract. The true product is *zero cognitive load*. That's not bundled services; that's you making decisions unilaterally within agreed guardrails. The fee should reflect decision authority, not revenue. Insight: your pricing model should penalize owner involvement, not encourage it—the fewer decisions they make, the lower their fee. Flip the incentive.

* **What if the business model is wrong because owners shouldn't be the ones setting it?** What if instead of asking "what does an owner want," you ask "what do guests, neighbors, and regulators want," and then you charge owners for making *that* reality? Owners want revenue; guests want quality; neighbors want quiet; regulators want compliance. These conflict constantly. Your job isn't to serve the owner—it's to balance these demands and charge owners for the privilege of that being solved. The current model assumes the owner is sovereign and others are problems to manage. Flip it: regulators and neighbors are partly sovereign too, and owners pay for that complexity being navigated. Insight: your value isn't to owners—it's to the whole ecosystem. Price accordingly.

## Fee Structure as Incentive Design

* **Absurd premise: charge a negative fee during seasonal troughs.** What if you paid owners a retainer *during* low-occupancy months because your job—maintaining the property, servicing systems, keeping the regulatory status alive, stewarding community relationships—becomes *more* important when revenue is zero? The absurdity breaks open a real problem: the percentage model is structurally broken for properties that don't perform. It incentivizes you to fire underperforming properties and hire new ones constantly. But that's unstable and burns owner relationships. Strategic insight: break your fee into two components: (1) a base retainer that covers operational costs when occupancy is low, (2) a percentage bonus that kicks in above a threshold. This makes you want to keep and improve underperformers rather than churn them.

* **What if you issued equity instead of taking a percentage?** Owners and managers become co-investors with aligned incentives across 3, 5, and 10-year horizons. You share in property appreciation, not just booking revenue. This eliminates the perverse incentive to extract maximum short-term revenue at the cost of long-term asset degradation, neighborhood relations, or regulatory risk. The constraint: owners would demand governance seats and full transparency. The insight: you'd be forced to build the actual business (profitable, sustainable, community-integrated) instead of optimizing a fee structure. Ownership alignment forces discipline that percentage fees hide.

* **Charge different fees for different owner types, not properties.** What if your fee reflected the *manager burden* the owner creates, not their property's performance? Hands-off owners pay less. Owners who call weekly, second-guess pricing, or create compliance nightmares pay significantly more—because they're increasing your risk and burn. The insight: this inverts the current model's lie (we're incentivizing you to perform) and tells the truth (we're pricing you based on how hard you make our job). It would horrify owners until they realize "if I stay out of it, I save 5 points." Suddenly silence becomes valuable. Your fee becomes a behavioral incentive, not a performance metric.

* **What if the fee was capped at a fixed dollar amount per property, not a percentage?** For the beachfront unit doing $3K/month, you take a capped fee of, say, $500. For the inland property at $800/month, same $500. This defunds the scalability math at first—but it forces you to become efficient in ways percentage models don't. You stop chasing high-revenue properties and instead get good at cost management and process automation for the middle market. The insight: percentage fees hide operational efficiency and scale problems. A capped fee exposes them and forces real innovation. You'd build better software, not just hire more people.

* **What if you took *zero* fee from the owner but charged guests a service fee on booking?** You become a guest-side platform, not an owner-side service. Guests pay for quality verification, responsiveness, problem-solving. Owners pay nothing and get a managed property. This inverts the power dynamic completely—you now serve guests and use that leverage to manage owners. The insight: this eliminates the core misalignment. Owners want revenue; guests want good experiences; you get paid for delivering the latter. It's structurally honest about where value flows and forces you to actually deliver guest satisfaction instead of optimizing hidden owner fees. The constraint: it requires technology and scale to work.

## Beyond Stewardship

* **Absurd premise: the company itself owns nothing; it only orchestrates.** What if you operated as a pure coordination platform where guests, owners, neighbors, and workers all held equity slices and voting power? The owner doesn't employ you; you co-own outcomes together. The guest isn't a customer; they're a stakeholder. Workers aren't contractors; they're members. Regulatory bodies get governance seats. This isn't a business—it's a cooperative. The question breaks: "stewardship" assumes a hierarchical principal-agent relationship. In a cooperative, stewardship *is* mutual. Insight: this model would require legal innovation (benefit corps, multi-stakeholder cooperatives) and would sacrifice speed for legitimacy. In a high-conflict STR environment, legitimacy might be more valuable than velocity. You'd survive a city ban because you're owned by the community, not parasitic to it.

* **What if guests became the long-term stakeholders?** Properties exist in communities where guests visit repeatedly over years. What if you organized around *guest loyalty* and community stability, not owner returns? Hosts become platforms for recurring guest experiences. You'd track guest satisfaction across visits, build neighborhood trust through reliable guest experience, and invest in keeping properties livable and neighborhoods stable. Owners would pay you to participate in this ecosystem, not to maximize their individual bookings. The insight: the current model assumes guests are interchangeable transactional units. What if you optimized for guest relationships and community stability, and monetized through owner confidence in that model? It's a tourism company, not a property company.

* **What if the company was owned entirely by employees and community members, with owners having a vote but not control?** Workers (cleaners, maintenance, support staff) and neighborhood representatives get equity. Owners get returns but not governance. This inverts the incentive: you're not extracting maximum value for owners; you're building a workplace and community that can sustain itself long-term. High turnover, contractor burnout, and neighborhood resentment become business failures, not external costs. The insight: current property management treats workers and community as externalities. A worker and community-owned model makes them core stakeholders. You'd invest in wages, training, and neighborhood relations because the people doing those jobs own the company.

* **What if the company was structured as a public trust managing properties in the public interest?** Like a land trust but for short-term rentals. The company issues bonds, receives tax breaks, and manages properties to maximize *community benefit* (affordable units, local employment, neighborhood stability) rather than owner returns. Owners voluntarily participate knowing lower returns in exchange for regulatory security and public legitimacy. The insight: this completely reframes the problem. Current regulation treats STRs as a community threat. What if you became the mechanism through which STRs serve community rather than contradict it? You'd be collaborating with regulators, not dodging them. It's a nonprofit model, not a startup model.

* **The actual collective could be organized around *seasonal workers and community resilience*.** What if you hired cleaning, maintenance, and support staff as a permanent cooperative workforce, not gig contractors, and the "owners" were just capital partners? The cooperative would: maintain workers through seasonal downturns with cross-training and internal projects; invest profits in neighborhood infrastructure (parks, community spaces); build guest experiences around local employment and cultural integration. Owners' returns would be capped at a reasonable dividend; excess profits fund community reinvestment. The insight: current models treat workers as disposable and community as a cost center. A cooperative model makes worker stability and community integration the business. It would also be nearly immune to regulation—you'd be solving exactly what regulators want (local employment, neighborhood integration, housing stability).
