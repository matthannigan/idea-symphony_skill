# Community Relations and "Good Neighbor" Operations - The Politician

---

## Authentic Vs. Performed Good-Neighbor Positioning

* **Early crisis-containment protocol as political differentiator.** The gap between compliance and genuine adjacency lives in response speed and transparency. Property owners who feel their interests are protected, neighbors who feel heard—both signal authenticity. Design a noise-incident protocol where your company notifies neighbors within 2 hours of any complaint (not 24), provides a direct line to your local contact, and publishes monthly incident logs by address. This shifts the narrative from "we meet code" to "we operate in daylight." The threshold where the signal erodes: when a preventable incident (understaffed response, ignored complaint escalation) makes the local news during the city council election cycle—at that moment, a single failure amplifies the "absentee operator" frame.

* **Stakeholder veto strategy for property selection.** Your crisis-response infrastructure requires that you formally turn down or divest from properties with structural risk: narrow street parking, student-dominated neighborhoods, properties near schools or senior centers, or owners with prior noise violations. The political win is that neighbors can say "that company refused problem properties." Make this selection logic public—publish neighborhood categories where you do/don't operate. This converts risk management into community positioning: you're the operator who says no, not the one who says maybe-we'll-fix-it-later.

* **Neighborhood liaison role as embedded political actor.** Your 30-minute response requirement is a mandate to hire locals, not gig workers. A permanent neighborhood liaison—ideally from the surrounding community—who attends HOA meetings, joins the neighborhood Facebook group, and shows up at city council unprompted signals that your company is rooted, not extracting. That liaison becomes your political capital: when housing advocates attack STRs at council, that person is sitting in the audience, known and credible.

* **Genuine acknowledgment as power-shifting move.** Neighbors who feel seen by a company are less likely to mobilize against permits or support regulatory crackdowns. Schedule quarterly "neighbor feedback sessions" (actual listening, not selling) where residents can air concerns directly to your local contact and the founders. Document what you heard, what you changed, and share results back. This is radical because most operators ignore non-owner voices—your willingness to do the opposite becomes the story.

* **Transparency as political protection.** Publish your response times, resolution rates, and incident classifications by neighborhood. Invite the neighborhood association to audit your logs quarterly. This is uncomfortable—you will have complaints on record—but a transparent operator facing visible complaints is far more credible than a hidden operator facing rumors. Political opponents lose the narrative of "secret problems"; the data becomes your defense.

## Housing Displacement Obligation

* **Acknowledge the harm, then structure for visibility.** Most operators ignore displacement entirely. Your political differentiation is frank acknowledgment: "STR expansion does reduce long-term rental supply, and we're aware of that harm." Then structure the company to be visible about its role. If you reach 30-50 properties and local opposition mobilizes around "housing loss," your credibility depends on having said this clearly before—not spinning it when it becomes political. The emotional texture (powerlessness, resentment, grief) can be acknowledged through your hiring: prioritize long-term housing advocates for your community liaison role, visible commitment to stable employment for local staff.

* **Convert housing advocates into allies, not opponents.** Before your first permit renewal cycle, approach the leading housing nonprofits in the city. Propose that your company commits to a cap on properties in any single neighborhood, public reporting on vacancy rates you influence, and annual funding to a housing trust fund (percentage of annual revenue). This is a coalition move: housing advocates need a win to give you cover, you need them to not testify against permits. Frame it as partnership, not charity.

* **Reframe the scale question before it becomes a crisis.** The danger point is when you're visible enough (30-50 properties) that you become a symbol but small enough that you're still blamed for the city's housing problem. Get ahead of this by being explicit about your role: "We manage X% of STRs in this city; the housing crisis involves zoning, development policy, and speculation—we're one piece, not the cause." This is honest and political: it makes you harder to scapegoat while acknowledging the real harm.

* **Offer structural alternatives to displacement guilt.** Some property owners care about housing impact. Offer them a tiered fee structure: owners willing to keep their property long-term rentals for 6 months per year get a discount on management fees. Owners willing to offer your company's long-term rental referral network priority get a different discount. This lets conscience-stricken owners act on concern, while keeping your business model flexible.

* **Partner with long-term rental programs as a political shield.** If you can connect displaced renters or property owners with permanent supportive housing programs, you shift from "operator removing units" to "operator supporting the housing ecosystem." Announce publicly: "We partner with [nonprofit] to support 5 long-term rental placements annually." This is a visibility play—it's not enough to solve the housing crisis, but it signals you're not indifferent.

## Articulating Community Benefit To Non-Owners

* **Lead with infrastructure investment, not owner profit.** A skeptical long-term renter's question tests whether the company exists primarily to extract wealth from their neighborhood. Answer honestly but strategically: "We employ 3 local people in property management, cleaning, and maintenance—all permanent, above-minimum-wage jobs. We funnel licensing fees and taxes to the city that might otherwise not exist. We have enforcement capacity (24-hour contact, incident logging) that reduces the total number of problem properties in the city because we can remove bad actors fast." This is not a feel-good claim; it's a structural argument that the company's management layer reduces neighborhood risk.

* **Offer non-owners a role in operations as proof of commitment.** Propose that your company's board or advisory committee includes one seat specifically for a long-term housing advocate or renter representative. This is unusual enough that it signals real commitment; the alternative (no non-owner voice) signals you don't care. The political move is to make this offer prominently and early, before opposition crystallizes.

* **Commit to visible enforcement against problem owners.** Non-owners care most about bad neighbor outcomes—noise, parking, disturbance. Your answer: "We will terminate management contracts for owners who chronically violate incident protocols or neighborhood standards. We have the authority, we will use it, and we publish which properties we've divested from and why." This is actionable benefit specific to non-owners: you reduce the total neighborhood burden by curating the operator pool.

* **Reframe noise/parking/incident management as community service, not liability management.** Your 30-minute response requirement and incident logging—frame these as neighborhood protection infrastructure, not operator compliance. Say: "We're not just meeting code; we're creating a neighborhood accountability system that didn't exist before. Non-owners benefit because there's now a 24-hour contact who responds to problems immediately and documents patterns." Make it structural: the company becomes the enforcement layer that long-term residents never had.

* **Create visible proof points early.** In year one, identify 3-4 high-conflict properties run by other operators. Quietly meet with those property owners; offer to take over management with performance commitments on noise/parking. When you succeed (and you will, with genuine standards), use it publicly: "We inherited problem property X; within 60 days, complaint volume dropped 80%." This is the direct benefit to non-owners: bad situations get better because a competent, accountable operator replaced a negligent one.

## The Community Partner That Changed the Conversation

* **Map the formal and informal gatekeepers before year two.** To become a respected policy stakeholder, you must first identify who actually shapes the city's STR conversation: the city council chair (formal authority), the housing nonprofit director (credibility with renters), the neighborhood association president (grassroots power), the developer lobby (growth narrative), and the legacy media reporter covering STRs (information flow). Your political task: by year two, all five know you by name and trust that you operate ethically. This requires showing up, not as an owner advocate, but as a data source and accountability voice.

* **Play the middle—not as a compromise, but as an honest broker.** Housing advocates hate STR operators; property owners hate regulation; city council is split. Your political position is: "We're an operator, we're profitable, and we believe in tough neighborhood standards because good standards protect our business long-term." This honesty is rarer than it should be. It gives housing advocates permission to work with you without endorsing STRs, and gives the city council political cover to fund STR compliance infrastructure.

* **Fund research that reframes the debate.** By year three, commission an independent audit of your properties' impact: noise patterns, unit conversion rates, parking usage, long-term renter displacement. Publish it publicly. The goal is not to claim you're perfect; it's to move the conversation from "all STRs are bad" to "management quality and operational standards matter." This research becomes council members' evidence for regulation that distinguishes good from bad operators.

* **Build a policy coalition by section, not by interest group.** Work with council members individually to understand what they need politically. Member A (pro-housing) needs evidence that your company's standards protect long-term rental stock. Member B (pro-business) needs evidence that you're a scalable economic development model. Member C (swing vote) needs evidence that the city can manage STRs effectively. Tailor your evidence for each, not by lying, but by emphasizing different truths. Your company becomes indispensable to council members' policy agendas.

* **Use the state preemption conversation as your opening.** The state legislature is considering preempting local STR bans. Most cities see this as a threat; you see it as an opportunity. Testify at the state hearing that cities should retain local authority—but only if they adopt meaningful standards. This positions you as favoring regulation (unexpected for an operator) and gives the city council political cover to enact stronger standards. You become the operator who supports tough rules because you can operate under them.

* **Create the "model operator" template and offer it to other markets.** By year three, other coastal cities will be asking how you managed community opposition successfully. Package your operational standards, liability structure, community liaison role, and incident logging as a replicable model. Offer other companies a licensing agreement on your system. This is the ultimate political move: you're not just managing your own properties, you're shaping the industry standard, which means every operator who adopts your model becomes evidence that community-friendly STR management is possible.

## Housing Affordability As Structural Headwind

* **Make scarcity your brand differentiator.** The affordability headwind is real and accelerating. Position your company as "quality over scale": you will cap managed properties at 40-50 total, all in neighborhoods already zoned for STRs, none in areas with tight rental supply. This constraint is a political signal: you're not trying to monetize the entire housing stock. Housing advocates lose their "extraction company" narrative because you've voluntarily limited yourself. This is counterintuitive for business growth, but it's your insurance policy against the next wave of STR crackdowns.

* **Advocate for neighborhood-specific rules before they're imposed.** Work with city council to establish zone-by-zone STR density limits. Propose that your company commits to staying below those limits as a matter of principle. This is positioning: you're not fighting regulation, you're help designing it. When the next housing crisis conversation happens, you've already demonstrated restraint.

* **Create a "no whole-building conversion" covenant.** Offer this to property owners and publish it: your company will never take on a property that's a whole-building conversion (where the owner has systematically converted all units from long-term to short-term). This covenant limits your growth potential but becomes your most credible political signal: you're actively declining the most harmful STR category. Housing advocates will cite it, council members will cite it, and you become the operator they can defend.

* **Partner transparently with affordable housing developers.** Identify affordable housing projects in the city. Propose that your company donates a percentage of annual revenue to those projects, or commits to employing residents from those developments in your operations. This is visible wealth redistribution: you benefit from the STR market, housing advocates benefit from your success. It's not enough to "solve" housing, but it shifts the narrative from "extraction" to "contribution."

* **Build a sunset clause into your ownership agreements.** Offer property owners a contract that includes a provision: if the city passes strict new STR regulations that limit your portfolio size, you'll buy out owners who want to exit at a fair price, using a portion of retained earnings. This is a political ace: if the city tightens rules, you don't abandon owners; you help them transition. It signals long-term commitment to the community, not short-term extraction.

## Public-Facing Community Score Visibility

* **Transform radical transparency into political immunity.** A public community score (published noise incidents, parking complaints, resolution times) sounds radical because most operators hide this data. But publish it, and you become nearly impossible to attack. A housing advocate testifying against STRs can't say "this company is negligent" when the incident log is public and shows 95% resolution within 48 hours. Opponents' narrative collapses under the weight of your data.

* **Make the transparency asymmetrical in your favor.** Publish your own incidents but also publish the baseline: "Properties managed by Company X had average 2.3 noise complaints per unit annually; our managed properties average 0.8." Transparency that makes you look better than the competition is not just good PR—it's a barrier to entry that competitors can't match. They either adopt your standards (and manage fewer problem properties) or stay hidden (and face mounting pressure).

* **Use the transparency requirement to cull problem owners early.** When you propose community score transparency to property owners, some will refuse. Those are your red flags: owners who won't accept transparency are signaling that their properties can't operate under scrutiny. Decline to manage those properties. This filtering is politically invisible—you quietly manage only accountable owners, while irresponsible operators stay in the spotlight.

* **Design the score to measure what neighbors actually care about.** Not just incidents, but response patterns: "Average time from complaint to contact: 47 minutes. Average time from complaint to resolution: 18 hours." Neighbors care about being heard quickly, not about abstract compliance. A public score that shows you listen fast becomes a narrative about respect, not bureaucracy.

* **Pair transparency with a neighborhood veto mechanism.** Propose (and implement) that an ongoing community score below a threshold triggers a neighborhood association review of that property. If the review goes poorly, you divest from that property. This sounds extreme—and it is—but it's the political genius: you're not just transparent, you're accountable to neighbors in a way that has real consequences. Opponents can't attack a company that lets neighborhoods fire problem properties.

## From Operator To Civic Organ

* **Embed your survival in measurable neighborhood outcomes.** The radical pitch: restructure the company as a benefit corporation or public benefit entity where your charter requires annual reporting on neighborhood indicators: long-term rental vacancy rates, displacement-prevention outcomes, noise incident trends, local job creation. Your profits are conditional on measurable improvement. This is a complete power inversion: instead of the neighborhood justifying your existence, you're justifying your existence to the neighborhood.

* **Create a neighborhood wealth-sharing mechanism.** Propose that 10% of annual profits go into a community development fund, governed by a board with majority representation from non-owners. This fund invests in housing preservation, support for displaced renters, local business development, or neighborhood infrastructure. Your survival becomes structurally linked to that fund's success. When a city council member asks "does this company give anything back?" the answer is a funded, visible institution.

* **Reframe "utility" language as political positioning.** You're not a property manager; you're a "neighborhood infrastructure company." Your local contact person isn't a liability manager; they're a "neighborhood liaison." Your incident logging isn't compliance; it's "public health monitoring." This language shift signals that you understand the company as embedded in the community, accountable to it, not just extracting from it. It's unusual enough that it changes how people think about your role.

* **Build in a community board veto on major decisions.** Before you acquire more than 5 properties in a neighborhood, the community board votes. Before you change core policies, community board consultation. This doesn't mean the community runs your business, but it means you can't make neighborhood-changing decisions without neighborhood input. It's the political move that neutralizes "absentee operator" accusations before they start.

* **Create a "commons dividend" model for property owners.** Property owners who join your portfolio automatically become members of the community fund governance board (at 10% of seats). This aligns owner and neighborhood interests visibly: owners succeed when the neighborhood thrives, not just when bookings spike. Owners become stakeholders in neighborhood outcomes, not just revenue extraction.

## Dissolving The Guest-Neighbor Boundary

* **Map the stakeholder resistance before you design the integration.** This is the most ambitious vision, so the political opposition will be sharp: city council members worried about "commercializing residential spaces," neighbors worried about "outsiders colonizing the block," property owners worried about "complicated social obligations." The political path forward requires that you don't announce this vision broadly—you pilot it in one neighborhood, with strong community buy-in and transparent metrics, before scaling. Pilot success becomes your political evidence that it works.

* **Design integration through mandatory participation, not optional.** You can't dissolve the guest-neighbor boundary if guests and neighbors have the option to opt out. Build it in: every property includes a "neighborhood welcome packet" with local business recommendations, neighborhood history, community calendar; every guest is invited to a weekly neighborhood coffee (optional, but normalized); staff live in the neighborhood (visible presence, relationship-building). The integration happens because it's structural, not because guests are altruistic.

* **Use the integration to solve the noise/parking problem simultaneously.** Neighbors resent guests because they're "consumers passing through." If guests are participants in neighborhood life—attending community events, eating at local restaurants, understanding the neighborhood as home—they're less likely to be high-conflict. Integration is not just an ideological win; it's a practical noise-reduction strategy. Frame it to council members as a crime-reduction and community-cohesion play.

* **Create accountability that runs both ways.** If guests are neighbors, they need to follow neighbor norms, and neighbors need to engage with them respectfully. Build a dispute resolution mechanism where a neighbor and guest (mediated by your local contact) can work through conflicts directly. This is the radical move: neighbors see guests as problems, but if they have to negotiate with them face-to-face, understanding often follows. Your company becomes the convener, not the buffer.

* **Measure success through friendship networks.** A year in, conduct a survey: of the guests who stayed, how many exchanged contact info with neighbors? How many returned to the neighborhood? How many became long-term advocates for the area? This is the data that proves integration works. It's unusual metric (most operators measure bookings and revenue), but it's the political signal that your company is creating actual community, not just short-term transactions.

## The Keystone Species

* **Identify which single behavior shifts the entire perception.** A keystone behavior is counterintuitive and unexpected: something that flips the narrative from "absentee operator" to "committed steward" because it's so unusual. Examples: your company publishes incident logs publicly (other operators hide them); your company refuses to manage party-house properties (other operators pursue them); your company funds neighborhood improvements from revenue (other operators pocket profits). Pick one behavior that is genuinely costly to your business, and do it visibly. Opposition has to contend with behavior they can't argue against.

* **Make noise transparency the keystone.** Every property publishes real-time noise incident logs (complaint received, time, resolution, outcome). This single behavior—transparency about noise, the issue opposition cares most about—neutralizes the narrative that you're negligent. When housing advocates testify about STRs at council, they'll have to acknowledge: "These properties are transparent about incidents." Transparency doesn't eliminate opposition, but it makes opposition harder because your data contradicts their assumptions.

* **Pair the keystone with a governance commitment.** Your company commits to annual third-party audits of noise standards and publishes results. This is expensive and exposes problems. But it's the keystone move: most operators resist scrutiny; you invite it. An operator that invites independent oversight becomes nearly impossible to portray as negligent. The behavior is disproportionate in effect because it signals deep confidence in your standards.

* **Use the keystone to recruit other operators.** Other property managers, seeing your transparency working as a political shield, will copy you. Within two years, the "transparency standard" becomes industry norm, which amplifies your original move. You weren't just protecting your own operation; you shifted expectations across the entire market. City council members will cite the emerging standard as evidence that regulation works, housing advocates will have to acknowledge that some operators are accountable, and your company becomes the origin point of that shift.

* **Make the keystone irreversible.** Once you publish incident logs, you can't stop without a credibility collapse. This constraint is intentional: you're committing to a standard that's costly to maintain, which signals you're not cutting corners as soon as opposition fades. The irreversibility itself is the political force—it proves commitment.

## The Neighborhood Pharmacy, Not The Chain Drugstore

* **Build the local contact role as a permanent neighborhood position, not a rotation.** The city requires a local contact within 30 minutes. Most operators hire gig workers or rotate staff. You hire a permanent, salaried neighborhood liaison who lives in the area, attends HOA meetings, knows property owners and neighbors by name. This person is not a liability manager checking a box; they're a community anchor. The political advantage: neighbors have a relationship, not a transaction.

* **Design the liaison role to report upward from neighbors, not downward from corporate.** Your liaison doesn't just respond to complaints; they actively solicit feedback. Monthly "office hours" at a local coffee shop where neighbors can air concerns, regardless of whether they're complaint-related. This is the inversion: instead of waiting for problems, you're proactively gathering intelligence about neighborhood sentiment. That intelligence becomes your early warning system for opposition building.

* **Pay the liaison enough that you can afford a good one and be selective.** This is the non-negotiable part of this model. A poorly paid, overworked liaison will burn out and create worse relationships than no liaison. Budget for genuine local talent—someone with community roots, problem-solving skills, and the authority to make decisions. This person's salary is your insurance policy against neighborhood opposition.

* **Use the liaison relationship to identify owner retention problems early.** A good liaison will hear complaints from property owners (difficult neighbors, changing regulations, increasing fees) before those owners decide to exit. Your early intervention on owner concerns prevents churn and builds owner loyalty. Owner retention is the business metric; neighborhood relationship is the political asset that unlocks it.

* **Make the liaison a public face for the company at official venues.** Your liaison, not the founders, shows up at neighborhood association meetings, city council hearings, and community events. They become the company's public presence in the neighborhood. This is radical for a startup—most founders want visibility—but it signals that your company is embedded, not just visiting. The liaison's presence, month after month, becomes proof of commitment.

## Opponents As Co-Designers

* **Structure the advisory panel as a genuine power-sharing mechanism, not a listening tour.** Most companies do "stakeholder listening" and then ignore what they heard. You create an advisory panel with 5-7 members drawn from housing advocates, noise-affected neighbors, and long-term rental organizers. The panel meets quarterly, reviews your policies, and has the authority to recommend changes. You commit to publishing your response to every recommendation: implemented, considered and rejected, or rejected with reasoning. This is radical because it genuinely constrains your autonomy, but that constraint is your political credibility.

* **Recruit the most vocal opponents first.** The person who testified against STRs at city council three times? Invite them to the panel. The neighborhood association president who led a petition against your industry? Invite them. This is counterintuitive, but vocal opponents often become the most effective inside advocates because they've invested in thinking through the problems deeply. Their credibility with other opponents is high; their endorsement, once earned, shifts the entire conversation.

* **Use the panel to pre-negotiate opposition before conflicts arise.** Before you open in a new neighborhood, convene the panel, present the property, solicit concerns, and design mitigation together. When opposition eventually emerges in that neighborhood, the panel can say "we reviewed this property thoroughly; we have conditions, but we're not opposed." Opposition loses its unified front because some former opponents are inside the system.

* **Make panel membership public and visible.** List the panel members on your website with photos and bios. This is exposure for them, but it also creates accountability: panel members become associated with your company's success, which gives them skin in the game. They're no longer pure critics; they're stakeholders in whether this works. That stake is the political transformation.

* **Fund panel members' attendance and participation.** Provide honorariums or research funding to panel members for their time. This is not a bribe; it's a signal that their work is valued and that you're not just extracting their volunteer labor. Paid participation also raises the stakes: panel members are now formally invested in the company's success in a way volunteers are not.

## Converting Opponents, Not Just Neutralizing Them

* **Segment opponents by motive and design distinct engagement strategies for each.** The housing affordability opponent sees STRs as housing removal; the noise-affected neighbor sees STRs as lifestyle disruption; the regulatory opponent sees STRs as compliance risk. These are three different people with three different concerns. A single "good neighbor" message won't move them; you need three different messages grounded in what they actually care about.

* **For the affordability opponent: offer a concrete transparency commitment.** They won't believe the company never displaces renters. But they will believe a company that publicly commits to: zero whole-building conversions, quarterly reporting on unit conversion rates by neighborhood, and partnership with housing nonprofits. Design the message around measurable constraint, not intention: "Here's what we will not do, and here's how you'll know."

* **For the noise-affected neighbor: offer a personal protection mechanism.** They don't care about the housing debate; they care about sleeping through the night. Show them the incident response protocol, the noise monitoring equipment, the direct line to your 24/7 contact, the monthly incident report they'll receive by address. Design the pitch around personal control: "You have direct access to the person managing the problem property, and we document everything you report."

* **For the regulatory opponent: offer early engagement in policy design.** This person cares about rule-making and enforcement. Propose that your company, in consultation with their organization, develops the operational standards you'll commit to upfront, before regulations require them. Frame it as: "Help us design standards that work, and we'll adopt them voluntarily. That becomes the baseline for the city's regulations." They move from opposition to co-authorship.

* **Create a "moved from opposition to acceptance" pathway with visible rewards.** When a formerly vocal opponent agrees to speak positively about your company (at council, to media), acknowledge it publicly: "We're grateful that [person/organization] initially challenged us; that feedback shaped our current standards." This is a graceful exit path for opponents: they can claim credit for improving the company without losing face. This is rare—most companies fight opponents to the end—which is why it's so effective.

* **Track and publish conversions as evidence of legitimacy.** Maintain a public list of organizations and individuals who moved from opposition to partnership. Update it annually. This is political evidence that the company is learning and improving, not just selling. Opposition sees that peers are engaged, which lowers the activation energy for their own engagement.

## "Good Neighbor" As A Political Product, Not Just A Brand Claim

* **Design the track record as political evidence, not marketing material.** City council members need ammunition to defend STR permits or favorable regulations. You provide it: documented incident response times (average 47 minutes, target 30 minutes), documented resolution rates (95% resolved within 48 hours), documented community feedback (quarterly neighborhood surveys showing sentiment trends). This data is not a brochure; it's political evidence.

* **Publish the track record in a venue council members can cite.** Your "Good Neighbor Operations Report" is annual, independently audited, and released publicly. It's boring, technical, and undeniable—exactly the document a council member can cite without looking partisan. Media won't cover it (too dry), but council members will use it constantly. This is the political product: evidence that the company is accountable.

* **Use the regulation cycle as your deadline for track record credibility.** When the city's next STR regulation review happens, you want 12-24 months of documented performance. This timing is intentional: by the time council members are debating regulations, you have a visible, audited track record showing you operate differently. This track record becomes the basis for carving exceptions into regulations for operators who meet your standards.

* **Create a tiered "good neighbor" certification that others can aspire to.** After three years of publishing your standards and demonstrating consistent performance, propose to the city that "certified good neighbor" STR operators (meeting your standards) get different treatment: zoning relief in exchange for stricter monitoring, tax breaks in exchange for public reporting, permit streamlining in exchange for binding oversight. This is the ultimate political move: you've defined excellence, and now you're offering other operators (and the city) a pathway to join you.

* **Make compliance with your standards a contractual requirement for all property owners.** Owners who won't commit to your Good Neighbor standards don't get management contracts. This is the enforcement mechanism that turns standards into reality. When council members ask "will property owners actually follow these?" you can say: "They sign contracts committing to them, with termination clauses if they don't. Our business model depends on enforcement."
