## Statewide Preemption as an Asymmetric Bet

* **The timing paradox courts disaster.** You're assuming you can execute a dual-track strategy while bootstrapping operations on $80K with two founders juggling 5–10 properties. Building compliance infrastructure for *both* regulatory futures means doubling your legal, systems, and operational overhead before you've proved the core model works. If preemption fails (and legislatures often disappoint), you've sunk capital into unnecessary compliance layers. Meanwhile, locking in long-term contracts with restricted-zone owners at "low cost" creates contractual leverage only if those zones actually open up—if they don't, you're managing properties in constrained markets where your competitive advantage evaporates.

* **Restricted-zone owners may not trust you to wait.** Property owners who've been locked out of STR profits are desperate, not stupid. They'll demand guarantees or buyout clauses if preemption doesn't pass by year X, which flips your "asymmetric bet" into a liability. Alternatively, they'll shop your playbook to larger operators with deeper capital, using you as market research. You'd need iron-clad confidentiality and non-compete language—which creates friction with exactly the operators-of-good-faith positioning you're also building.

* **Regulatory betting creates reputational fragility.** If city council discovers a startup is strategically positioning to profit from legislative preemption, it becomes ammunition for STR opponents who already view you with suspicion. You'd be framed not as a "good neighbor" but as a speculative player betting against the community's stated preference. This could poison relationships with city planners, HOAs, and the long-term renters you're theoretically concerned about. A single hostile council hearing could kill your brand before year one ends. Better to focus on excelling within *current* rules and adapting if preemption happens.

* **The dual-infrastructure cost is understated.** "Building infrastructure to thrive if local rules hold" requires different vendor relationships, insurance policies, and operational workflows than preparing for preemption. You're not running two lightweight parallel tracks—you're maintaining two separate compliance regimes, two sets of contractor networks, and two pricing models. With two founders, this dilutes focus and execution quality at the exact moment you need to nail operational excellence to retain those first 5–10 properties and build word-of-mouth trust.

* **Preemption odds may not favor the bet.** State legislatures are cautious on housing density. Preemption passes only if tourism/real-estate lobbies overpower housing advocates, and coastal cities are increasingly pro-housing. Your $80K bet should ride on *building a business that works within current rules*, not on legislative outcomes you can't control. If preemption happens, you're already positioned as a proven operator; if it doesn't, you haven't gambled your capital on a long-shot legislative play.
