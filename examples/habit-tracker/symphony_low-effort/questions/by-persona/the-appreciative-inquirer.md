# Brainstorming Questions from The Appreciative Inquirer: Modern Habit Tracker Web App

---

## Discovery: What Has Already Worked

1. **The app you wish existed**: Think about a moment when a tool — any tool, digital or analog — made a positive habit feel effortless and even joyful. What was it about that experience — the friction it removed, the feedback it gave, the way it fit into your day — that made you want to return to it? How could those qualities become design principles for this habit tracker?

2. **Competitors' brightest moments**: Even in a crowded market, some habit apps have created genuine, lasting change for at least some users. When has an existing tool like Habitica or Streaks succeeded beyond its creators' expectations — what user stories emerged that surprised everyone? What strengths in those products, even the ones we intend to surpass, deserve to be carried forward rather than discarded?

3. **The 47-day user**: The PRD envisions an app that is easier to use on day 47 than day 2. Where have you seen or experienced a product, service, or practice that genuinely got more seamless with time — one that adapted to you rather than demanding you adapt to it? What made that possible, and how might that principle translate into the habit-tracker's progression model?

---

## Dream: The Future Worth Building

4. **The gratitude test**: If this app became something people genuinely loved — not just used, but felt grateful for — what would they say to their friends about it? What would a user who has tracked a habit for six months, without ever feeling burned or guilt-tripped, describe as the single thing the app did differently from everything else they'd tried?

5. **The invisible product**: The PRD sets an aspiration of being "invisible-but-present by week six." Imagine the app has fully achieved this — a user barely notices it, yet their habits are quietly flourishing. What does a Tuesday morning look like for that person? What does the app's role feel like: a mirror, a scaffold, a quiet companion? What metaphor captures the relationship you want users to have with it at month three?

6. **The dropout who returned**: Picture a user who had abandoned every previous habit app after week two. They try this one, and six months later they are still using it — and they have built a habit that genuinely changed their life. What happened in weeks one and two that was different? What did the app do — or refrain from doing — that kept them from feeling like a failure?

---

## Design: Structures That Make the Dream Reliable

7. **Grace as architecture**: The PRD's "streak recovery with a brief note" feature is a quiet act of compassion built into the product. What other small structural choices — in notification design, onboarding flow, or the weekly review format — could carry the same message: that a missed day is a data point, not a moral failure? What would it look like to make psychological safety a first-class design constraint alongside check-in speed?

8. **The weekly review as a wedge**: The PRD raises a sharp question — is weekly review underused in competitor apps because it's bad, or because it's buried? If you were to make the Sunday review the signature, defining experience of this product — the moment users look forward to most — what would that review feel, look, and sound like? What existing strengths in the app's core loop (cue/routine/reward framing, partial logging, grace periods) could the weekly review surface and celebrate?

9. **Monetization that earns its keep**: The "hard no" list in the PRD — no ads, no data selling, no upsells in notifications — is itself a product decision that communicates values. What does a monetization model look like when it is built entirely on the foundation of user trust and demonstrated value? How might the paid tier be designed so that upgrading feels like a natural next step for a user who has already experienced the app's core strengths — not a paywall, but a graduation?

---

## Destiny: First Steps and Lasting Commitments

10. **The constraint as a gift**: A solo developer with a three-month MVP window and a "boring, stable stack" preference might seem like limitations — but what existing capabilities, patterns, and decisions already in place make this team uniquely suited to build a minimal, trustworthy, fast habit tracker? What does the solo-dev constraint enable (focus, coherence, opinionated design) that a larger team might struggle to achieve?

11. **The small experiment that reveals the most**: If you could run one small test — a prototype, a landing page, a paper mockup, a week-long personal trial — before writing a single line of production code, what would it be? Which open question from the PRD (iOS-first vs. web-first, offline support, partial logging UX, the "inactive for 10+ days" flow) would, if answered confidently, make every subsequent decision clearer and faster?
