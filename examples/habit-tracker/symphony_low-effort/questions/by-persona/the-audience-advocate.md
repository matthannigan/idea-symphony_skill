# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Modern Habit Tracker Web App

---

## Who Actually Gets Left Behind

1. **The burned user's emotional scar tissue**: The PRD targets adults who have already bounced from at least one habit app — but what does that prior failure feel like when they open a new one? What specific interface moments (a badge unlocking, a streak counter appearing, a push notification at 8pm) are likely to trigger the "here we go again" feeling, and how would you know if your design accidentally reactivates that response?

2. **The 3-habit free tier as a capability ceiling**: From the perspective of someone with a chronic health condition managing medications, hydration, sleep, exercise, and stress journaling, the free tier's 3-habit limit is not a soft nudge toward paid — it is a wall that excludes the users who may need the tool most. What assumptions are embedded in choosing 3 as the free ceiling, and who does that number serve?

3. **Literacy and language accessibility**: The Atomic Habits cue/routine/reward framing is legible to users familiar with the self-help genre, but what happens to the person who has never read that book, who processes information differently, or whose primary language is not English? Is the product being designed with its most fluent users in mind, or with its least fluent?

---

## The Emotional Journey Nobody Talks About

4. **What silence communicates**: The PRD explicitly avoids guilt language in the weekly review — but silence is also a message. When a user opens the app after 10 inactive days and sees nothing, no judgment, no context, no acknowledgment that time passed, what do they actually experience? Is absence of guilt the same as presence of compassion, and how would you tell the difference?

5. **The partial-completion dignity problem**: The "partial" log — noting you did 15 of 30 planned minutes — is framed as a UX complexity question (how to capture it without adding friction). But from the user's perspective, it is also an emotional moment: admitting shortfall, deciding whether to record it, wondering how it will look in history. What does the interface communicate about the value of imperfect effort, and does that match what users actually need to hear in that moment?

6. **Shame-free does not mean shame-proof**: Removing badges, streaks, and guilt language reduces designed shame, but users bring their own. Someone who has missed three weeks will construct their own narrative from whatever data the app shows — a gap in the log, a low completion rate, a count of days since last check-in. What responsibility does the design have for how users interpret neutral data about themselves?

---

## Whose Voice Shaped This

7. **The secondary user who never gets to speak**: The PRD names parents helping kids build habits as a v2 secondary audience. But the children who would actually use or be subject to those habits have no voice in the spec at all. If this feature is built, whose agency does it serve — the parent's desire to instill habits, or the child's development of intrinsic motivation? What are the implications of building habit surveillance infrastructure for minors, even with the best intentions?

8. **Community input versus assumed community**: The PRD's target user is constructed from inference — "adults 25–45 who have tried at least one habit app before and bounced." Have people who match that description been interviewed, tested with prototypes, or observed in their actual morning routines? What they say they want (less gamification) and what actually sustains their engagement (some forms of social accountability) may not be the same thing.

---

## Barriers the Design Assumes Away

9. **The 30-second morning check-in and context collapse**: The core loop assumes a user has 30 uninterrupted seconds in the morning to review habits. But the people who most struggle with habit formation often have the most disrupted mornings — caregiving responsibilities, shift work, irregular sleep, housing instability. What does the product look like for someone whose mornings are not reliably theirs?

10. **Offline and intermittent connectivity as an equity signal**: The PRD frames offline support as a "how common is the subway scenario" question — implicitly a convenience feature for urban commuters. But offline support is also an access question for users in areas with poor connectivity, users on limited data plans, and users who use shared or public devices. What would it mean to treat offline-first not as a technical preference but as a commitment to serving users across economic circumstances?
