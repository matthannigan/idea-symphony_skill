# Brainstorming Questions from The Provocateur: Modern Habit Tracker Web App

---

## What If the App Was Designed to Make Habits Fail?

1. **Deliberate failure as a feature**: What if the app was explicitly designed to help users quit habits they no longer want, not just build new ones — and what would a "successful quit" UI look like compared to a successful streak? *The insight: most trackers treat abandonment as silent failure; surfacing intentional quitting could distinguish this product and reduce shame spiraling.*

2. **The disposable habit**: Imagine the app deletes every habit record after 90 days, permanently — no archive, no export, no memory. What would users actually lose, and what might they gain? *The insight: if the data disappears, what value is left? Forces a reckoning with whether the tracker serves the habit or has become the habit.*

---

## What If the User Is the Enemy of Their Own Habit?

3. **Solving the wrong problem entirely**: What if the real barrier isn't tracking consistency but the fact that users have chosen the wrong habits to begin with — habits that conflict with their actual values — and the app is optimizing a fundamentally broken input? What would a "habit audit" experience look like that challenged the user's choices rather than enabled them? *The insight: most apps assume the habit selection is correct; questioning it early could prevent the burnout cycle the PRD's target user has already experienced.*

4. **Friction as the product**: What if the check-in deliberately took 60 seconds instead of 10, and that friction was framed as the point — a micro-ritual, not an obstacle? Which habits would users still track, and which would they drop? *The insight: the <10s design goal may optimize for volume of tracking at the cost of depth of engagement; some habits may benefit from a moment of reflection rather than a tap.*

---

## What If the Streak Model Is Backwards?

5. **Inverting the streak**: Imagine the app celebrated the longest gap in a habit — the recovery distance — rather than the unbroken streak. Users compete with their past selves on how far they bounced back after a miss, not how long they avoided missing. What would that change about the emotional design? *The insight: the PRD's "streak handling with grace" feature is still streak-centric; radical reframing of what counts as achievement could reach users who bounce off streak apps entirely.*

---

## What If the Competition Is Not Other Habit Apps?

6. **The impossible competitor**: What if the real competitor isn't Habitica or HabitBull but the user's alarm clock — the first touchpoint in every morning routine? What would it mean to design a check-in experience so embedded in an existing morning ritual that the app becomes invisible, not an app at all? *The insight: positioning against habit apps may be the wrong frame; the product might be better understood as infrastructure for an existing ritual rather than a destination.*

---

## What If Privacy Is a Design Primitive, Not a Policy

7. **Zero-knowledge self-tracking**: Take the privacy posture to the absurd extreme — what if the app stored nothing on the server, ever, and the server's role was purely relay, not retention? What would users actually lose, and what new trust dynamics would it create with the target demographic? *The insight: "minimal analytics, no third-party tracking" is a policy stance; asking what zero-server-memory forces in the architecture reveals how much of the current design quietly assumes server-side habit data is necessary.*
