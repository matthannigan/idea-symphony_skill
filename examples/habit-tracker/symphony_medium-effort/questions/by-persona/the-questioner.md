# Brainstorming Questions from The Questioner: Modern Habit Tracker Web App

---

## Hidden Assumptions About What Users Actually Want

1. **The simplicity assumption**: Why do we assume that users who bounced from previous apps did so because those apps were too complex, rather than because the apps failed to deliver on the intrinsic motivation that got users to download them in the first place — and how does this distinction change what we build?

2. **The streak anxiety assumption**: The PRD asserts that streak anxiety is a failure mode — but what if streak anxiety is actually a feature for a meaningful subset of users, and what would it mean for differentiation if we're optimizing against a mechanic that some users specifically seek out?

3. **The anti-gamification stance**: What if the explicit rejection of XP, badges, and leaderboards is itself a product-positioning bet rather than a behavioral-science conclusion — and what evidence would change our mind about where the line between harmful and helpful extrinsic motivation actually sits?

4. **The "day 47" vision**: The PRD describes a product that gets easier on day 47 than day 2 — but what does that curve actually look like for users who are building fundamentally different habit types (a 10-second "drink water" habit vs. a 30-minute workout), and do we need different simplification strategies for different habit complexities?

---

## What We're Assuming About the Competitive Landscape

5. **The differentiation frame**: We've named Habitica, Streaks, and HabitBull as competitors — but what if the most relevant competition isn't other habit apps at all, and users' actual alternative is a paper calendar, a notes app, or simply not tracking? How does knowing the true counterfactual change the value proposition?

6. **The "crowded market" framing**: Why do we assume that market crowdedness is a strategic threat rather than evidence of validated demand — and what would it look like to design for the users who tried three different apps and still haven't found what they need, rather than users who haven't tried any?

7. **The solo-dev constraint as weakness**: The REQUEST frames a solo developer / small team as a constraint, but what if this constraint is actually a differentiating feature — how might a product built without VC pressure or growth-hacking mandates design differently, and is that difference something worth communicating to users?

---

## Probing the Behavioral Science Claims

8. **The Atomic Habits framing**: The PRD bakes in cue / routine / reward framing from Atomic Habits — but what if users who've already read that book (likely a substantial portion of the target demographic) find this framing patronizing or over-familiar, and what's the alternative if we strip the branded behavioral model out?

9. **The "grace" mechanic**: The one-day miss recovery feature is positioned as a behavioral-science-informed design — but what assumptions does this embed about what causes streak breaks, and how would the mechanic need to change if the primary cause is not forgetting but active avoidance (i.e., the user knew they didn't do it and chose not to log)?

10. **The weekly review bet**: The PRD hypothesizes that weekly review is underused in competitors because it's buried, not because it's unwanted — what's the minimum viable test of this hypothesis before committing significant design investment to it, and what would a negative result look like?

---

## Questioning the Monetization and Trust Architecture

11. **The free tier design**: The 3-habit free limit is framed as a conversion mechanism — but what if 3 habits is actually the right number for most users most of the time, meaning the free tier functions as a complete product for a large segment? How does that reframe the paid tier's job-to-be-done?

12. **The privacy-as-marketing question**: The PRD commits to minimal analytics and no third-party tracking — but how does this commitment get communicated to users in a way they can verify, and what would it mean to make privacy a legible, trust-building feature rather than a design constraint that's invisible to users?

---

## Who We're Not Thinking About

13. **The lapsed user problem**: The PRD mentions users who've been inactive for 10+ days as an open question — but who are we actually designing for in that moment: the user who wants to come back quietly, the user who wants to be held accountable, or the user who has genuinely decided the habit isn't right for them? How do we distinguish these three, and does a single re-engagement flow serve all of them?
