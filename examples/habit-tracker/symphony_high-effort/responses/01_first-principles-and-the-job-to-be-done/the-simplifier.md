# The Simplifier on First Principles and the Job-to-Be-Done

## The irreducible job, rebuilt from the literature

* **Memory + commitment, that's it.** Behavioral science says habits stick when you remove friction from deciding and provide evidence. You need a log (memory) and a signal of progress (commitment). Strip the spec: one-question check-in, one weekly view. That's the core loop. Everything else — streaks, badges, charts, social features, export — serves psychology theater, not behavior change. Start with just: "Did you do it today?" and "How many this week?" See if that moves the needle before you build the app competitors are copying.

## Tracking vs. deciding as the framing verb

* **Flip the interface, keep the data.** If deciding is the problem, the app's job is to lower the activation energy of *choosing* to act, not recording that you acted. Move check-in to the morning: "Will you do this today?" — a commitment before the day, not a confession after. Tracking should be invisible (background log), not the stage. This cuts out notifications, streak anxiety, and guilt entirely. The product becomes a decision prompt, not a performance auditor. Radically simpler, and closer to how habits actually form.

## Is the PRD's diagnosis of churn correct at root?

* **Churn may be goal failure, not UX failure.** If someone sets "meditate daily" and quits on day 8, better onboarding won't save that — they didn't actually want to meditate. Before you build features to reduce guilt or anxiety, ask: what if the app has friction at habit *creation*, not removal? "Are you sure about this?" and "How many days in a row will you actually do this?" — harsh, but it filters unrealistic habits upfront and puts the onus on the user to be honest. Cut the engineering; invest in clarity at the entry point.

## The minimum viable behavioral intervention

* **Three elements only: check-in, weekly tally, delete button.** No notifications, no streaks, no community. The intervention is: daily binary feedback (did it or didn't) + one visual per week (you hit 5 of 7) + the power to quit without shame. If that doesn't hit 25% D30 retention, the problem isn't the app — it's that your target user doesn't want the habit. Stop building features. Ask a different question: who are the users who *do* stick, and what made them different? The simplest product teaches you what actually matters.
