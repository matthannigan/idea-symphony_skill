## The Irreducible Job, Rebuilt from Behavioral Science Literature

* **Memory and commitment are real, but so is automaticity.** The spec treats habit formation as a conscious tracking problem—log and commit—but behavioral science (Lally, Phillipps) shows the actual job is moving behavior from intention-dependent to automatic execution. A memory prosthetic helps with reminders; a commitment device adds accountability. But most of the spec (streaks, achievement badges, detailed analytics) serve engagement metrics, not automaticity. A first-principles rebuild would prioritize: minimal friction at decision point, consistent cue-reward pairing, identity reinforcement over outcome tracking. Notifications become less about celebrating yesterday's win and more about removing tomorrow's friction.

* **The spec assumes tracking failure is the bottleneck; it's not.** Current features assume users fail because they forget or lack motivation signals. But literature on goal-setting failure suggests most users set habits poorly—habits misaligned with actual values, or with unrealistic frequency. A first-principles tracker might flip the entire UX: make habit creation effortful (force the user to define the cue, the existing behavior to anchor to, the reward they actually experience). Make check-in a 2-second tap. The spec does the opposite. If this diagnostic is right, removing UX friction at check-in while removing friction at creation would save months of development and hit retention targets faster than any notification tweak.

* **Streaks are a side effect, not a primary feature.** The spec treats streaks as core engagement. From first principles: a streak is the visual artifact of consistent execution, not the cause of it. Behavioral literature emphasizes that the psychological benefit comes from identity ("I am someone who does X"), not from a number going up. A minimal tracker might track frequency over rolling windows (90-day consistency) rather than unbroken chains, reducing guilt-driven churn when life happens. Streaks create an all-or-nothing frame that contradicts habit science, which emphasizes that two good days a week beats zero after one miss.

* **The core loop collapses to three moves, not ten features.** At absolute minimum: (1) reminder with cue clarity (what triggers this?); (2) one-tap logging (did I do it?); (3) weekly reflection (patterns, not shame). Every other feature—goals, data export, detailed analytics, social sharing—exists because competitors have it. A first-principles product might launch with just those three and iterate only if retention data shows bottlenecks at a specific step. The spec optimizes for feature parity with Habitica and Streaks; a fresh build optimizes for the smallest behavioral intervention that shifts D30 retention.

## Tracking vs. Deciding as the Framing Verb

* **Tracking is retrospective theater; deciding is where behavior lives.** The fundamental insight from Fogg's Behavior Model and habit research is that the decision moment—when the cue appears and the person must choose—is where intervention matters. Most apps frame the interaction as "tracking" (you did it, log it, celebrate it), which makes the app a record-keeper after the fact. A first-principles reframe asks: how do we make the deciding moment easier and more automatic? The product might shift from a check-in flow (did you do it?) to a prompt-and-decide flow (here's your cue, ready?). Notifications stop being rewards for yesterday and become cue replacements for tomorrow. The feature set collapses: no analytics, no streak visualizations, no achievements—just cue clarity, decision friction removal, and pattern recognition for the person choosing.

* **Outcome-framing vs. identity-framing is baked into the interaction model.** When you check off a box and see a streak counter, the psychological frame is "I completed a task today" (outcome). When the app asks "who do you want to be?" and provides friction-free execution, the frame becomes "I'm someone who does this" (identity). The spec's check-in design, with badges and streak notifications, subtly reinforces outcome thinking. A first-principles redesign would flip UX language—not "Streak: 42 days" but "You've chosen this 42 times. Who are you becoming?" Not "Great work today!" but "This is who you are now." Small changes in framing, massive shifts in retention because identity-based habits are more durable than outcome-based ones.

* **Decision friction comes from three sources; only one is the app's problem.** First: does the user know when and where to act? (cue clarity—app's job). Second: can the user actually do it given their context and ability? (real-world friction—app can't fix this). Third: do they actually want to? (goal-fit—app shouldn't hide this). Most habit apps obsess over point one and pretend points two and three don't exist. A first-principles app would design for all three: ruthlessly clear cues, realistic difficulty assessment at setup, and brutal honesty about goal-fit. If someone wants to "exercise more" but has a 90-minute commute and no gym membership, the app's job is to say that's hard, not to offer notifications and streaks as motivation band-aids.

## Is the PRD's Diagnosis of Churn Correct at Root?

* **Churn is a goal-setting failure being diagnosed as a UX failure.** The PRD attributes dropout to streak anxiety, guilt notifications, and gamification overload. But if users are setting habits they don't genuinely want—habits they think they *should* want—no UX improvement fixes that. At root: users self-select into the app with bad goals, the app removes friction to check-in, users feel the gap between aspiration and actual desire, and they churn. The PRD's solution (reduce guilt, simplify notifications) treats the symptom. A first-principles diagnosis would ask: is the product attracting users with low habit readiness? And if so, should there be hard friction at goal-creation, not only at check-in? Maybe the app needs to be harder to set up, not easier—to screen for genuine commitment before the user enters the tracking loop.

* **The PRD assumes motivation is something the product supplies; it isn't.** Guilt-trip notifications, gamification, and streak anxiety are the spec's attempt to provide extrinsic motivation when intrinsic motivation is weak. From first principles: if a user is relying on an app to motivate them to exercise, they're setting the wrong habit. The habit might be "go to the gym," but the real habit readiness issue is that they don't want to go. The product can't fix that. A first-principles approach would assume: our users are intrinsically motivated to build these specific habits. If they're not, they'll churn no matter what we build. So the retention target (>25% D30) might be realistic only for users who already want the habit. The PRD may need to reframe success: not "our UX is so good users stick around even with weak motivation," but "we attract users with genuine motivation, and keep them because we remove friction."

* **Habit readiness is a pre-product question the product doesn't address.** Before entering the app, users should ask: Do I actually want this habit? Is it aligned with my values? Do I have the ability to execute it? Am I willing to do it at the frequency I set? The PRD assumes the product's job begins after goal-setting. From first principles: the product's job might begin before setup, with a readiness questionnaire that's deliberately hard to fake. Users who can't articulate why they want the habit shouldn't be allowed to create it. Users who are setting the habit to please someone else should see a warning. This would lower signups and MAU, but would flatten the churn curve by filtering out users with low goal-fit before they enter the tracking loop and become churners.

## The Minimum Viable Behavioral Intervention

* **The smallest possible change: remove one click from the check-in loop.** If the core behavioral job is turning a decision into a habit, every friction point delays automaticity. The smallest change: notification opens to a pre-filled check-in screen (one tap: done) rather than opening the app to a home screen (three taps: navigate to habit, tap habit, check in). Measuring this: does it shift D7 retention? If not, friction isn't the problem. If yes, keep iterating. But the spec's roadmap includes push notifications, weekly digests, achievement badges, data export, social sharing. None of these shift the core loop. A first-principles approach: launch with check-in and weekly reflection only. Measure D7 and D30 retention. If both are below target with those two features, the problem isn't a missing feature; it's a goal-fit or ability problem.

* **The structural minimum: cue, decision, reward. Everything else defers.** Cue (notification or calendar): the thing that triggers the behavior. Decision (one-tap check-in): did you do it?. Reward (immediate visual feedback or identity affirmation): you are someone who does this. Defer: streak counting, analytics, goals, social features, gamification, data export. These are enhancements, not core. A minimal product might look like: open notification, tap "done," see "you've chosen this 42 times," close app. That loop, if repeated reliably, should move D30 retention. If it doesn't, the problem is upstream (users don't want the habit, aren't getting the cue, face real-world friction). The spec includes all of these defer features at launch, which optimizes for product complexity and feature parity, not for behavioral change.

* **The testable hypothesis: >25% D30 retention requires only identity reinforcement, not achievement gamification.** Most products assume retention comes from streak dopamine and badge unlocks. A first-principles hypothesis: retention comes from identity ("I'm someone who exercises"). If true, the smallest intervention is a weekly message that reinforces identity, not achievements—not "Streak: 42!" but "42 times you chose to exercise. You're building the identity of someone who moves." Test this with just that messaging on a stripped-down tracker. If D30 retention jumps from 10% to 25%, you've found the core mechanism. If it doesn't, badges and streaks won't save you either. The PRD assumes adding more rewards and gamification will solve churn; a first-principles test would isolate the single reward mechanism that actually works before building anything else.
