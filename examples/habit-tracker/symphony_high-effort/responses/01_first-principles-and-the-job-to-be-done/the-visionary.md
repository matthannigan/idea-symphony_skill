# The Visionary on First Principles and the Job-to-Be-Done

## Question 1: The Irreducible Job, Rebuilt from Behavioral Science Literature

* **Rethink memory + commitment as a unified phenomenon, not two separate services.** The current spec treats tracking and commitment as independent features — a log that you can view, plus notifications that motivate. But behavioral science on implementation intentions suggests the real innovation is making the decision-moment *identical* to the recording moment — what if the product didn't separate "deciding to do this" from "proving you did it"? Strip out historical tracking, weekly summaries, and streak counts; the only record is the last decision itself. The commitment doesn't live in past performance — it lives in the next choice.

* **Identity formation, not behavior logging.** The PRD assumes users need evidence of their habits to stay committed; behavioral science on self-concept suggests the opposite — over-measurement can actually fragment identity. What if the product completely eliminated the scoreboard and instead created a space where users narrate *who they're becoming* through their choices, with no quantification at all? The memory becomes linguistic and embodied, not numerical.

* **Friction at creation is the real leverage point.** The spec removes all friction from check-in; behavioral science on goal-setting and realistic planning suggests the opposite — users who succeed have already done the hard work *before* they enter the app. What if the entire interface was designed to delay habit logging until the user could articulate *why* this habit matters, what situations trigger it, and what success looks like in those terms? The app becomes a prescritive tool for thinking, not a passive record.

## Question 2: Tracking vs. Deciding as the Framing Verb

* **Flip the entire interaction model.** Instead of "log what you did," the core loop becomes "decide what you'll do next." Every interaction is a choice point, not a checkbox. The app becomes a decision-making environment where the user rehearses commitment in real time — they don't look at past streaks, they imagine the next moment when the habit will matter and practice choosing. Tracking disappears; deciding becomes visible through previews of upcoming decision points.

* **Eliminate the notion of "completion" entirely.** The current model treats each day as a binary success/failure; the shift to decision-framing means there's no such thing as "missing" a day because the app doesn't care about days. Instead, it shows the next opportunity to act and asks "Are you ready to decide right now?" The temporal unit shifts from days to decision moments, which may cluster differently for each person.

* **Identity decisions over outcome tracking.** If the product frames every interaction as "Who do you want to be in the next five minutes?" rather than "Did you follow the habit?", the psychological work changes completely. You're not managing a streak; you're rehearsing a self-image through micro-decisions. The app becomes a mirror for who you're becoming, not a scoreboard of what you've done.

## Question 3: Is the PRD's Diagnosis of Churn Correct?

* **Churn is a goal-readiness problem masquerading as a product problem.** The PRD's solution is to reduce guilt and anxiety through better UX; but what if the real issue is that the product makes it too easy to adopt habits you don't actually want? The paradigm shift: instead of removing friction from adoption, add *intentionality friction* — force users to spend time understanding why a habit matters before they can activate it. The product should fail fast for uncommitted goals and create space for genuine intention-building. Some users will bounce, and that's success because they've discovered they don't actually want the habit.

* **Reframe retention as "finding the right user" not "fixing the product."** If the target user is someone with intrinsic motivation gaps, the product can't engineer engagement; it can only provide honest feedback. What if the app's first interaction wasn't a habit setup wizard but a readiness assessment that either shows users they're ready and engaged, or tells them "This tool isn't for you yet"? The retention metric becomes "Did we help the right people discover they're serious?" rather than "How many users stayed?"

* **The absence of friction reveals the absence of commitment.** Behavioral science on implementation intentions shows that barriers force deliberation; if the product is frictionless and users still churn, it's because they never really decided in the first place. The radical move: make habit creation *harder*, not easier, so the people who succeed are those who've already done the psychological work.

## Question 4: The Minimum Viable Behavioral Intervention

* **Reduce to a single interaction: anticipation without execution.** Forget check-in loops entirely. What if the product did only one thing: every morning, it shows you the next moment when you might act on your habit and asks you to *imagine yourself doing it* — not to commit, not to execute, but to vividly rehearse the decision moment. That's it. No streaks, no notifications, no data export. The behavioral intervention is pre-decision visualization, which research shows improves follow-through more reliably than any tracking system. You measure success not by engagement but by whether people report they acted, weeks later, without the app's prompting.

* **Create a decision-rehearsal minimum product.** Strip away everything except two screens: (1) a habit statement, and (2) a single question: "What would it feel like to do this right now?" Let users sit with that question, rehearse the feeling, and leave. No logging, no proof, no history. Come back tomorrow with the same question. The retention metric isn't engagement — it's whether the user later reports taking the action, unprompted, in real life.

* **The minimum loop is pre-commitment reflection, not post-action recording.** Instead of gamifying completion, gamify decision-readiness. Before each opportunity, the product asks "How ready are you to act?" on a scale; if the user reports low readiness, the app suggests postponing the habit setup entirely. The minimum viable intervention is helping people discover whether they actually want to change *before* they log their first action.
