# The Devil's Advocate responds to Topic Cluster 07

## 1. Ritual, Feature, or Buried Screen

* **The keystone habit assumption may be backwards.** You're betting that if users see the review, they'll do it and it'll stabilize their habit stack. But what if the review only works *after* habits are already sticky? Force a review when a user has three broken streaks and they'll skip it—it becomes avoidance, not foundation. Have we considered whether the review should only unlock after users have established momentum? The buried-vs-weak hypothesis is testable: pull competitor data on when users *do* engage reviews (day 14? day 60?) and whether reviewers actually retain habits longer than non-reviewers.

* **Recovery design is critical but absent.** You mention the absence destabilizes everything downstream, but your spec doesn't address what actually happens when someone misses a Sunday. Does the app nudge them to catch up, or does Tuesday's view show last week's data as stale? Without explicit recovery mechanics, you're building a house of cards where one lapse breeds another.

## 2. The Review as Mirror, Not Report

* **The self-compassion framing is culturally loaded.** Some users *thrive* on accountability spreadsheets—it's not a bug for them. Others will find any reflection triggering. You can't design away this variance with language alone. Have we considered user segmentation: offering both "growth scorecard" and "reflection ritual" templates so users pick the mode that matches their psychology rather than forcing everyone through one emotional framing?

* **Showing data without interpretation is incomplete.** A mirror reflects what's there without judgment, but raw numbers aren't mirrors—they're judgments waiting to happen. If the review just shows missed days without context, you're still hitting perfectionists with failure. What if the review *required* a user to write one sentence about *why* a habit lapsed before closing? That transforms the spreadsheet into actual reflection.

## 3. The Sunday Evening Ritual (Aspirational)

* **Aspiration ≠ Design.** Users love lots of things they don't do. The question is why they'd prefer a 3-minute review in *your* app over scrolling, resting, or literally anything else on Sunday evening. Have we identified what competing activity the review displaces? Sunday evening is prime decompression time—you're asking people to do introspection when they want to disengage. What if the review was Monday morning instead, when people are already in a planning mindset?

* **"Look forward to" is vague and risky.** If it works, great. If it doesn't and users dread it, you've built a reminder that they're failing. There's no middle ground—it either becomes a cherished ritual or a weekly guilt trigger. What's your contingency if the review lands in the middle, where 60% of users skip it and the rest resent it?

## 4. The Sunday Ritual (Narrative)

* **Narrative design doesn't survive contact with exhaustion.** Your moment-by-moment experience assumes a user with mental energy and goodwill toward self-reflection. That's not Sunday evening for most people. Have we prototyped this with users who are actually tired, stressed, or dealing with a week that went sideways? A reflective three minutes might stretch to thirty minutes of spiraling, or collapse to a perfunctory checkbox. What specific UX safeguards prevent the review from becoming a dread cycle rather than a grounding one?

* **The "look forward to" premise needs evidence.** Show me competitor data on users who've sustained weekly review habits beyond month 3. What do they report? Is it actually the review itself, or the relief that comes from deciding to *not* do something else? If we're designing the experience but can't articulate why users would return to it, we're designing theatre, not habit architecture.
