# The Audience Advocate on Topic Cluster 07: The Weekly Review as Keystone Habit

## Question 1: Ritual, feature, or buried screen—what is the review actually doing?

* **Surface prominence vs. interaction quality aren't enough frames.** From the user's perspective, a buried review screen *feels* like homework, but so does an un-buried one that delivers shame instead of reflection. The real question isn't "where is it?" but "what does it do for me emotionally and practically?" Many users who struggle with perfectionism won't use a review feature at all if it becomes another place to perform failure. Before committing to the Sunday review as differentiation, talk directly to people who've skipped reviews in other apps—not about the app's features, but about the moment they decided *not* to open it. That honesty will tell you whether you're solving a visibility problem or a core motivation problem that no placement can fix.

## Question 2: The review as mirror, not report

* **Self-compassion in design is invisible to developers but everything to users.** A scorecard review triggers shame spirals in exactly the people who need habit support most—those with history of perfectionism, depression, or chronic shame. These users experience data about themselves as judgment. A mirror-based review asks "What's working? What felt hard? What do I want to try next?" without a final grade. Structurally: show trends (not failures), frame difficulty as insight (not shortcoming), and let users add their own context ("I missed this because...") so the app never positions itself as the arbiter of their "score." Emotionally, the language matters—"Let's look at this together" beats "Here's your stats." This distinction costs nothing technically but separates a tool that supports actual people from a productivity theater that looks good on paper.

## Question 3: The aspirational Sunday ritual—what would users actually say?

* **Users who look forward to the review are experiencing a moment of relief, not achievement.** They're not saying "I crushed my goals"—that's the fantasy version. Real users say things like "It helped me see what's really possible," or "I actually felt less guilty afterward," or "It reminded me why I started." The experience they'd evangelize is the one that feels lighter afterward, that reframes a rough week as information rather than failure. This requires an app that normalizes struggling—celebrates the user who showed up three days but also *noticed* they were overwhelmed and adjusted their expectations. When a user leaves a weekly review thinking "Okay, I understand what I'm dealing with," they'll do it again. The ritual they choose isn't the one that measures them; it's the one that knows them.

## Question 4: The Sunday ritual—what does it actually feel like moment to moment?

* **The experience of opening the review should feel different from checking stats.** A user sits down Sunday evening, a bit tired or reflective, and the app asks something genuinely curious: "What surprised you this week?" (not "How many days did you complete?"). They spend two minutes writing a sentence or two about what worked, what was hard, what they learned. The app listens without ranking them. Then they see a gentle visual reflection—maybe a small story their data tells, or a pattern they didn't notice. The whole thing takes three minutes and they close it feeling *slightly* more aware of themselves, not graded. The design difference: invite honesty over performance, curiosity over judgment, and give the user full ownership of their interpretation. That's the ritual people return to because it treats them like a person, not a productivity metric.
