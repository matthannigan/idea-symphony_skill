# Safety as Lived Experience - The Pragmatist

---

## Hazard Surface Inventory at Child's-Eye View

* **Do a literal floor-crawl 24 hours before the party.** Get on your hands and knees in every room the children will use. You will find things you missed standing up: electrical cords running under tables, decorative moon rocks or star-shaped confetti on the floor, balloon ribbon tangles at knee height, tablecloth corners that trail low enough to grab. Write them down, fix them before guests arrive. This takes 20 minutes and catches 80% of hazards.

* **Group hazards into three removal tiers: fix now, contain, or eliminate the activity.** Sharp corners on furniture get foam bumpers from a hardware store — a practical approach that takes 10 minutes and costs under $5. Small space-prop pieces (plastic astronaut helmets, mini rockets) get moved to a high shelf or removed entirely from the party area. Any activity requiring adult direct contact — like a craft using scissors — gets timed to when you have an adult stationed at that table. Don't try to supervise everything simultaneously; batch the risky activities into windows.

* **Balloons are the highest-priority choking risk and also the most common space-party decoration — pick one role for them.** A practical approach might be: keep balloons at ceiling level only (attached to weights, not handed to children), which removes the popped-fragment choking risk while keeping the visual. Hand-held balloons for 5-year-olds in a group of 11 will get popped, chewed, and dropped within 20 minutes. Decide before the party, not during.

* **Make a one-page hazard checklist you can run through in 5 minutes the morning of the party.** Columns: location, hazard type, mitigation. A checklist is more reliable than memory when you're also managing food, guests, and a 5-year-old's excitement. Template it once, use it every time you host. Include: loose cords, small objects on low surfaces, balloon height, door latches on rooms you want closed, bathroom access path clear of tripping hazards.

## Dietary and Allergy Realities

* **Put a direct allergy question on the RSVP — not buried in the invitation text, but a labeled field.** Whether you use a paper RSVP, a digital form (Google Form takes 5 minutes to set up), or a text message reply, ask explicitly: "Does your child have any food allergies, dietary restrictions, or sensory food preferences we should know about?" Vague phrasing like "any special needs?" misses half the responses. Parents answer direct questions; they overlook open-ended ones.

* **A practical approach to day-of disclosure: have one safe fallback food item already set aside.** Before the party, designate a small portion of the cake or a substitute treat (fruit cups are a reliable universal option) that you've confirmed contains none of the top eight allergens. If a parent discloses an allergy at drop-off, you have something to offer immediately without scrambling. This costs almost nothing and eliminates the moment where you're improvising in front of a child who can't eat what everyone else has.

* **Confirm allergen information two days before, not day-of.** After RSVPs close, send a single follow-up message to all confirmed families: "We're finalizing food — please reply by [date] if your child has allergies we should know about." Two days gives you time to adjust the menu or make a grocery run. Day-of disclosure leaves you with no options. Frame it as a reminder, not a hassle, and most parents will respond.

* **Keep the food menu simple enough that you can actually track ingredients.** A space-themed party doesn't need elaborate food to land — "astronaut ice cream" (freeze-dried, widely available), simple sandwiches cut into rocket shapes, and store-bought cupcakes from a single known bakery give you a short, auditable ingredient list. Every additional dish you add is one more allergen variable to track. To make allergy management feasible, constrain the menu first.

## Supervision Blind Spots and Self-Limiting Activities

* **Map the three highest-risk transition moments in your party flow and assign a named adult to each one.** The three that consistently produce incidents in groups of young children are: (1) the shift from structured activity to free play, when children scatter; (2) bathroom breaks, where the path to the bathroom is unsupervised; (3) the move from indoors to any outdoor space. For each, write down who is covering it before the party starts. "Everyone keep an eye out" means no one is specifically watching.

* **Design outdoor or active games to have a hard physical boundary, not a rule-based one.** A rule-based limit ("don't run past the fence") requires enforcement. A physical one (play area is inside a defined circle marked with rope or chalk) is self-explanatory to a 5-year-old. For jumping activities, a single crash mat or a marked landing zone works the same way. Spend 10 minutes setting up physical boundaries before guests arrive rather than issuing corrections during the party.

* **Bathroom breaks should be a scheduled group event, not ad hoc.** At 60-minute intervals (or before and after each major activity), announce a bathroom break for anyone who needs one. An adult walks the group to the bathroom and back. This eliminates the scenario where a child wanders away from the party looking for the bathroom and no one notices for several minutes. It also reduces the number of mid-activity interruptions.

* **For activities where children test limits — jumping, throwing — build in a natural stopping mechanism rather than relying on verbal instruction.** A practical approach: use consumable inputs. A craft where each child gets exactly 10 stickers ends when the stickers are gone. A ball-toss game where each child gets 5 throws ends naturally. Consumable inputs create self-pacing without requiring an adult to monitor continuously. Pair this with clear visual signals (a basket empties, a mat fills) so children can see the end point themselves.
