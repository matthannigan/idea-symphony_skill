# Brainstorming Questions from The Questioner: Community Tool Lending Library

---

## Hidden Assumptions About Who This Is For

1. **The assumed borrower**: Why do we assume the primary beneficiaries are homeowners doing home improvement projects — and what does it mean for the library's design if renters, who may be the majority in an urban neighborhood, have fundamentally different tool needs and usage patterns?

2. **The definition of "tool"**: What if the library's implicit mental model of "tools" (hammers, drills, ladders) is already excluding categories of equipment — gardening gear, cooking appliances, party/event supplies, art materials — that would better serve the actual breadth of the community's needs?

3. **Walking distance as equity**: The REQUEST describes ~2,000 households within walking distance, but who is that walk actually accessible to — and what does access look like for households with mobility constraints, young children, or irregular work schedules that don't match storefront hours?

---

## What Sustainability Actually Requires

4. **The enthusiasm cliff**: The REQUEST names "sustainability beyond initial enthusiasm" as a key concern — but what specific mechanisms, structures, or incentives distinguish a library that survives that first-year enthusiasm drop from one that quietly closes, and have we thought carefully enough about which of those mechanisms are actually built into this plan?

5. **The replacement cost trap**: When a tool breaks or wears out, who decides whether to repair or replace it, who authorizes the spending, and where does that money reliably come from year after year — and what happens if that answer is "we'll figure it out when it happens"?

6. **Volunteer burnout as a design constraint**: With 8–10 core volunteers, how much operational load can the library actually sustain without burning people out — and what would it mean to design the entire system around that ceiling rather than hoping volunteer capacity will grow?

---

## The Liability and Trust Problem

7. **The assumption that rules prevent problems**: Why do we assume that a liability waiver and safety policy will adequately manage risk — and what if the real liability exposure comes not from formal borrowing agreements but from informal loans, word-of-mouth sharing, or tools that are returned in worse condition than anyone checked?

8. **Trust asymmetry**: What if the communities that most need equitable access — households without established neighborhood relationships, recent immigrants, residents who distrust institutions — are also the least likely to participate in a lending system that requires identity verification, deposits, or other trust-building friction?

---

## The Storefront as Both Asset and Constraint

9. **What the space shapes**: The donated storefront is framed as an asset, but in what ways does a fixed physical location constrain how the library could operate — and are there hybrid or satellite models that would actually serve a ~2,000 household radius better than a single 500 sq ft space?

10. **The landlord dependency**: Why do we assume the donated space is a stable foundation rather than a conditional one — and what contingency does the library have if the landlord's circumstances change, the donation lapses, or terms shift after the library is established?

---

## Community Buy-In as a Design Question, Not a Communications Task

11. **Who isn't in the room**: The 8–10 committed volunteers presumably share some demographic or social characteristics — and who in the neighborhood's diverse mix of homeowners, renters, and cultural communities is not yet represented in that founding group, and how does their absence shape what gets built?

12. **The difference between outreach and co-design**: What if "building community buy-in" is being framed primarily as a communications challenge when it's actually a governance and ownership challenge — and what would it look like to give the community genuine decision-making power over the library rather than just inviting them to use it?

13. **Reciprocity as an alternative to charity**: Have we considered what changes if the library is built around a reciprocity or mutual-aid model — where borrowing obligates some contribution back — rather than a service model where the nonprofit provides and the community receives?
