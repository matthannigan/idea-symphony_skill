# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Community Tool Lending Library

---

## Who Actually Shows Up — and Who Doesn't

1. **The invisible non-user**: Who are the community members who would benefit most from this library but will never walk through the door on their own — and what specific barriers (language, hours, physical mobility, distrust of institutions, lack of awareness) would keep them away even if they knew it existed?

2. **The renter experience**: Renters often have the greatest need for tools but the fewest long-term attachments to place. How might the library's membership model, communication channels, and physical location feel different to a renter who may move in six months versus a homeowner who has lived on the block for twenty years?

3. **First-time borrower anxiety**: Think about the emotional journey of someone who has never borrowed from a community library before — what do they think when they approach the storefront, what do they feel when they ask for help with something they've never used, and what would make them come back a second time rather than quietly deciding it wasn't for them?

---

## Dignity, Power, and Who Gets to Define Success

4. **Designed with vs. designed for**: How will community members — especially those with the least institutional access — have meaningful input into which tools are stocked, what the hours are, and how the space feels? What mechanisms ensure their voices shape the library rather than simply validate decisions already made by volunteers?

5. **The dignity of borrowing**: For some community members, asking to borrow something carries a sense of vulnerability or stigma. How might the library's framing, physical environment, and staff interactions be designed so that borrowing feels like a smart, proud community act rather than an admission of need?

6. **Who defines "equitable access"**: Equity is often declared as a goal but measured by whoever runs the program. What would it look like to let the community define what equitable access means to them — and how would you know if you were achieving it by their standard rather than yours?

---

## Practical Barriers Hidden in Plain Sight

7. **The scheduling gap**: A borrower who works two jobs or provides childcare often can't visit during daytime hours or plan borrowing days in advance. How might the library's hours, reservation system, and pickup/return process be designed around the schedules of the people with the least scheduling flexibility — not the most?

8. **Language and literacy**: In a diverse urban neighborhood, some residents may not be comfortable with English-language signage, written borrowing agreements, or digital registration systems. What does a genuinely multilingual and low-literacy-friendly experience look like at every touchpoint, from discovery through return?

9. **Tool literacy, not just tool access**: Owning a drill is only useful if you know how to use it safely. How might the library address the gap between access to tools and confidence to use them — and how would it avoid assuming that people who borrow a tool already have the knowledge they need?

---

## The Emotional Arc Over Time

10. **From stranger to regular**: What is the experience curve of a first-time borrower becoming a committed library member — where are the moments of delight, confusion, friction, or dropout, and what would it take to design each transition deliberately?

11. **When something goes wrong**: A tool breaks during a borrowing period, or a community member is accused of damaging something they didn't damage. How might the library handle these moments in a way that preserves the borrower's dignity and the community relationship, rather than making them feel like suspects or liabilities?

12. **The quiet dropout**: People who stop using a community resource rarely complain — they simply disappear. How will the library notice when members disengage and learn what happened, especially among groups that are least likely to fill out a feedback form or speak up at a meeting?

---

## Community Agency and Long-Term Belonging

13. **Ownership vs. participation**: There is a difference between a community that uses a resource and a community that feels ownership over it. What would it take for residents — not just volunteers — to see this library as theirs, to defend it, to recruit their neighbors, and to shape its future when the founding energy fades?
