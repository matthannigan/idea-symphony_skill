# Tool Inventory, Demand, and the Commons - The Pragmatist

---

## Inventory Selection Under Space and Budget Constraints

* **Start with usage data from peer libraries.** Before speculating about what your community needs, contact 3-5 established tool libraries (Berkeley Tool Lending Library, SF Tool Library, Boston Builds Back) and ask them to share anonymized usage data for their first year. What actually got borrowed? What sat on shelves? This beats internal projections because you're learning from real patterns, not assumptions. Budget ~20 hours to compile this data and run a quick survey of your core volunteers about what they see neighbor needs as.

* **Apply the 80/20 rule ruthlessly to initial selection.** With 500 sq ft and modest budget, stock approximately 60-80 core tools that you predict will account for 80% of borrowing activity: cordless drill (multiple condition tiers), multiple types of saws, leveling tools, pipe wrenches, circular saw, sanders. Skip the rarely-used specialty tools (tile cutters, power washers, scaffolding) for year two. Calculate replacement cost per tool, then work backward: if a drill costs $80 and lasts 2 years with active use, budget for two replacement drills per year in your operating costs before you buy the first one.

* **Tier tools by season and volunteer capacity.** Spring/garden tools (shovels, rakes, hoes, pruners, soil testing kits) can be stored densely and borrowed in predictable waves March-May. Fall/weatherization tools (caulk guns, weatherstripping, draft stoppers) peak October-November. With 8-10 volunteers, you have roughly 16-20 volunteer hours per week available. Keep this tier simple: swap 30% of inventory seasonally, don't maintain dual inventories. Plan staffing around these peaks three months in advance.

* **Use spreadsheet tracking from day one.** Before you buy a single tool, set up a simple Google Sheet with columns: tool name, quantity, acquisition cost, expected lifespan (months), replacement cost, storage area, seasonal-peak months. Update it after 90 days of operation with actual borrowing frequency and damage rates. This keeps you grounded in real costs instead of abstract inventory theory. The data also tells you which tools to cull when budget is tight.

## Tragedy of the Commons: Reservation Windows and Leading Indicators

* **Implement a three-day maximum checkout window with a one-week hold queue.** Borrowers keep tools for three days; then they're due back. If a tool is requested while checked out, it goes to the next person in queue. This aligns individual incentive (I can get it soon) with collective incentive (the tool stays in circulation). Three days is long enough for most home projects (simple repairs, small builds), but short enough that popular tools cycle through 2-3 people per week instead of accumulating on one shelf. The one-week hold prevents queue-jumping and makes the wait visible and bounded.

* **Track two leading indicators: queue depth and damage-per-return.** Every Friday, tally the number of outstanding holds per tool. If a tool has 3+ holds, it's experiencing scarcity pressure. That's your warning flag, not the lagging damage reports from three weeks ago. Run a second dashboard metric: damage reports per 10 checkouts. If it spikes above 1 in 10 (one damaged tool per 10 returns), people are rushing or not caring for gear because they assume it's scarce and hard to access. Both metrics are measurable by month two and tell you whether the incentive structure is working.

* **Use social visibility to shift behavior before enforcement kicks in.** Post a simple chart in the library: "Most borrowed this month: cordless drill (47 checkouts, 3 holds), level (32 checkouts, 1 hold), circular saw (18 checkouts, 5 holds)." People see the wait queue for popular tools and self-ration before you implement penalties. Pair this with a low-friction late-return penalty: $2 per day overdue, capped at replacement cost. Make it automatic (recorded in the checkout spreadsheet) so staff don't have to confront members. The social visibility does 80% of the work; the small fee catches the remaining 20%.

* **Create a "borrow-to-donate" incentive for high-demand tools.** If a member returns a cordless drill in good condition for three consecutive months, offer them a $10 store credit or skip a late fee. This rewards consistent, careful use of the tools under greatest strain. Inversely, if someone returns a tool damaged, they go on a 30-day "replacement only" list: they can only borrow tools that are in duplicate or triplicate, never the sole copy. This is transparent, automatic, and doesn't require staff judgment.

## Forced Curation: Choosing 40 Tools from 800

* **Use the "members would pay for" test.** Imagine you had to charge $0.50 per borrow to break even on replacements. Which 40 tools would members actually pay to borrow, or put on a list, or advocate for to friends? Skip the specialty power tools (tile saws, concrete mixers, scaffolding) that are niche. Keep multiples of bread-and-butter tools: drills, saws, levels, wrenches, sanders. This reveals that a tool library is essentially renting the tools that would otherwise sit idle in people's garages 80% of the time. The 40 you keep are the ones that solve the "I need it for 2 hours but don't want to buy it or borrow a friend's" problem.

* **Weight by storage footprint and maintenance burden.** A power drill takes up 0.1 sq ft and can be maintained (battery swap, cord check) in 2 minutes. A table saw takes up 3 sq ft, requires dust collection setup, and is a liability if someone misuses it. With 500 sq ft and limited volunteer time for maintenance, choose tools that return high utility per square foot and low maintenance overhead. A coping saw (manual, $15) solves many problems and takes almost no space. A full workshop of stationary power tools is a commitment you probably can't sustain.

* **What the 40 reveals about the library's actual purpose.** If you end up with mostly hand tools and small portable power tools, the library is about equitable access to occasional-use items and lowering the barrier to home maintenance. If you push toward larger tools, it's positioning as a workshop community hub. Both are valid, but one requires more space, training, insurance, and ongoing staff investment. Be explicit: with 500 sq ft and 8-10 volunteers, you are not a full workshop. You're a curated rental service for the highest-leverage tools. That's not a limitation — it's a focus.
