# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Community Tool Lending Library

---

## Who Actually Shows Up — and Who Doesn't

1. **Invisible non-users**: The people who never walk through the door are arguably more important to understand than early adopters — what are the specific barriers (awareness, hours, language, social comfort, distrust of institutions) that would keep the most tool-needy households from ever becoming members, and how would we know we're reaching them vs. congratulating ourselves on serving the already-connected?

2. **The renter experience**: Homeowners and renters have fundamentally different relationships to tools — renters may face restrictions on what modifications they can make, may have less storage for borrowed items, and may be more transient — so how does the library design account for the fact that for many renters, the value proposition is genuinely different from what homeowners need?

3. **First visit emotional journey**: Map the emotional arc of a first-time visitor who arrived with low confidence or high need: what are they feeling when they walk in uncertain about what they're doing, what moment in the interaction is most likely to make them feel judged or unwelcome, and what would need to be true for them to leave feeling respected and likely to return?

4. **Language and literacy access**: In a diverse urban neighborhood, what proportion of potential members may be more comfortable in a language other than English, or may struggle with written forms and instructions — and are the membership process, signage, tool instructions, and communication channels designed to work for them, or do they quietly exclude?

---

## Dignity, Agency, and Power in the Experience

5. **Borrower dignity at the counter**: The moment of borrowing can carry implicit power dynamics — a person asking for something from a volunteer who controls access — so what specifically about the interaction design (language used, forms required, deposits asked, questions posed) might inadvertently signal that the borrower is being evaluated or is less trusted, and how would we redesign those moments to affirm agency?

6. **Accountability without surveillance**: Loan tracking, damage deposits, and overdue policies are operationally necessary, but from the community member's perspective they can feel like surveillance or an assumption of bad faith — so how do we design these systems so that the people with the least financial cushion (who may fear fines or loss of a deposit) don't self-select out?

7. **Who designs the rules**: The 8-10 volunteers will likely draft the policies, hours, tool list, and membership terms — but these reflect the volunteers' circumstances, literacy levels, and implicit norms — so what mechanisms would ensure that the people the library is meant to serve have genuine influence over these decisions, not just a comment box after the fact?

---

## Practical Access Barriers That Go Unstated

8. **Hours of operation and work schedules**: A tool library open weekday afternoons serves a fundamentally different population than one open weekend mornings or evenings — and the people most likely to need tools (those doing their own home repair to save money) are often those with the least schedule flexibility — so who are we actually making this available to with whatever hours we choose, and whose work lives does that schedule assume?

9. **Transportation and load-carrying**: "Walking distance" means different things across ability, age, and whether you're also carrying a 30-pound tile saw — so what do we actually know about how community members would physically get tools home and return them, and are there members of the community for whom the physical logistics of borrowing make it effectively inaccessible regardless of hours or membership terms?

10. **What people don't ask for**: Community members often won't request a tool category they've never had access to and therefore don't think of as available to them — so beyond surveying existing demand, how does the library proactively surface possibilities ("did you know you could borrow X to do Y?") in ways that expand what people imagine is possible for them, particularly for renters or people new to home repair?

---

## Cultural Fit and Belonging

11. **Whose community norms govern the space**: A shared physical space in a diverse neighborhood carries implicit cultural norms about how to interact, what counts as proper use, how conflict is handled, and who is a "good borrower" — so whose norms will implicitly govern this space when the volunteers are the ones running it, and how do we prevent the library from feeling like a space that works naturally for some neighbors and awkwardly for others?

12. **Trust-building with historically under-served groups**: Some residents may have prior experiences with nonprofit or government services that felt paternalistic, required invasive documentation, or didn't deliver — so what does the library need to do differently in its early interactions specifically to earn trust with community members who have reason to be skeptical, rather than assuming good intentions will speak for themselves?

---

## Long-Term Community Experience

13. **What success feels like from the inside**: The organization may measure success through loan volume, membership counts, and financial sustainability — but from the community's perspective, success might mean something different: feeling like the library is genuinely theirs, that it reflects their needs, that they'd defend it if it were threatened — so how would we know if the library has achieved that kind of community ownership, and are we designing for it from the start?
