# Brainstorming Questions from The Questioner: Community Tool Lending Library

---

## What Is This Library Actually For?

1. **Hidden purpose**: Why do we assume the primary value this library creates is access to tools — and not something else, like a recurring reason for neighbors to interact with each other or a visible symbol of shared ownership in the neighborhood?

2. **Defining success on whose terms**: What if the 8-10 volunteers and the ~2,000 households have fundamentally different ideas of what "success" looks like for this library — and what happens to the design if we surface that tension early rather than after launch?

3. **The problem beneath the problem**: Why do we assume that tool access is the actual barrier people face when undertaking home projects? What if the real barriers are knowledge, time, confidence, or simply not knowing neighbors who could help — and how would that change what this library should be?

4. **Renter vs. homeowner framing**: The REQUEST notes a mix of homeowners and renters. Why do we assume both groups want the same things from a tool library, and what would the design look like if we started from the renter's perspective rather than the homeowner's?

---

## Are We Asking the Right Questions About Sustainability?

5. **Sustainability's unstated definition**: When we say "sustainability beyond initial enthusiasm," what exactly are we trying to sustain — the organization's finances, the volunteer community, the physical inventory, the membership base, or the library's cultural relevance? Have we considered that these might require different strategies that could conflict?

6. **What if enthusiasm never fades**: Why do we assume enthusiasm is the fragile variable here? What if the harder sustainability challenge is not keeping volunteers motivated but keeping the library physically functional as tools wear out — and what does a design look like that treats replacement cost as a first-class constraint from day one?

7. **The model we're not questioning**: Have we considered why this is being designed as a lending library rather than a different model — a shared workspace, a tool co-op with member ownership, or a pay-what-you-can repair cafe — and what assumptions about the community are embedded in the lending library choice?

8. **Who sustains the sustainers**: The library depends on 8-10 volunteers, but what sustains those volunteers' willingness to keep showing up? Have we asked what they personally need from this project — recognition, community, skill-building, something else — and how the design could meet those needs rather than just relying on their goodwill?

---

## What Do We Assume About "Equitable Access"?

9. **Equitable by what measure**: What if "equitable access" means very different things to different community members — free membership for low-income households, multilingual materials, hours that work for shift workers, tools selected for the kinds of projects renters can actually do — and what happens if we try to optimize for all of them simultaneously?

10. **Who is hardest to reach**: Why do we assume that physical proximity (walking distance) and open doors are sufficient to make the library genuinely accessible? What would we learn if we asked the households least likely to use the library — not just the most enthusiastic early adopters — what would actually need to change for them to show up?

11. **The invisible barrier of knowing**: What if the largest equity gap isn't tool access but tool knowledge — and what would the library become if we treated "how to use this tool safely" as part of what the library lends, alongside the tool itself?

12. **Membership as a gatekeeping mechanism**: Have we considered that any membership system, even a free one, creates friction that disproportionately affects the people most likely to benefit from the library? What would a design look like that assumed no formal enrollment at all?

---

## What Are We Not Seeing About Risk and Liability?

13. **Whose definition of risk**: The REQUEST flags "liability and safety concerns" as a consideration — but liability to whom, and for what? Why do we assume the primary risk is a member injuring themselves with a borrowed tool, rather than risks like volunteer burnout, tool theft, neighborhood conflict over access, or reputational harm to the nonprofit if something goes wrong publicly?

14. **The unasked safety question**: What if safety isn't primarily a liability-management problem but a design problem — and what would the library look like if we treated tool condition and user readiness as the core safety levers, rather than waivers and rules?

15. **What if we imagined a worst-case failure**: What would it look like if the library failed badly — not just quietly wound down, but failed in a way that damaged the nonprofit's standing or fractured community relationships? What assumptions in the current design would we want to revisit if we took that scenario seriously?

---

## What Possibilities Are We Foreclosing Before We Start?

16. **The storefront assumption**: We have a 500 sq ft storefront, so we're designing around a storefront — but why do we assume a fixed physical location is the best model for distributing tools across a neighborhood? What would a neighborhood-distributed or hub-and-spoke model make possible that a single storefront cannot?

17. **What if the tool library made money**: Why do we assume this is purely a cost center that needs to minimize expenses? What if certain services — tool sharpening, small repairs, project consultations — could generate enough revenue to fund tool replacement, and how would that change what the library does and who it attracts?

18. **The neighbor-to-neighbor alternative**: What if the community already has informal tool-sharing networks among neighbors that this library might compete with or disrupt rather than complement? Have we considered how the library could strengthen those existing relationships rather than replace them with an institutional system?
