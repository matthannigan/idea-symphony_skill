# Brainstorming Questions from The Devil's Advocate: Community Tool Lending Library

---

## Sustainability Beyond the Launch Window

1. **Founder dependence**: Have we considered what happens when the 2–3 most engaged founding volunteers burn out or move away — and if we have no succession plan, does the library survive beyond year two? What structures (documented roles, cross-training, rotating leadership) could prevent the whole operation from resting on a few heroic individuals?

2. **Enthusiasm half-life**: The initial launch will likely draw a surge of donations, borrowers, and volunteers — but what happens in month eight when novelty fades? Have we modeled what "steady-state" participation actually looks like, and designed incentives that sustain it rather than just ignite it?

3. **Replacement cost time bomb**: Tool lending inherently degrades inventory over time through wear, loss, and damage. Have we accounted for a realistic annual replacement budget — not just acquisition costs — and does the funding model cover it, or does the library slowly hollow out its own collection?

---

## Liability, Safety, and Legal Exposure

4. **Injury attribution**: One potential challenge is that when a borrower injures themselves using a library tool, the chain of liability is murky — was the tool defective, improperly maintained, or misused? Have we consulted with an attorney about whether a waiver-plus-inspection protocol meaningfully limits the nonprofit's legal exposure, or does it just create a false sense of coverage?

5. **Uninsured damage scenarios**: If a borrowed ladder falls through a renter's window or a borrowed drill damages a wall, who pays? Have we mapped out the realistic damage scenarios — not just catastrophic ones — and verified whether the nonprofit's existing insurance covers third-party property damage caused by lent tools?

6. **Safety certification gap**: Some tools (chainsaws, pressure washers, electrical equipment) carry real injury risk for untrained operators. Have we considered whether the library has an obligation to verify borrower competency before lending high-risk items, and what happens if we don't and someone is hurt?

---

## Equity and Access in Practice

7. **Credential barriers disguised as policy**: Requiring a government-issued ID, a deposit, or a credit card to borrow can quietly exclude the residents who most need the library — renters without stable addresses, undocumented neighbors, or people without bank accounts. Have we stress-tested each access requirement against the actual demographics of the neighborhood to ensure "equitable access" is real and not rhetorical?

8. **The digital divide in operations**: If borrowing, reservations, and communication rely primarily on a website or app, we may inadvertently favor tech-comfortable residents and shut out older adults or those without smartphones. What is the analog fallback, and is it treated as equally valid rather than as a backup of last resort?

9. **Language and literacy assumptions**: In a diverse urban neighborhood, library communications, waivers, and instructions may default to English-only literacy. Have we considered what it costs — in money and volunteer effort — to provide multilingual access, and what it costs in community trust if we don't?

---

## Governance, Conflict, and Decision-Making

10. **Missing conflict resolution protocol**: When a borrower returns a tool broken and disputes responsibility, or when a volunteer is accused of favoritism in lending decisions, who decides and how? Have we established a clear, written dispute resolution process before those conflicts arise — because they will arise?

11. **Scope creep under community pressure**: Community members will inevitably request tools the library doesn't stock, push for longer loan periods, or ask for delivery to homebound neighbors. Each accommodation seems reasonable in isolation but collectively could overwhelm volunteer capacity. Have we defined explicit boundaries for scope, and who has authority to enforce them?

---

## Operational Fragility and Single Points of Failure

12. **The key-holder problem**: If only one or two people can open the storefront, the library effectively closes whenever those individuals are unavailable. Have we mapped every operational dependency — physical access, inventory software, financial accounts — and ensured at least two people hold each critical capability?

13. **Inventory system collapse**: Manual spreadsheets and paper logs fail under volume and volunteer turnover — a borrowed tool goes unrecorded, and weeks later no one knows where it is. Have we evaluated what happens to inventory integrity during the inevitable periods of reduced volunteer coverage, and is there a system resilient enough to survive those gaps without creating a loss spiral?
