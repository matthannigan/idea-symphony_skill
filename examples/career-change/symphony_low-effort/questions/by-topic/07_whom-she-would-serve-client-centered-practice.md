# Topic Cluster 07: Whom She Would Serve — Client-Centered Practice

## Questions

1. **What immigrant and refugee clients actually experience**: When clients walk into a legal aid office, what unspoken fears and logistical barriers do they bring — distrust of institutions, language barriers, fear of documentation, somatic dimensions of distress — and how would a practitioner with clinical training be positioned to address the emotional and bodily dimensions of that distress in ways a purely legally-trained attorney might not? How does nursing experience with patient history-taking — drawing out information from frightened, non-verbal, or traumatized people — transfer into legal intake and case-building when immigration cases hinge on details clients omit because they don't realize they're relevant?
2. **Cultural and linguistic access gaps**: From the community's perspective, what languages, cultural frameworks, and communication styles matter most for immigrant and refugee populations in this geographic area — and does she have existing fluency or community relationships in those contexts, or would she be entering as an outsider who still needs to build that trust?
3. **Dignity, agency, and the patient-to-client power shift**: Immigration law often places clients in passive positions — waiting, complying, deferring — which for trauma survivors can re-traumatize. What would it mean to practice in a way that actively restores client agency, and what does that approach require in time, methodology, and institutional culture?
4. **What ICU patients lose when she leaves the bedside**: She has been a de facto advocate for immigrant patients in the ICU — someone who noticed when they had no legal support. The people she would serve as an attorney are different people from those she's helping now. What does her departure mean for the patients left behind, is there anyone positioned to fill that advocacy gap, or does it simply disappear? And what do those repeating ICU failures actually call her toward — a specific, repeating failure pattern, or a broader ethical orientation that immigration law in particular may or may not channel?
5. **Triage logic across disciplines**: In the ICU, triage determines who gets scarce resources under life-or-death pressure — a structurally identical problem to case prioritization in immigration law, where attorneys must decide which clients to take on against an overwhelming backlog and life-altering stakes. What does your existing fluency with clinical triage tell you about whether you could develop the same discipline in legal practice, and where the two systems diverge in ways that might surprise you?
6. **The face that changed everything**: Walk me through one specific patient encounter — the immigrant or refugee whose situation made you feel most powerless as a nurse. What were you watching happen that you couldn't fix with clinical care? And when you imagine yourself in that same room, three years from now, holding a law degree instead of a stethoscope — what changes, and what doesn't?
