# Topic Cluster 08: Family, Mentors, and the Felt Sense of the Decision

## Questions

1. **What "supportive but worried" actually means**: Family is described as supportive but thinking this is risky. What do they think, feel, say, and do when they imagine her pursuing this — financial concerns, social/identity status concerns, or wellbeing concerns? What would they need to see or understand to shift from worried-support to confident-support, what invisible labor of transition would they absorb across three years of law school, and does their opinion matter in ways she hasn't fully examined?
2. **Mentor fit and gatekept pathways**: Public interest immigration attorneys are themselves in a high-burnout field, often under-resourced, handling emotionally devastating caseloads. From that community's perspective, what qualities distinguish attorneys who sustain this work for years from those who burn out, and does her profile fit that pattern? Law school culture rewards a particular kind of preparation; what does a non-traditional 32-year-old entrant need to do differently to build relationships, access clinical programs, and find placement — and are those pathways genuinely accessible or informally gatekept?
3. **What she feels, not what she analyzes**: Burned out *and* deeply committed can co-exist, but they can also be in tension — burnout can narrow empathy even as commitment remains intellectually intact. What does she actually feel when she imagines herself in an ICU in five years versus a courtroom in five years — not what she thinks she should feel, but what shows up? What is she not saying about what she needs from her work life that may be as important as the cause she's drawn toward, and who does she want to be at 42 — does the attorney identity she's imagining match her values and temperament, or is it an idealized projection?
4. **What sustainable advocacy actually requires**: When you've seen people sustain long careers in demanding human-centered work — nursing, social work, advocacy — what seems to make the difference between burning out and burning brightly? Which of those conditions could you build into a legal career that you couldn't build into bedside nursing, and what personal, financial, professional, and relational conditions currently exist, which could be built, and which are genuinely uncertain?
5. **Two high-burnout systems, one person**: Nursing and public interest immigration law are structurally alike in their burnout profiles — both involve high emotional stakes, inadequate institutional resources, and populations in acute crisis — but they produce burnout through different mechanisms. ICU burnout often comes from moral injury around futile care and rapid death; immigration burnout often comes from systemic injustice, case backlogs, and clients living in prolonged limbo. What if we borrowed the concept of "burnout mapping" from occupational health research: does the specific burnout you're experiencing in nursing match the burnout signature of immigration law, or are you moving toward a different flavor of the same problem?
6. **The attorney's Tuesday**: Imagine it's a Tuesday, five years from now. You're a public interest immigration attorney. Walk me through your morning — the commute, the cases open on your desk, the client who calls before noon with a crisis. What does the emotional texture of that day feel like compared to a Tuesday in the ICU? Where do you feel relief, and where do you feel a new kind of weight?
