# Topic Cluster 03: Lifetime Economics, Loan Forgiveness, and Anchoring

## Questions

1. **Lifetime earnings delta and break-even timeline**: Public interest immigration salaries run $50–85K depending on geography and seniority versus your $82K nursing salary today, with experienced nursing reaching $95–120K+ via overtime and travel contracts. Across a 30-year horizon the lifetime earnings delta could exceed $500K–$1M before loan repayment. At what age and salary level does the law path break even with the nursing path in cumulative lifetime earnings, and how does the math change once PSLF forgiveness ($150–200K+ wiped after ten qualifying years) and geographic arbitrage are factored in?
2. **PSLF/LRAP as a load-bearing system dependency**: Your financial plan likely depends heavily on Public Service Loan Forgiveness plus law school loan repayment assistance programs. PSLF has structural fragility: narrow eligibility, employer qualification can change, historically high rejection rates, and program rules have shifted before. What happens to your financial picture if PSLF is modified, defunded, or your role doesn't qualify — and have you war-gamed the scenario where forgiveness doesn't materialize on schedule?
3. **Income-driven repayment cash flow at $60K**: On an IDR plan (e.g., SAVE) a $60K public interest salary might generate monthly student loan payments of $100–300 rather than the $1,500–2,000 a standard 10-year plan would require. What does your monthly cash flow actually look like at that starting salary after taxes, housing, and the residual $22K nursing loan balance — and is it livable in the cities where public interest immigration work is concentrated?
4. **Salary floor anchoring**: Your current $82K nursing salary, which feels like a ceiling in your burnout state, may function as a *floor* once you look back from a $58K public defender salary at 37. How does that reversal sit with your decade-out sense of financial identity, and how does carrying $200K+ in total debt change your career options or psychological headroom — including the risk of accepting a higher-paying corporate job to manage debt rather than staying in public interest work?
5. **What if you treated law school debt as purchasing a fixed block of future time?**: Imagine the opposite were true — instead of framing $150K+ in new debt as a financial burden, you reframe it as a prepaid purchase of seven to ten years of public interest work before financial pressure forces a pivot to private practice. Does that time feel worth the price? And here's the absurd flip: what if the question isn't whether you can afford to go to law school, but whether you can afford *not to*, given that the alternative is continuing to accumulate years in a field that's already cost you something irreplaceable?
