# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Career Change from ICU Nursing to Public Interest Law

---

## The Clients She Would Serve: Immigrant and Refugee Needs

1. **What immigrants and refugees actually experience vs. what advocates assume**: When immigrant and refugee clients walk into a legal aid office, what are the unspoken fears and logistical barriers they bring with them — distrust of institutions, language barriers, fear of documentation — and how would a practitioner with a clinical nursing background be positioned to address the emotional and somatic dimensions of that distress in ways that a purely legally-trained attorney might not?

2. **Cultural and linguistic access gaps**: From the community's perspective, what languages, cultural frameworks, and communication styles matter most for the immigrant and refugee populations in this geographic area — and does she have existing fluency or community relationships in those contexts, or would she be entering as an outsider who still needs to build that trust?

3. **What clients say they need vs. what they actually do**: Immigration legal cases often hinge on details that clients omit not because they're hiding them but because they don't realize they're relevant. How might her nursing experience with patient history-taking — drawing out information from frightened, non-verbal, or traumatized people — transfer into the legal intake and case-building process in ways that serve clients more deeply than standard legal interview techniques?

4. **Dignity and agency in the legal process**: Immigration law often places clients in a passive position — waiting, complying, deferring. People who've fled trauma or persecution may experience this loss of agency as a re-traumatization. What would it mean to practice law in a way that actively restores client agency, and what does that approach actually require of the attorney in terms of time, methodology, and institutional culture?

---

## Her Current ICU Patients: The Costs of Staying and the Cost of Leaving

5. **What immigrant ICU patients lose if she leaves bedside nursing**: She has already been a de facto advocate for immigrant patients in the ICU — someone who noticed when they had no legal support. The people she would serve as an attorney are different people than the ones she is currently helping. What does her departure from bedside nursing mean for the patients she leaves behind, and is there anyone positioned to fill that advocacy gap, or does it simply disappear?

6. **The emotional debt of witnessed suffering**: The people she has watched navigate "impossible situations with no legal support" in the ICU — what do those experiences actually call her toward? Is the felt pull toward law a response to specific, repeating failures in the system she witnessed, or is it a broader ethical orientation? Understanding which it is matters for whether immigration law in particular is the right channel, or whether policy, social work, or hybrid roles might address the same failures.

---

## Her Family and Personal Support Network: Stakeholder Perspectives Often Overlooked

7. **What "supportive but worried" actually means**: Her family is described as supportive but thinking this is risky. From an empathy-mapping perspective — what do they think, feel, say, and do when they imagine her pursuing this change? Are their concerns primarily financial, social (identity and status), or about her wellbeing? And critically: what would they need to see or understand to shift from worried-support to confident-support, and does their opinion matter to her in ways she hasn't fully examined?

8. **The invisible labor of transitions on people nearby**: Major career transitions don't happen in isolation — they redistribute emotional and practical labor onto the people around the person making the change. Who in her life absorbs the secondary stress of her burnout now, and who would absorb the secondary stress of three years of law school? What do those people actually need from her in terms of honesty, communication, and planning — and has she asked them?

---

## Future Legal Mentors and the Immigration Law Community

9. **What immigration law practitioners actually look like from the inside**: The people she imagines working alongside — public interest immigration attorneys — are themselves in a high-burnout field, often under-resourced, handling emotionally devastating caseloads with systemic constraints. From that community's perspective, what qualities distinguish attorneys who sustain this work for years from those who leave or burn out? And does her profile — someone entering with clinical empathy but without the legal socialization that comes from law school culture — fit the sustainable-practitioner pattern or present new risks?

10. **The mentorship gap for non-traditional law students**: Law school culture rewards a particular kind of preparation and professional identity. Someone entering at 32 with a clinical career background is not the typical law student. From the perspective of potential mentors in immigration law, what does a non-traditional entrant need to do differently to build relationships, access clinical programs, and find placement in public interest roles — and are those pathways genuinely accessible or informally gatekept?

---

## The User Herself: Centered as a Stakeholder in Her Own Transition

11. **The emotional journey of this decision, mapped**: What does her experience actually feel like right now — not her analysis of it, but her felt sense? She says she is burned out but deeply committed to serving vulnerable populations. Those two things can co-exist, but they can also be in tension: burnout can narrow empathy even as commitment remains intellectually intact. What does she feel when she imagines herself in an ICU in five years versus in a courtroom in five years — not what she thinks she should feel, but what actually shows up?

12. **Unarticulated needs she hasn't named yet**: What she says she wants is a career that combines legal advocacy for immigrants with meaningful work. But what she actually does — spending two years volunteering with refugee resettlement while maintaining a demanding clinical career — suggests something about her existing capacity for service, her need for variety across types of work, and her tolerance for high-demand contexts. What is she not saying about what she needs from her work life that might be as important as the cause she is drawn toward?

13. **Who she becomes on the other side**: Public interest law will ask her to internalize a different professional identity — to think like an attorney, to argue, to win or lose on behalf of clients, to operate within a legal system that produces outcomes she may not control. From the perspective of the person she is now, who does she want to be at 42, and does the attorney identity she is imagining match the values and temperament she actually holds — or is it an idealized projection of what justice-work could look like?
