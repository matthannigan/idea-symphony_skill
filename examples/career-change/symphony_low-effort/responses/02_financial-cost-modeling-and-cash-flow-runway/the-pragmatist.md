# Financial Cost Modeling And Cash-Flow Runway - The Pragmatist

---

## All-In Transition Cost Model Year By Year

* **Start with a bottom-up model, not a top-line estimate.** Tuition at a public law school in your state could run $25–35K/year versus $55–75K at a private institution; living costs vary from $18K (shared housing, low-cost market) to $40K+ (high-rent city). Before committing, build a spreadsheet with three columns — low, mid, high — for each year. The inputs you control most are school selectivity, in-state residency, and housing. Those three variables alone can swing total cost by $120–150K across three years.

* **Foregone income is the largest line item, and people consistently underweight it.** At $82K/year with typical raises, opportunity cost over three years is not $246K — it's closer to $270–285K once you factor in lost 401(k) matching, raises, and professional advancement. A practical approach is to treat foregone income as a hard cost in your model alongside tuition and living expenses so you see the true economic outlay, not just the debt number you'll carry at graduation.

* **In-state residency eligibility is a near-term lever worth researching now.** Many states require 12 months of domicile before you qualify for in-state rates. If you're targeting a specific state's law school, moving there a year before enrollment — and picking up per-diem nursing shifts — could save $20–40K in tuition while also rebuilding your savings buffer. That's one of the highest-leverage structural moves available before you ever set foot in a classroom.

* **Fellowships and need-based grants meaningfully alter the calculus at public-interest-oriented schools.** Schools with strong immigration law clinics — CUNY, UDC, some regional state schools — often have institutional grants for students with public interest commitments. A practical first step is contacting financial aid offices at your target schools and asking directly what the average debt load is for public interest students at graduation; schools that track and publish that number are usually serious about funding it down.

* **Model a "bridge year" scenario before committing to the standard path.** One underexplored option: spend 12–18 months doing LSAT prep, building savings, verifying your in-state eligibility, and doing informational interviews at immigration nonprofits. That bridge year could add $70–90K to your savings runway, reduce per-year living cost pressure significantly, and let you enter 1L with enough cushion to avoid part-time income dependency during the most academically demanding year.

## Existing Debt Compounding While Income Is Zero

* **Run the nursing loan numbers now, not later.** At $22K remaining on an existing loan, even a modest interest rate of 5–6% will add $3,300–$4,000 in interest over three years of non-payment if you enter forbearance. That's a known, calculable cost — not a scary unknown. The practical move is to contact your loan servicer today, confirm your current rate and outstanding balance, and run the forbearance scenario versus income-driven repayment at zero income. IDR at $0 income typically means $0 payments and interest may be subsidized on certain loan types.

* **PSLF clock starts on your first qualifying payment at a qualifying employer — not at bar passage.** A common misread is that PSLF requires 10 years of practice. What it requires is 120 qualifying monthly payments while working full-time at a nonprofit or government employer. If your first legal job is at a legal aid organization or immigration nonprofit, those payments start immediately. At entry-level public interest salaries of $50–65K, your IDR payments will be low and years 1–10 become a structured, if slow, payoff plan. Model this explicitly: the 10-year PSLF timeline from first legal job, and what total payment you'd actually make versus the balance forgiven.

* **Consolidation timing matters for PSLF eligibility.** If your nursing loans are FFELP loans rather than Direct Loans, they must be consolidated into a Direct Consolidation Loan to qualify for PSLF. Consolidating now resets any payment count, but since you have no qualifying PSLF payments yet, that reset costs you nothing. Confirm your loan type with studentaid.gov before law school begins — this is a 20-minute task that could prevent a years-long eligibility mistake.

* **Total day-one debt load on a realistic scenario: model it as a range.** Conservative case — in-state school at $30K/year, shared housing at $20K/year, nursing loans capitalizing at 6%: total debt at graduation is approximately $155–165K. Aggressive case — private school at $70K/year, urban living at $35K/year: total debt is $315–330K. The difference between those paths is not effort or ambition — it is school selection and housing decisions. Making those choices deliberately is the entire game.

## Savings Runway And Monthly Burn Rate

* **Calculate your actual monthly burn rate this week with your real bank statements.** "Approximately $30–45K annual living costs" is a range wide enough to make your runway analysis useless. Twelve months of actual spending — rent, food, transportation, subscriptions, discretionary — gives you a real burn rate. If your current monthly spend is $3,200, that is $38,400/year and your $45K savings covers about 14 months. If it is $2,600, you get 17 months. That difference is not trivial when you're planning a 36-month program.

* **A practical approach is to set a savings target before enrollment rather than relying on the $45K as-is.** If you can push your savings to $75–80K over the next 18–24 months while continuing to work — cutting discretionary spending and picking up overtime — you enter law school with a meaningful buffer. At $2,800/month burn in a mid-cost-of-living city, $80K covers 28 months, getting you to the summer between 2L and 3L when summer stipends or firm salaries can begin contributing. That's a fundamentally different risk profile than starting with $45K.

* **Living cost reduction through deliberate location and housing choice is the highest-leverage expense lever.** A shared house with two other students in a mid-size city (Pittsburgh, Detroit, Albuquerque) could put your housing cost at $700–900/month, cutting a major line item in half compared to renting solo in a high-cost market. This is not a sacrifice — it is a strategic cost structure decision. Many strong public interest law programs exist outside New York, Boston, and San Francisco. Choosing school and city together is a portfolio decision, not two separate ones.

* **Model three funding scenarios explicitly: savings-only, savings plus fellowship, savings plus part-time income.** Most students rely on some combination. The value of modeling all three is that it shows you where the real floor is — the minimum you need the scenario to deliver to avoid hitting zero before graduation. A practical floor is $0 savings at the start of 3L, not $0 at graduation. You need buffer for the bar exam study period (typically 2–3 months post-graduation with no income) and the time between bar passage and first paycheck.

## Part-Time Income Realism During 1L Through 3L

* **Treat 1L income as essentially zero and plan accordingly.** The ABA's standard guidance limits outside employment during 1L to 20 hours/week, and many schools actively discourage even that. More critically, 1L GPA significantly affects law review eligibility, scholarship retention, and summer internship competitiveness — including at public interest organizations that rely heavily on class rank for fellowship decisions. Banking on 1L income is planning to compromise the academic foundation of your entire JD. Model it at zero; treat anything you earn as upside.

* **ICU per-diem nursing during 2L and 3L is structurally more feasible than most law students' side income options.** Per-diem critical care nursing pays $45–65/hour in many markets. Working two 12-hour shifts per month at $55/hour generates approximately $15,800/year — a meaningful contribution — with schedule flexibility that accommodates class and clinic commitments better than retail or service work. The practical constraint is maintaining your RN license (continuing education hours, which are manageable) and finding a per-diem position willing to accommodate student scheduling. Research this market in your target city before enrolling.

* **2L summer income is a real variable — public interest fellowships pay $15–25K for 10 weeks.** Organizations like Equal Justice Works, Skadden, and ACLU offer summer fellowships to 2L students with demonstrated public interest commitment. Your refugee resettlement volunteer experience is directly relevant. A practical approach is to track these fellowship deadlines now (most applications open in fall of 2L year) and treat a 2L summer fellowship as a base-case assumption, not a stretch goal. That $15–25K is the most likely funded income during law school.

* **Evening and part-time JD programs exist and change the calculus entirely.** Several ABA-accredited schools offer part-time JD tracks, typically four years instead of three, with classes in evenings and weekends. This allows you to continue per-diem nursing at a meaningful rate — potentially 24–30 shifts per month during lighter academic periods, 8–12 during intensive ones. The tradeoff is a longer timeline (graduation at 36 vs. 35) and potentially weaker access to full-time 1L networking. But the financial math is dramatically different: 48 months of partial nursing income versus 36 months of near-zero income is a $100–150K swing in net financial position at graduation.
