# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Career Change from ICU Nursing to Public Interest Law

---

## The People You Would Serve: Immigrant and Refugee Clients

1. **Clients who've been failed before**: From the community's perspective, immigrants and refugees who have encountered lawyers, advocates, or caseworkers in the past may have experienced broken promises, unexplained delays, or services that dissolved when funding ran out — so how does a prospective public interest attorney reckon with the trust deficit that exists before they walk in the door, and what would it actually feel like to be a client encountering this new attorney for the first time?

2. **Language and power in the legal encounter**: The people you would serve might experience a legal consultation as deeply asymmetric — the attorney holds knowledge, jargon, and institutional access they cannot access independently — so what does it mean to genuinely center client dignity and agency in legal practice rather than in mission statement language, and what have you seen (or not seen) in the immigration legal spaces you've encountered through volunteering that tells you whether that gap is being bridged?

3. **What clients need vs. what the system funds**: What they say they want — a path to safety and stability — and what the legal system is actually designed to deliver are often different things; how would you think about the moments when a client's most urgent need falls outside the scope of what legal representation can address, and what does your nursing experience with that same kind of scope-of-practice gap teach you about how to hold that tension without it becoming another source of burnout?

---

## The Populations Most Underserved: Who Gets Left Out

4. **Clients with the fewest resources**: Think about the emotional journey of someone who is undocumented, has no money, limited English, and is experiencing a legal crisis — they arrive feeling desperate and invisible, encounter a system that is backlogged and bewildering, and need to leave feeling heard and with a next step that is actually achievable — where in that journey do public interest legal organizations typically lose people, and how would you design your practice to serve people at the end of that access gap rather than the clients who are easiest to help?

5. **Trauma and the legal intake process**: Refugees and asylum seekers often carry histories of violence, loss, and institutional betrayal, and standard legal intake — forms, dates, documentation of harm — requires them to narrate trauma in a context optimized for evidentiary output rather than human care; from your ICU experience holding space for patients in crisis, what do you think you'd do differently in a legal intake setting, and what organizational norms might you struggle against?

6. **Clients who fall between systems**: The people we serve might include mixed-status families, individuals with concurrent criminal and immigration proceedings, or those whose legal needs cross jurisdictions in ways no single organization is funded to handle — how would you think about serving those clients who fall between the remits of different nonprofit silos, and what would it mean to be an advocate for people the system wasn't designed to see?

---

## Your Nursing Experience as an Asset to Clients

7. **Clinical knowledge in immigration advocacy**: Immigrant and refugee clients facing detention, deportation, or family separation often have unaddressed medical needs that legal representatives are not trained to notice or document — how might your eight years of ICU nursing become a genuine asset to clients rather than an incidental background detail, and are there specific client populations (asylum seekers documenting torture, detainees with serious illness, victims of gender-based violence) where clinical fluency would meaningfully change the quality of representation?

8. **Reading distress without language**: In the ICU you learned to read patient distress through behavior, physiology, and non-verbal signals in patients who could not communicate — from the community's perspective, how much of what your immigrant and refugee clients most need to convey might similarly be difficult to articulate in a legal consultation, and how would that perceptual skill translate into legal practice in ways a traditionally trained attorney might not develop?

---

## Emotional Sustainability and Long-Term Service Capacity

9. **Trading one high-burnout field for another**: The people you would serve in public interest immigration law are best served by advocates who stay in the field long enough to develop deep expertise, institutional relationships, and the kind of trust that takes years to build — so what do you actually know about why immigration attorneys burn out, what the rate of attrition looks like in the public interest legal sector, and what structural differences (or similarities) exist between ICU nursing exhaustion and the moral injury that drives people out of public interest law?

10. **When you can't win**: Think about the clients you would carry through a years-long asylum process that ultimately fails — what do you know about your own emotional architecture, from nursing, that tells you whether you are someone who can sustain that kind of loss on behalf of others, and what support structures would you need around you to remain an effective advocate over a twenty-year career rather than burning brightly for five?

---

## Alternative Paths: Serving the Same Community With Less Disruption

11. **What clients need that isn't a JD**: The people you're motivated to serve need legal representation, but they also need health navigation, trauma-informed advocacy, language access, and someone who understands how illness and legal crisis interact — so how clearly have you mapped which of their needs actually require a law degree and which could be addressed through a different kind of role, and have you spoken directly with immigrant and refugee clients about what they most wished they had access to?

12. **Hybrid roles that don't require starting over**: From the community's perspective, a person with deep clinical expertise and two years of refugee resettlement volunteering is already unusually equipped — so what would you lose, and what would your clients lose, if you pursued a path that combined your existing credentials with legal advocacy training (medical-legal partnership work, certified immigration counselor credentials, policy-focused master's programs) rather than a full JD, and have you actually explored whether those hybrid paths serve the same population at similar scale?

---

## Community Voice in Your Decision

13. **Asking the community what it needs**: Are we designing this career change with the community or for the community? — the people you're seeking to serve have views about what kinds of advocates they want and trust, what gaps in service they most acutely feel, and what a person with your background could most usefully do; have you found ways to ask them, and if not, what would it mean to make their answer a significant input into your decision rather than something you discover after three years of law school?
