# Brainstorming Questions from The Devil's Advocate: Career Change from ICU Nursing to Public Interest Law

---

## Burnout as Diagnosis, Not Direction

1. **Escape vs. calling**: Have we considered that burnout is notoriously poor at identifying what you should run *toward* — it only tells you what you're fleeing? If the driving force is ICU exhaustion rather than a genuine legal vocation, what mechanisms would distinguish a true calling from a well-rationalized exit, and how would you test that before committing three years and six figures?

2. **Public interest law burnout**: One potential challenge is that immigration and refugee legal work carries its own brutal emotional load — chronic underfunding, relentless caseloads, clients in life-threatening circumstances, and a legal system that frequently loses. Have we considered whether trading one high-burnout field for another simply resets the clock, and what evidence would suggest your resilience profile is specifically suited to legal advocacy rather than bedside care?

---

## Financial Assumptions Under Stress

3. **The savings runway assumption**: The plan seems to assume $45K in savings provides a meaningful buffer across a three-year JD, but law school costs for even modestly priced programs routinely run $30K–$60K per year in tuition alone. Have we considered what the actual all-in cost projection looks like — tuition, living expenses, forgone $82K salary, and $22K in existing loans compounding — and whether the financial picture survives contact with real numbers?

4. **PSLF as a load-bearing assumption**: Public Service Loan Forgiveness is frequently cited but has historically had staggering rejection rates and has been subject to legislative risk. Have we considered how robust this career plan is if PSLF is reformed, capped, or eliminated before the 10-year forgiveness window closes, and what the fallback looks like if you're carrying $150K+ in law school debt on a $55K public interest salary?

5. **Part-time income during law school**: Law school accreditation rules (ABA Standard 304) have historically restricted first-year students from working more than 20 hours per week, and many competitive programs discourage any outside employment. Have we considered whether the financial model accounts for near-zero income during the JD, and whether nursing's marketability for weekend or per-diem shifts during years 2–3 has actually been stress-tested against program demands?

---

## Credentials, Fit, and the Legal Hiring Market

6. **The nursing credential as asset — or niche**: Have we considered whether the nursing background, while genuinely distinctive, might also box you into a very narrow slice of the legal market — specifically health-adjacent immigration cases or medical legal partnerships — rather than opening the full breadth of immigration and refugee law? If the niche turns out to be smaller than anticipated, what's the fallback?

7. **LSAT preparation and law school tier**: LSAT performance is a significant gatekeeper, and law school ranking has an outsized effect on public interest career trajectories, clinical placements, and fellowship access. Have we considered what happens if LSAT preparation, on top of full-time ICU shifts, produces a score that only opens the door to lower-ranked programs — and whether attending a lower-ranked school changes the calculus on debt, career outcomes, and access to competitive public interest fellowships?

---

## Alternative Paths and Opportunity Cost

8. **The intermediate options under-examined**: One potential challenge with this framing is that it presents a binary — stay in ICU nursing or complete a full JD — when there are intermediate roles that combine clinical expertise with immigrant advocacy: medical-legal partnership coordinators, health policy advocates, social work with legal accompaniment, or paralegal roles in immigration nonprofits to test fit before the law school leap. Have we considered whether these paths were genuinely evaluated and ruled out, or whether the binary framing itself is an assumption worth interrogating?

9. **The 32-at-matriculation timeline**: Entering law school at 32 or 33, graduating at 35 or 36, then completing a fellowship or public defender rotation, means reaching senior associate level in public interest organizations in the early-to-mid 40s. Have we considered whether this timeline interacts with other life plans — partnership, children, aging parents, housing — and whether the window at age 32 is actually as flexible as it appears when personal circumstances may be less fixed two or three years into a JD program?

---

## Identity and Mission Sustainability

10. **Mission alignment vs. institutional fit**: Public interest law organizations often have fierce internal cultures shaped by scarcity, ideological alignment, and high turnover. Have we considered whether the mission to help immigrants and refugees requires specifically becoming an attorney, or whether that identity claim has been tested against what it actually feels like to spend most working hours on motions practice, document review, immigration court procedure, and pro se client management — rather than the human-centered work that drew you to the field?
