# Validation Steps Before Committing - The Momentum Builder

## Question Summary: Minimum Viable Test of Legal-Fit

* **Start with accredited representative advocacy.** Your foundation is already there—you've logged 2,000+ volunteer hours in resettlement. The opening move is becoming a certified accredited representative through EOIR/BIA in the next 6 months. This is a real credential with real stakes: you'll be representing clients in front of immigration judges. And here's what gets exciting: that credential unlocks access to pro bono legal networks, allows you to take on supervised cases, and generates authentic signal about whether you thrive in advocacy under pressure. You'll know within 8–10 cases whether the problem-solving satisfaction is there. From that foundation, if it's working, you could layer in paralegal work at an actual immigration firm (even 10 hours/week part-time), which gives you a window into attorney workflow without the JD commitment.

* **Build through sustained case intensity.** The signal you're looking for isn't volunteering at scale—it's depth of engagement with complex cases. Rather than spreading 10 hours/week across six months, concentrate it: take on 2–3 genuinely difficult cases (family separation, appeal, withholding of removal) and see them through to resolution. The pressure and complexity will reveal whether you're energized by legal reasoning under uncertainty or whether you find it paralyzing. That's not something 50 shallow cases will tell you. And once you've lived that intensity, you'll have actual war stories to bring to law school interviews and a concrete sense of whether law school is the next step or a detour.

* **Test the emotional fit alongside the intellectual fit.** Eight years in the ICU prepared you for high stakes, but immigration law's emotional terrain is different—you're fighting bureaucracy and timeline scarcity rather than physiological crisis. The validation test is: volunteer intensively for 12 months (not casually), take on cases where you'll be the primary point of contact for clients, and check in monthly with a question: "Am I energized or drained by this work?" If you're writing detailed case notes with enthusiasm and thinking about clients between shifts, that's signal. If you're experiencing the same burnout dynamic (compassion fatigue, powerlessness, mission creep), that's equally important signal—and it might point toward a different path entirely (policy work, healthcare-adjacent law, etc.).

* **Use the test to refine, not just validate.** What you're really looking for isn't a yes/no answer—it's refinement of your actual practice interest. Maybe you discover you love client advocacy but dislike appellate procedure. Or that you're energized by systemic work but exhausted by individual representation. The MVT in the 6–18 month window should give you specific, actionable answers to bring into law school (clinic selection, internship strategy, bar exam geography). The momentum here is: take the test seriously, let it shape what you actually apply for, and then JD becomes a vehicle for the specific legal practice you've validated rather than a blind leap.

## Question Summary: What "Not Crazy" Actually Requires

* **Build the go/no-go checklist from existing anchors.** You're already asking the right question—this is exciting. What makes it "not crazy" is when you flip the burden of proof: instead of proving it's safe, define the specific conditions where it becomes obvious you should go. Start with the financial floor: you have $45K in savings. Can you identify a realistic law school cost (in-state public, JD + living expenses, minus scholarships) and work backward to your target LSAT score range that unlocks aid? Most public interest trajectories need LSAT 155+, which opens access to regional schools with better scholarship packages. That becomes anchor one. Anchor two: sustained volunteer signal (the MVT above). Anchor three: family conversation—not "I'm doing this," but "I've validated X, Y, Z. Where do you see the risk?" Their concerns might point you toward milestones you hadn't considered (loan repayment, timeline, geographic stability).

* **Let the LSAT be the gate, not just the first hurdle.** The psychological move here is elegant: take the LSAT seriously, aim for your best possible score, and let that result inform the entire next decision. If you score 165+, top schools that offer substantial need-based aid become available—that de-risks the financial piece materially. If you score 150, you get clear signal about either (a) needing another prep round, or (b) the signal itself suggesting law school may not be the move. The "go" moment isn't when you feel ready—it's when you have scholarship clarity, validated legal-practice signal from the MVT, and explicit family alignment that this is a legitimate pivot, not an escape. And here's the momentum: each of those milestones is achievable independently, so you're not staking everything on one bet.

* **Make the 12-month validation the hinge point.** The version of this that is definitely not crazy looks like: LSAT window (3 months), intensive legal practice validation (12 months), law school applications (months 13–16), and decision by month 18. By month 18, you'll have: confirmed sustained interest through real cases, a clear LSAT band and scholarship range, law school acceptance(s), and family buy-in. That's not a leap—that's a grounded transition. The "not crazy" doesn't mean risk-free; it means every major component has been tested before you commit. And if at any point in those 18 months you discover something (the work isn't for you, the financial reality is worse than expected, family circumstances shift), you've only invested time and LSAT prep, not $120K+ in tuition.

* **Name the escape-vs-calling tension directly.** Here's the deeper validation you need: before law school, spend 3 months explicitly asking whether you're running *from* nursing or running *toward* law. The test is specificity: Can you articulate what about immigration law itself energizes you beyond "it helps vulnerable people"? (You felt that same pull in nursing, and it led to burnout.) The "not crazy" milestone is when you can point to something specific in legal practice—problem-solving style, case strategy, time horizons, work patterns—that feels sustaining in a way bedside care doesn't. Once you have that clarity, law school becomes a calculated investment in a specific career direction, not a $120K scramble to escape something painful.

## Question Summary: The Support Structure That Makes It Real

* **Map your network before applying.** You have an unfair advantage that you might not be seeing: eight years of clinical work creates relationships with healthcare advocates, social workers, and community partners—many of whom have probably brushed up against immigration issues and know lawyers. The move here is to systematically ask: "I'm exploring immigration law. Who in your world does this work?" You might be surprised how quickly a 30-person network surface 3–5 lawyers actively doing this work. And here's the momentum: those conversations don't cost anything, they prime you with realistic perspective, and they create anchors for your law school journey (informational interviews, potential clinic supervisors, future networking contacts).

* **Find the person two steps ahead.** Ideally, you connect with someone who made a healthcare-to-law transition specifically (nurse to lawyer, PA to lawyer, social worker to lawyer). That person is gold—they've navigated the exact career-capital conversation you're trying to have. If you can't find that specific profile, find someone doing immigration law who values clinical experience or came from a service background. The validation here isn't "tell me it's possible"—it's "walk me through what surprised you, what you'd do differently, and whether clinical background was an asset or a liability in law school and practice." Getting that conversation in the next 6 months grounds your decision in someone else's real experience, not your projection of what it might be like.

* **Build a cohort of people navigating similar transitions.** One informational interview is helpful; three people in different stages of similar pivots is a support structure. You might find: someone in law school right now from a healthcare background (they can guide school selection), someone 2–3 years post-JD in immigration law (they can talk about practice reality), and someone considering making a similar jump (you navigate the validation period together). That cohort becomes your reality-check system—when you're exhausted during law school or doubting your decision, you have peers who went through the same fire and chose this anyway. The move is to explicitly ask your current network: "I'm exploring this. Can you connect me with three people doing or considering similar transitions?" Your 2 years of volunteer work gives you credibility to ask.

* **Get explicit mentorship on the long game.** Beyond informational interviews, identify someone further along in immigration law (5+ years practice) and ask for quarterly check-ins or a formal mentorship over the next 2 years. Not intensive—four 30-minute calls/year costs them little and anchors your decision-making. The questions for those conversations are concrete: "What would a sustainable practice look like for you?" "Where do you see lawyers burning out in this field?" "What training or background do you wish you'd had before law school?" That mentorship serves a dual purpose: it keeps your decision tethered to reality, and it begins building the relationship that might matter for internships, clerkships, or job placement in a few years. The support structure that makes this real is accountability to people further along who care about your success.
