# Total Cost & Funding the JD - The Pragmatist

## Question 1: All-in cost of attendance and realistic debt at graduation

* **Summary.** Model a tier-based funding scenario. Use your $45K strategically for years 2–3 when living expenses may stabilize and loan interest compounds faster. Target schools in lower cost-of-living regions (outside major metros) where $60–70K COA is achievable. Front-load federal loans in year 1, preserve savings for years 2–3. Realistic outcome: $180–210K total debt (including existing $22K). To survive this, you need either: (a) a loan forgiveness program commitment from day one (10-year PSLF track), (b) geographic flexibility to move to a lower-salary-but-stable public interest job immediately, or (c) a 5-year post-law-school payoff plan where you cap annual discretionary spending at $15K. Plug in actual school-specific COA, run a debt-at-graduation calculator monthly, and define a hard ceiling above which you pause enrollment.

## Question 2: Hidden pre-law costs already eating the runway

* **Summary.** You've likely already accounted for LSAT prep, but the application spread ($1,000–1,200 across 10 schools) will hit before admission decisions land. Budget $2,500–3,500 for (a) LSAT course/materials, (b) applications, (c) LSAC fees, (d) travel to campus visits if you're selective. This shrinks your cushion to $40–42K. Mitigate by: reducing applications to 6–7 target schools instead of 10 (focus on likelihood + fit, not volume), using fee waivers aggressively (most schools grant them automatically if GPA + LSAT are borderline), and deferring campus visits to after acceptance (virtual tours suffice for initial screening). The real risk: scope creep. If you add a pre-law semester at a local college, add tutoring, or wait an extra year to strengthen your profile, your working capital shrinks further. Make the LSAT a one-shot commitment (3–4 months of prep, one test date) and lock application timeline to 2 months.

## Question 3: Part-time work feasibility under ABA constraints

* **Summary.** Nursing's per-diem advantage is real, but constrained. Year 1 is effectively non-negotiable as income-zero due to ABA limits (20 hrs/week max) and 1L intensity. Realistic year 1 work: 8–12 hours/week of weekend nursing, netting $200–300/week (~$10K/year) if you can sustain it. Years 2–3 are stronger: typical schedule loosens, and you could pursue 16–20 hour weeks ($15–20K/year). However, 2L and 3L summers are traditionally reserved for law firm internships (competitive schools, public interest networks) that build your post-bar credentials. Working instead of interning costs you 2-3 critical networking contacts and may weaken your hiring profile. A pragmatic path: work year 1 for emergency cash only; prioritize competitive internships years 2–3 to build your public interest network and post-bar employment; defer aggressive income-generation to post-graduation. This trades short-term cash for long-term career capital.

## Question 4: Savings runway and minimum cash floor at graduation

* **Summary.** Define two numbers: (a) floor for bare survival ($8–10K for emergency fund + moving costs + bar prep), and (b) floor for stability ($25–30K to absorb 3-month job search without panic). Your $45K shrinks to ~$10–15K by end of 1L after tuition, housing, and loan repayment. The realistic question is whether you hit the survival floor at graduation or overshoot into stability. If schools are $70K COA, your budget per year is $70K - (federal loans) - (work income) = fully financed by loans by year 2–3. By graduation, you'll have minimal savings. This is fine if you're confident in your public interest job market fit (nonprofit legal clinics, EOIR positions, immigrant advocacy orgs). It's riskier if you haven't secured post-bar employment by graduation. Actionable step: by November of 3L, have 2–3 firm offers in hand or a commitment letter from a legal nonprofit. If neither materializes, consider a brief stint (6–12 months) in a higher-paying legal role to rebuild cash reserves before dropping into public interest work.

## Question 5: The 1L winter moment — sustaining motivation through burnout risk

* **Summary.** This is the emotional crux. You're in debt, isolated from nursing colleagues, and the immediate impact on vulnerable populations feels distant. Here's what sustains pragmatists: concrete evidence that you made the right trade. By January of 1L, identify one tangible win: (a) volunteered at a legal clinic and saw a client move forward, (b) formed a meaningful study group with 2–3 like-minded peers who also want public interest, or (c) aced an immigration law elective and realized you can compete in the field. These moments are not abstract inspiration — they're proof of concept. Also build accountability: commit now to a summer 1L experience (legal internship, nonprofit clinic) that connects you to actual immigrant advocacy work. If you spend January reading property law in isolation, you'll struggle. If you spend January knowing your summer internship is with an EOIR clinic or refugee legal services, the dark moment becomes temporary. The pragmatist move: treat 1L as a means to an end, not as intrinsically meaningful. Your throughline is mission, not law school prestige. When burnout hits, remember that.
