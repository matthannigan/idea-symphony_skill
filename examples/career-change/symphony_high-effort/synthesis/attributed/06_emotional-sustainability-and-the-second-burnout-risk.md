### Question 1: Trading one high-burnout field for another — the transferable load

**Full question**: Public interest immigration law is notorious for burnout — underfunding, impossible caseloads, vicarious trauma from detention and deportation cases, chronic secondary trauma, and a culture of martyrdom that mirrors nursing. The emotional mechanism that generates burnout in nursing — high stakes, inadequate resources, feeling unable to change the system — maps closely onto immigration law as a transferable load. After 8 years of absorbing patient suffering, something in you is clearly built to hold it, but something is also depleted: what specific features of *how* you would practice (practice area, caseload size, organizational type, supervision, peer support, practice mix) would you need to control for, and how realistic is it to build those buffers at the entry level of a new career rather than after?

* **Burnout is a structural property of the organization, not a failure of personal discipline — so the buffers have to be institutional.** All seven perspectives converge here: caseload caps, built-in clinical supervision, mandatory peer debriefing, reasonable hours, and rotational practice mix are preconditions you cannot willpower into existence. The mechanism that generated burnout in nursing (high stakes, inadequate resources, low control over a system you cannot fix) maps almost exactly onto an underfunded immigration nonprofit. The actionable move is to identify the specific feedback loop that depleted you and choose an organizational context that structurally interrupts it, rather than hoping personal resilience will hold.
  * **Research specific orgs and ask current staff about real hours, caseloads, peer supervision; if none offer structural protection at entry, name the first 3–5 years as a calculated risk.** Most nonprofits cannot offer junior attorneys these buffers. Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Identify the feedback loop (high stakes → inadequate resources → impossible caseloads → moral injury) and pick practice area and organizational context first, then check whether that context prevents the loop from forming.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Contact 3–5 nonprofits this month and volunteer 10 hrs/week for 6 months in substantive legal roles to get direct evidence the daily load is survivable before incurring ~$180K debt.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **You can't demand fewer impossible cases at entry level — you get what the organization assigns; the realistic version is 3–5 years as someone else's workhorse before you can shape your own practice.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Your capacity to hold suffering is both the asset and the danger — the coping mechanisms that made you a good nurse are what burned you out.** Five perspectives (Audience Advocate, Empath, Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker, Storyteller) converge that the very container you built in the ICU can become compulsive: holding suffering becomes evidence of your worth, and there is infinite suffering in immigration law to absorb. Bringing the same habits (staying late, absorbing crises, treating boundaries as betrayal) guarantees a faster repeat. The change has to be in how you relate to the work, demonstrated before you commit, not hoped for.
  * **ICU skills transfer; ICU coping mechanisms may not — the same willingness to absorb others' crises will burn you out again, faster.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **The capacity can become compulsive: part of you believes holding suffering is your job and proof of worth; the question is whether you can practice without making it a moral duty to absorb the system's cruelty into your nervous system.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Your ability to hold suffering got you *into* burnout, not out of it — you need evidence you've already changed your boundaries, not hope a new field will do it for you.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Negotiate structural buffers as a day-one hiring condition, framed as how you work best rather than as a burnout risk.** The Connector and Storyteller offer a reframe the others don't: your nursing background is leverage. A rotational structure (year one mostly cases, year two policy/training, year three mentorship) or an explicit caseload ceiling can be requested at the entry level if framed as "I learn best through variety" or "if I take 15 cases instead of 25, which critical work doesn't happen?" rather than as a confession of fragility.
  * **Cross-training as a depletion buffer: negotiate a rotational structure as a hiring condition; nursing taught you to recognize depletion, which is an asset, not a liability.** Original detail from The Connector. *—The Connector*
  * **Visible buffers as a month-two negotiation: walk into the supervisor's office and ask what work doesn't happen at a lower caseload; the entry-level question is the difference between repeating nursing and not.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Target organizational types where caseload caps and retention are already institutionalized — newer orgs, government agencies, or larger nonprofits with practice groupings.** The Pragmatist and Systems Thinker note that some contexts have the buffers baked in: government immigration roles often carry formal caseload caps and union protections; orgs with board-level retention metrics invest in supervision because their funding depends on keeping staff.
  * **Target newer orgs replacing burnout departures, or government agencies with formal caseload caps and union protections, or larger nonprofits with practice groupings so you don't carry the whole emotional weight alone.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

### Question 2: Texture of sustainable advocacy — what daily life needs to feel like, and the calling-as-trap

**Full question**: Beyond the outcome of helping immigrants, what does the *texture* of your daily work life need to feel like for this career to sustain you for two or three decades, and is that texture realistically available in public interest immigration law? "Deeply committed to helping vulnerable populations" is a genuine motivation but can also become a trap — the strength of commitment makes it harder to set limits, the limits are what prevent burnout, the burnout eventually destroys the capacity to help anyone. How do practitioners successfully separate the strength of their commitment from the absence of self-protective limits, and what would need to be true about a legal practice structure — not just personal discipline — to make that separation durable?

* **The calling-as-trap is the central danger: strong commitment overrides the body's limit signals, so the separation between commitment and self-protection must be built into structure, not left to willpower.** Six perspectives (Audience Advocate, Empath, Storyteller, Connector, Pragmatist, Systems Thinker) converge: the strength of your commitment is exactly what makes you vulnerable to exploitation, because organizations that want to extract maximum value activate your sense of calling. Durable separation requires clinical supervision watching your boundaries, peer consultation that can take co-counsel when a case gets too entangling, and a culture that treats "I cannot take more" as resource management, not moral failure.
  * **The permission to not be necessary all the time has to be baked into structure (supervisors who enforce boundaries, manageable caseloads, a culture that doesn't valorize suffering) because you won't give yourself that permission.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Separate the commitment archetype from the burnout archetype — committed people aren't more prone to burnout *if* the org lets them set limits; the "martyrdom loop" is organizational, not personal.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Reframe the work from "How much can I give?" to "How can I be reliable enough to serve people for 20+ years?" — the clients who've watched advocates burn out know the ones who stay are the ones with boundaries, not the most passionate.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*

* **Distinguish commitment from saviorism by separating the strength of commitment from decisions about scope.** The Storyteller's reframe, echoed by the Audience Advocate and Empath: bounded commitment ("I am committed to your legal success; I am not the right person for your housing issue; I will represent you through your hearing but I am not your therapist") creates space for deeper work within the boundary. Saviorism (absorbing a client's terror, staying up late because you're holding their fear) serves your own sense of being necessary, not the client. The structures that support the first are clear success metrics and explicit stopping points; the structures that enable saviorism are ambiguous metrics ("she feels completely safe").
  * **Saviorism takes on the emotional weight; commitment refers out and sets explicit stopping points — clear metrics support the first, ambiguous metrics ("she feels safe") enable the second.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*
  * **Separate meaning from martyrdom before law school; find mentors and clinical supervisors who explicitly teach and model sustainable practice and question you when you overwork.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*

* **Get specific about which texture of helping replenishes you versus which depletes you — they are not one thing.** The Empath, Connector, and Systems Thinker converge that "helping immigrants" hides a crucial distinction: one-on-one relationship versus systemic change, being the expert who solves versus standing alongside, intellectually engaging work versus crisis response. The right caseload and role depend entirely on which texture you're actually drawn to. If you need sustained relationship, a 300-case load destroys you; if you're energized by intellectual complexity, seek heavier writing/research roles.
  * **Some helping feels like water (replenishes) and some like blood loss (empties); a 300-case load destroys someone who needs sustained relationships, while clinics feel too small for someone who needs systemic change.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **The rhythm principle: the nervous system depletes when intensity never drops; a mixed docket (deportation defense + green-card help + Know Your Rights education) changes the rhythm without changing commitment.** Original detail from The Connector. *—The Connector*
  * **Track when you feel *restored* versus *drained* during substantive volunteering; if restored by client impact + intellectual work but drained by time pressure + chaos, seek heavier writing/research and lighter crisis-response roles.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Define "sustainable" operationally before enrolling, and commit to structural rules in advance.** The Pragmatist's contribution: the word "commitment" is doing too much work. Break it into concrete answers — what outcomes matter, what a productive day feels like, when you stop work — and pre-commit to only working for orgs with written caseload policies, refusing assignments beyond them, and switching jobs if they're not enforced.
  * **Answer three questions in writing before law school (what outcomes matter, what a productive day feels like, when you stop work) and pre-commit to org rules, refusing over-limit assignments, and switching jobs if limits aren't enforced.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Commitment that survives is commitment-without-fusion — learnable before law school by interviewing sustainable attorneys about exactly what separates their commitment from their depletion.** The Connector draws on contemplative traditions: monks sustain decades of service through practices that build separation (specific self-care times, community accountability, explicit rejection of martyrdom). Spend your last nursing year learning that distinction from practitioners rather than discovering it after five burned-out years.
  * **Commitment-without-fusion from monastic traditions: caring *with structure*, not caring less; learn it from sustainable attorneys before entering, not after.** Original detail from The Connector. *—The Connector*

### Question 3: Distinguishing burnout from misalignment, from the clients' perspective

**Full question**: From the community's perspective, what they need is not someone who is merely less burned out than they were before — they need someone who will stay. What is the human cost of attorney turnover in immigration law (delayed cases, lost institutional knowledge, severed trust), and what specific evidence from your two years of refugee resettlement volunteering — consistency, initiative, types of tasks chosen — suggests your commitment is durable rather than reactive?

* **Attorney turnover is a measurable injury to clients, not a private career question — so durability is something you owe the community, not just yourself.** Six perspectives (Audience Advocate, Storyteller, Systems Thinker, Connector, Empath, Devil's Advocate) converge that when an attorney leaves mid-case, institutional knowledge walks out the door: the client re-tells their trauma to a stranger, deadlines slip, deportations happen in transition, and communities that have already lost advocates learn to distrust passionate newcomers. Well-run organizations treat attorney wellness and client outcomes as directly correlated, which dissolves the false tension between caring for yourself and serving clients.
  * **An 18-month asylum case or 2-year VAWA petition loses its institutional knowledge when an attorney leaves at month 14; communities have learned to be cautious about passionate people who leave.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Traced through Julio's family: the new attorney's file is thorough on dates but sparse on context, doesn't know his trauma manifests as selective silence, and the institutional knowledge is just gone.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*
  * **The retention paradox: non-burned-out staff stay longer, so attorney wellness is directly correlated with client outcomes — "sustainability for yourself" does not contradict "durability for clients."** Original detail from The Connector. *—The Connector*

* **Durability shows up in consistency through the unglamorous, non-crisis parts — not in intensity peaks.** All seven engage the diagnostic; the convergent signal is the same. The evidence that predicts staying is whether your refugee work shows up in the boring administrative tasks, the follow-up months later, the meetings where nothing dramatic happened, and the relationships deepened with specific families. Crisis energy is a burnout precursor; sustained engagement in the slow parts is the durability marker. Audit the two years for which pattern you actually showed.
  * **Look for sustained engagement, not intensity peaks: follow-up with families, unglamorous admin work, showing up when nothing dramatic happened.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Burnout-driven people show high crisis energy then drop away; durability is consistency in non-crisis months, follow-through on small projects, staying engaged when no impact is visible.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A recommendation that says "incredibly hardworking, took on whatever needed doing" is a burnout precursor; "showed up consistently, deepened relationships with three families, had boundaries about what she could take on" is durability.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*
  * **Durability is doing the boring, repeated, frustrating parts and staying committed anyway; ask how you actually felt about nursing by year six, when it stopped being a calling.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*

* **Two years of salaried volunteering is a weak predictor of full-time sustainability — the precarity is the variable you haven't tested.** The Devil's Advocate's load-bearing dissent: you volunteered with a salary, security, and the freedom to walk away. A volunteer doing one intake per week looks consistent; the same person doing 30 intakes and carrying 80 cases as their sole income looks like someone heading for the exit. Volunteering is a terrible predictor of full-time durability in high-stress fields.
  * **You volunteered with a salary and an exit option; consistency under no financial pressure does not predict consistency when it's your sole income and your caseload is crushing.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Test durability now in a higher-stakes, no-pay role and seek a mentor who stayed in immigration law without burning out.** The Pragmatist's concrete moves: commit for 12 months to a high-responsibility volunteer role where there are real consequences if you don't show up, and find a 10+-year practitioner to ask "What would you have done differently at year 2 to protect year 10?" — more predictive than any abstract commitment test.
  * **Commit 12 months to a higher-responsibility, no-pay role with real consequences for not showing up; and ask a long-tenured attorney what they'd have changed at year 2 to protect year 10.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Distinguish whether you build durable community relationships or thrive in bounded, episodic engagement — they fit different roles.** The Connector's unique angle: your refugee work was a temporary structure you could leave; long-term accountability to an embedded community is structurally different. Knowing which mode genuinely fits you determines which immigration role works, not just which feels meaningful.
  * **The social-architecture lens: are you the type who builds durable, embedded community relationships, or do you thrive in bounded episodic engagement? That determines role fit.** Original detail from The Connector. *—The Connector*

### Question 4: The growing wellness infrastructure in legal education

**Full question**: Law schools have been investing measurably in mental-health and wellness programs over the past decade, partly in response to documented mental-health crises in the legal profession — how do these structural investments, combined with the availability of clinical placements in high-meaning practice areas, change the sustainability equation for someone entering legal education with prior occupational burnout?

* **Law-school wellness infrastructure does not transfer to practice — what matters far more is the employer you choose afterward.** Six perspectives (Systems Thinker, Devil's Advocate, Audience Advocate, Empath, Pragmatist, Connector) converge: a meditation room and counseling office do not fix an unsustainable caseload or change the underlying incentive structure that burns lawyers out at staggering rates. Boundaries learned in a three-year academic environment dissolve when a real client faces deportation in three weeks. Select your first employer more carefully than your law school, because the first two years of practice shape your trajectory more than school does.
  * **Wellness infrastructure creates entry conditions, not guarantees; select your first employer more carefully than your law school because the first two years matter more.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Wellness programs don't change the underlying incentive structure — lawyers still burn out at staggering rates because the work itself is the problem; it's the cheap consolation prize.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A therapy office doesn't fix an unsustainable workload; ask about clinical programs specifically (caseload per student, supervision built in, whether students are trained to set boundaries).** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*

* **The clinical placement is the real asset — a multi-year, supervised, lower-caseload test drive of the actual work.** The Storyteller, Empath, and Systems Thinker reframe the value: clinics in immigration work let you practice with real clients, real supervision, and built-in reflection for an extended period — a trial run nursing school never offered. Doing 2L summer and 3L clinical work at an immigration nonprofit gives a far stronger signal than abstract commitment, and lets you experiment with scale without immediately drowning in 200 cases.
  * **The clinical placement is a three-year test drive: real clients, real cases, but calibrated for learning with supervision and smaller caseload — a much stronger signal than abstract commitment.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*
  * **The placement offers experimentation with scale: doing the work in a container (10 cases, supervision) rather than immediately drowning in 200; the value is permission to try without being expected to save the world.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Use the clinic to test yourself rigorously: deliberately overload, work without close supervision for a period, see whether you crash or adapt — that evidence tells you more than the wellness infrastructure.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **For someone with a pre-existing burnout pattern, wellness programs can become one more thing to optimize and feel guilty about — and shame may block access entirely.** The Empath's distinctive caution: a person who turns commitment into martyrdom can turn the mindfulness class into another performance metric, feeling like a failure for not meditating enough. Legal culture still treats needing support as weakness, so the people who need it most may never use it.
  * **Wellness programs work best for people who don't burn out anyway; for a burnout-prone person they become another optimization target, and shame keeps the people who most need help from accessing it.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*

* **The clinical environment can name and interrupt burnout-causing patterns in real time, while you build skills, rather than after you've repeated them.** The Storyteller and Connector see law school as a potential pause point: good clinical education surfaces perfectionism and boundary-less commitment explicitly ("burnout comes from people with the strongest commitment, so we'll teach you to set limits and still be effective"), changing the psychology before the burnout-to-burnout cycle repeats.
  * **Law school as a moment to interrupt the pattern: good clinical education names perfectionism and boundary-less commitment while you study, not after you've repeated them.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*
  * **The infrastructure is unevenly distributed: a school that models the 80-hour week teaches martyrdom through its structure; one with flexible schedules and sustainable clinics teaches something else — a concrete filter for where to apply.** Original detail from The Connector. *—The Connector*

* **Evaluate wellness programs on implementation, not stated commitment, and use law school deliberately to learn sustainable-practice architecture.** The Pragmatist's specifics: check whether there's a full-time director versus a 0.5 FTE bolt-on, whether programs are required or optional (optional = nobody goes), and take courses on law-practice management and nonprofit organizational design so you can evaluate and improve the structures you later enter.
  * **Evaluate on implementation (full-time director? required attendance? usage rates?) and take courses on practice management and organizational design so you can later evaluate and improve org structures.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

### Question 5: Imagine the opposite succeeds catastrophically

* **The failure mode is structural repetition: you change the subject matter but keep the same operating system, and burnout returns on the same timeline around year 7–10.** All seven converge on the prompt's own hypothesis. The catastrophe is not that immigration law is too hard but that you import the nursing pattern (exceed your limits, internalize responsibility for impossible outcomes, delay boundary-setting until crisis) into a field with the same structural pressures. Success accelerates it: prestige brings the hardest cases, supervisory load, and the belief that your presence is the limiting factor in whether clients get justice.
  * **The failure mode is structural repetition, not incompetence: same approach (exceed limits, internalize responsibility, delay boundaries) yields burnout on the same year 7–10 timeline; the question is whether you understand the pattern and will interrupt it structurally.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **You'd take on every case, stay until midnight, treat law as an emergency that never ends; the warning sign present from year one is treating it as ongoing emergency rather than a long-term practice with sustainable rhythms.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **The success trap: brilliant helping professionals burn out *again* when success means more responsibility, harder cases, and mentoring — more of exactly what burned them out, wrapped in prestige.** Original detail from The Connector. *—The Connector*

* **The warning signs are plausible-sounding justifications that each look locally rational but collectively reprogram your limits.** The Systems Thinker, Storyteller, Empath, and Devil's Advocate converge on the texture of the slide: "This case is too important to say no." The pattern typically surfaces at months 12–18 (working nights, no time off, thinking about cases on weekends), and most people choose neither to interrupt it nor leave, hoping it stabilizes.   * **The failure mode is bringing the same *emotional contract* ("I will sacrifice myself because people need me") into a new field; interrupting it requires real therapy and community accountability in your nervous system, not theory.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*

* **The failure was optimizing the wrong variable — you'd conclude bedside nursing was the problem when the actual problem was your relationship to limitation and helplessness.** The Devil's Advocate's sharp framing: you'd change the field but not the relationship, and discover the catastrophe was repeating the same emotional pattern because you never addressed it.

### Question 6: Burnout ecology across high-stakes helping professions

**Full question**: Research on burnout in emergency medicine, public defenders, and humanitarian aid workers shows a structural commonality — it's not the workload but the combination of high stakes, low agency, and invisible outcomes that erodes people; which of those three factors is driving your ICU burnout, and does the same factor appear in the public interest law roles you're imagining, or does the job structure actually change that variable in a meaningful way?

* **Diagnose which of the three factors (high stakes, low agency, invisible outcomes) actually drives your burnout — the answer determines whether immigration law solves your problem or repeats it.** All seven converge on this as the cluster's central diagnostic. High stakes alone don't deplete; the combination does, and which factor dominates for you is decisive. If low agency is the driver, some immigration roles (policy, leadership, smaller firms) offer more; if invisible outcomes is the driver, immigration law may be as bad or worse, since cases drag for years and you may never learn the result.
  * **Match your specific burnout driver to a role that changes that variable: policy advocacy has higher agency than casework; removal defense has some agency but uncertain outcomes; if invisible outcomes was your driver, you may trade one problem for another.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Different immigration roles have different combinations: a nonprofit attorney may have moderate stakes + higher agency (you set strategy) + invisible outcomes; a policy attorney has moderate stakes + high agency + visible outcomes — which combination wouldn't deplete you is your answer.** Original detail from The Connector. *—The Connector*

* **Low agency may be *more* entrenched in immigration law than in nursing — a structural feature you cannot fix by choosing a better organization.** The load-bearing dissent from the Devil's Advocate, Systems Thinker, and Empath, which overturns the optimistic "law gives you more agency" assumption that appears elsewhere in the cluster. In nursing you can advocate for staffing and protocols; in immigration law you advocate within a legal and political system explicitly designed to restrict immigration, so you can win at the margins but cannot change the system generating the demand. This is not an organization-selection problem.
  * **Low agency is more entrenched: in nursing you can push for better staffing/protocols; in immigration law you advocate within a system designed to restrict immigration — you can't change the system driving demand; that's structural, not solvable by org choice.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Entry-level lawyers have *less* agency, not more: you can't undetain clients, change immigration law, or speed the courts; more purpose but not more ability to change outcomes — a recipe for a different, possibly worse, type of burnout because powerlessness is now legal rather than medical.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **The factor driving your nursing burnout may be visibility-without-agency ("I know exactly what would help and I'm not allowed to do it"); immigration law has the same structure, possibly worse — which might be a dealbreaker, since it doesn't solve the factor actually burning you out.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*

* **Invisible outcomes may be worse in immigration law, but some organizations build feedback loops that make outcomes visible — and that is a concrete, testable workplace choice.** The Connector, Systems Thinker, Devil's Advocate, and Storyteller converge: you may spend 40 hours on an asylum brief and never learn the result, since cases drag for years and clients aren't obligated to share. But some nonprofits follow up with clients and track outcomes; whether invisible outcomes deplete or energize you is the single most predictive variable, and you can test your own response before committing.
  * **Some nonprofits build feedback loops (follow-up, celebrating wins, tracking outcomes) and others don't — a concrete workplace choice that changes the burnout equation; seek orgs that make outcomes visible.** Original detail from The Connector. *—The Connector*
  * **Test your response to invisible outcomes specifically: volunteer 6–8 months on *completed* (decided, not pending) cases and track whether you find closure in the discrete outcome or feel unsettled not knowing what happened next — that predicts your sustainability better than any other variable.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **High stakes + invisible outcomes is the specific combustion: in nursing outcomes are visible (recovery, discharge, death); in law a denial offers minimal reasoning and you may never know if your 40-hour brief mattered.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **The agency variable spans a real spectrum across roles — consciously choose higher agency rather than assuming it appears.** The Connector's framing via the exit/voice/loyalty lens: nurses follow orders (low agency); a founding attorney in a small practice has more. If low agency burned you out, the prescription is to deliberately select a smaller firm, a policy role, or a leadership track, not to hope agency materializes.
  * **The agency variable across fields: if low agency burned you out, consciously choose higher agency in law (smaller firm, policy, leadership), don't assume it will appear; ask where the agency is in the specific role you're imagining.** Original detail from The Connector. *—The Connector*

### Question 7: Trading burnout for a different fire

**Full question**: What if emotional sustainability isn't about finding a less intense version of helping people but about finding the specific intensity that doesn't deplete you — and what would it mean to distinguish between the burnout of being inside a system that makes you feel powerless and the energizing exhaustion of fighting a system from outside it, and how would you know the difference before committing three years and significant financial exposure to finding out?
