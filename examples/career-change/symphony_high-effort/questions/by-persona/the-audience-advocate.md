# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Career Change from ICU Nursing to Public Interest Law

---

## The Immigrant and Refugee Clients You Would Serve

1. **Whose legal crises are most invisible**: Among the immigrants and refugees this career path targets, which subpopulations face the most acute legal needs yet are least likely to seek help — and what does that tell you about whether more public interest attorneys is actually the bottleneck, versus distrust of institutions, language access, or physical proximity to legal services?

2. **What the experience feels like from the other side**: When an immigrant or refugee client sits across from a public interest attorney for the first time, what emotional and practical barriers might they bring into that room — fear of deportation, distrust of authority, shame, exhaustion — and how would your background as an ICU nurse, someone trained to project calm in crisis and to communicate under stress, equip or limit you in meeting them where they are?

3. **The gap between what clients ask for and what they actually need**: In immigration law, clients often present with one legal question (e.g., "can I renew my visa?") when the underlying situation is far more complex. From the community's perspective, what does it feel like when your legal problem is reframed and redirected by a professional — and how would you build enough relational trust that clients feel heard rather than processed?

4. **Cultural competency beyond language**: Serving immigrants and refugees well requires more than speaking their language or using interpreters. What cultural, religious, or familial frameworks might shape how your future clients make decisions — about whether to pursue a case, whether to testify, whether to involve family — and how prepared are you to navigate those frameworks without imposing your own?

## The Burnout Geography: Trading One High-Stakes Arena for Another

5. **What burnout actually looks like in public interest law**: The REQUEST names emotional sustainability as a concern. From the perspective of the clients you would serve: what happens to them when their attorney burns out, cycles off, or leaves the nonprofit sector? What is the human cost of attorney turnover in immigration law — delayed cases, lost institutional knowledge, severed trust — and how does that reshape your responsibility to sustainability?

6. **The secondary trauma question**: ICU nurses absorb patient suffering daily; public interest immigration attorneys absorb client trauma — detention, family separation, deportation fear — often without the same institutional support structures. What does the experience of carrying that weight look like over a five-year arc for the attorneys currently doing this work, and what does it tell you about the kind of self-care infrastructure you would need to build before entering the field, not after?

7. **Distinguishing burnout from misalignment**: The REQUEST asks whether this is a "genuine calling or burnout-driven escape." From the community's perspective, what they need is not someone who is merely less burned out than they were before — they need someone who will stay. What specific evidence from your two years of refugee resettlement volunteering suggests your commitment to this population is durable rather than reactive?

## Access, Equity, and the Public Interest Pipeline

8. **Who actually gets public interest legal representation**: Even within the public interest sector, legal aid and immigration nonprofits are chronically under-resourced, which means attorneys triage ruthlessly. From the perspective of the clients who don't make the cut — those with the weakest legal merits, the most complex situations, or the least sympathetic narratives — what does it feel like to be turned away, and how do you sit with being the person who has to make those calls?

9. **The geography of access**: Public interest legal organizations cluster in urban centers. If you end up at a well-resourced nonprofit in a major city, you may be serving a relatively well-connected immigrant population compared to those in rural areas or detention facilities. What communities would fall outside the reach of the positions most likely open to a new attorney with no prior legal practice — and does that gap matter to you?

10. **Loan repayment programs and who they actually serve**: Programs like PSLF and loan repayment assistance programs (LRAPs) sound like a solution to the financial barrier, but they require ten years of qualifying payments and stable nonprofit employment. From the perspective of the organizations that offer these roles: what attrition patterns do they see among attorneys banking on PSLF — and what does it mean for clients when an attorney leaves before their cases resolve?

## Your Nursing Background as a Resource for Clients

11. **What your clinical experience makes possible for clients**: An ICU nurse who becomes an immigration attorney brings something most public interest attorneys don't have: a firsthand understanding of how immigrant patients navigate a healthcare system that is simultaneously trying to help them and legally obligated to report them in some circumstances. How specifically could that knowledge — of healthcare bureaucracy, medical trauma, advance directives, language barriers in clinical settings — translate into better advocacy for clients whose immigration cases intersect with health crises?

12. **Medical-legal partnership as an underexplored path**: Some legal aid organizations operate medical-legal partnerships embedded inside hospitals and clinics, where attorneys work alongside clinicians to address the legal determinants of health — housing, immigration status, benefits. From the perspective of the patients and families who encounter these programs, what makes this model more accessible than a standalone legal office — and is this a path you've considered that might let you keep a foot in both worlds rather than abandoning one for the other?

## Power, Voice, and Who Gets to Define the Problem

13. **Designing with the community, not for it**: The community of immigrants and refugees you hope to serve has advocates, organizers, and legal experts within it — people who have navigated these systems themselves and now help others. Before you invest three years and significant debt in a JD, what does the experience of those community insiders suggest about whether the most urgent gap is more attorneys, or something else entirely — translation services, know-your-rights education, policy advocacy, or community organizing? And what would it mean to ask that question directly of the communities involved before deciding?
