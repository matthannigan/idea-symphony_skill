# Brainstorming Questions from The Provocateur: Career Change from ICU Nursing to Public Interest Law

---

## What If the Career Change Is Already Decided — and the Question Is Wrong

1. **Wrong problem, wrong frame**: What if "should I make this change?" is not actually the decision being made — what if the decision was already made the moment you started volunteering with refugees, and the real question is what's standing between you and acting on it? What does that reframe reveal about which obstacles are real and which are just permission-seeking?

2. **Imagine burnout as a feature, not a bug**: What if the burnout from ICU nursing is not an obstacle to reckon with but the precise training that makes you suited for the grind of public interest law — and the right question is not "can I survive another high-stakes, under-resourced field?" but "what does it mean that I keep choosing them?" What pattern does that reveal about what you actually need from work?

---

## Reversals That Crack Open Hidden Assumptions

3. **Flip the credential requirement**: What if law school was free and took six months, but the real barrier to helping immigrants was something else entirely — lack of trust from the communities you want to serve, insufficient cultural fluency, or a broken system that lawyers can't actually fix? What would that reveal about whether a JD is the right tool for the job, or just the most legible one?

4. **Make the stakes absurdly high — then read what stays**: Imagine you had to choose: you can help exactly one immigrant family per year with extraordinary depth and legal mastery, or keep 50 ICU patients alive this year as a nurse. Neither is hypothetical — both are real. Which one do you choose, and what does that answer tell you about where your sense of purpose actually lives? *The real insight: when you strip away the career-change framing and force a pure values comparison, what survives?*

---

## Collisions Between Domains That Don't Belong Together

5. **Treat the career change itself like a patient**: What if you applied ICU triage logic to this decision — assess stability, identify what's acutely life-threatening versus chronically worsening, and decide what to treat first? Under that framework, is the nursing burnout the presenting complaint masking a deeper diagnosis, or is the immigrant advocacy calling the root cause that everything else is symptomatic of?

6. **Imagine the opposite succeeds catastrophically**: You become a celebrated immigration attorney. Fifteen years from now, you're burned out again — this time from immigration law, not nursing. Looking back, what would have had to be true about how you made this transition for that outcome to be almost inevitable? What warning signs would you have ignored? *The real insight: the failure mode of this career change is not "you can't do it" — it's "you do it exactly the same way you did nursing, without changing what burned you out."*

---

## Absurd Premises That Reveal What You Actually Value

7. **Make the transition deliberately harder, not easier**: What if instead of looking for pathways that smooth the transition — bridge programs, online law school, loan forgiveness — you designed the hardest possible version of this path on purpose? Full-time, top-14 law school, maximum debt, no shortcuts. What would that tell you about how committed you actually are — and is commitment even the right thing to be measuring, or is it a trap that leads people to confuse suffering with meaning? *The real insight: distinguishing genuine vocation from the cultural story that worthwhile things must be hard.*
