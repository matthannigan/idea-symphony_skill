# Brainstorming Questions from The First Principles Thinker: Career Change from ICU Nursing to Public Interest Law

---

## The Core Need: What Problem Is Actually Being Solved?

1. **Disaggregating calling from vehicle**: At its core, this person wants to serve vulnerable immigrants and refugees in high-stakes situations — but is a law degree the only vehicle that achieves that goal, or is it one convention-weighted option that crowds out equally effective but less legible paths?

2. **Burnout as signal vs. noise**: Strip away the career framing entirely — what fundamental unmet need does "burnout" signal? Is it about the physical and emotional load of bedside nursing, the absence of systemic influence, or the mismatch between effort expended and durable change created — and does each of those map differently onto a legal career?

3. **Impact per unit of sacrifice**: If the goal is maximum positive impact on immigrant and refugee lives, what is the actual marginal contribution of having a JD versus achieving that impact through a non-attorney role — and what evidence would distinguish a genuine difference in leverage from a credentialing assumption?

## Deconstructing the Decision Structure

4. **The irreversibility test**: What is truly irreversible about choosing either path now — and what is actually recoverable later — because a decision analysis built on first principles should weight only the genuinely foreclosed options, not the ones that feel permanent but aren't?

5. **Hidden assumptions about law school as the entry point**: The path as stated begins with LSAT preparation and a 3-year JD, but what fundamental capability gap does law school actually address? What components of that gap could be acquired through supervised practice, certificate programs, or paralegal work at lower cost and time — and which cannot?

6. **Reframing the financial question**: The financial framing presents this as income loss during law school plus debt accumulation, but the fundamental question is whether the net present value of the legal career trajectory — including Public Service Loan Forgiveness and higher systemic impact — exceeds the compounded opportunity cost of staying in nursing. What would that calculation actually look like if built from scratch without assuming law school is prohibitively expensive?

## Rebuilding the Transition from Fundamentals

7. **Minimum viable entry**: If this person were designing the fastest path to doing meaningful immigration legal work — not the most prestigious or credential-complete path — what would the first 12 months look like, and how does that compare to the conventional "LSAT then law school" sequence as a discovery mechanism before full commitment?

8. **The nursing background as a structural asset, not a credential question**: Setting aside whether nursing experience looks good on a law school application, what fundamental capabilities does 8 years of ICU nursing actually confer — triage under uncertainty, translating complex information to frightened people, advocating for patients against institutional inertia — and in which specific legal practice contexts do those capabilities compound into genuine differentiation rather than becoming irrelevant?

## Sustainability as a First Principle

9. **Trading one unsustainable system for another**: The fundamental issue is not whether immigration law is better than ICU nursing in the abstract — it is whether this specific person can sustain high-stress advocacy work for vulnerable populations over a 20-30 year career. What does the evidence from people who made similar transitions tell us about the structural conditions (caseload, support, autonomy, mission alignment) that determine whether public interest law becomes a calling or a second burnout?
