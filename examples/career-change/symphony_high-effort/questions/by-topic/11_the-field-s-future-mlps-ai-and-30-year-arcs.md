# Topic Cluster 11: The Field's Future — MLPs, AI, and 30-Year Arcs

## Questions

1. **Clinician-advocates as a rising hybrid role at the MLP frontier**: Medical-legal partnerships are expanding across major U.S. health systems, with dozens of new programs embedding attorneys in clinical settings each year — given the trajectory of MLP expansion, where will the highest-leverage employment opportunities exist for an immigration attorney with ICU-nursing credentials by the time a 2026-enrolled JD student graduates in 2029, which institutional contexts (federally qualified health centers, academic medical centers, refugee health programs) will most likely create demand for that hybrid profile, and how should that shape program and clinic choices during law school?
2. **Humanitarian crisis caseload and AI's reshaping of entry-level work**: Forced displacement globally has been rising for over a decade with no structural reversal, suggesting steepening long-term demand for asylum, TPS, healthcare-access litigation, and detention-conditions practice; simultaneously, AI-assisted document drafting and case management is already reducing hours required for routine immigration paperwork. How should someone entering the field now think about which sub-specialties will see the steepest growth, and how does AI reshape the skills and case complexity that define an entry-level attorney's first five years — does that change what clinical experiences to prioritize in law school?
3. **The flourishing version — five years in and the attorney you want to become**: Picture the version of this career change that goes exceptionally well: you finish law school energized rather than depleted, you land a position that excites you, and you look back at the transition with pride rather than regret — and imagine it is five years from now and you are practicing immigration law and feel, on most days, that you have found your vocation. What does a typical week look like? What kinds of cases are you handling, who are your clients, what is the texture of your daily work, what do clients say to each other about you, and what would have had to be true about how you managed the years of training, the financial pressure, and the identity shift for that outcome to emerge? If your nursing background and your legal training combined into something that neither field currently offers its clients alone — a form of advocacy that no one else in your city does quite as well — what gap in the system would it fill, and who would be most grateful that it existed?
4. **The leverage point that transcends both fields**: Imagine a world where a single practitioner's eight years of ICU-level pattern recognition — the ability to read a patient's deteriorating trajectory before the monitors confirm it — is applied not to individual cases but to the systemic failure patterns of immigration enforcement and healthcare access simultaneously; what kind of structural intervention (policy, institution, practice) could only be imagined by someone who has stood in both systems, and how would pursuing that vision change the way you think about the JD as a tool rather than a destination?
5. **The 30-year arc, not the 3-year cost**: Imagine projecting this decision not as a three-year financial sacrifice but as the founding gesture of a 30-year body of work — what would the field of immigrant health-rights advocacy look like if it were shaped by people who came from clinical settings rather than from traditional legal pipelines, and what would you need to build, write, or lead in year 15 that you can only credibly do if you started the way you're contemplating starting now?
6. **The day that makes it worth it**: Picture yourself five years from now, sitting across from a family — perhaps a Congolese mother and her teenage daughter, navigating an asylum application — and you are the attorney in the room instead of the nurse in the hallway. What does that moment feel like, and what would have to be true about the path you took for you to feel that this version of yourself was worth becoming?
7. **The community that already exists around this transition**: People have already made the leap from healthcare to public interest law, and some of them have written about it, organized around it, or are willing to talk. Who in that community would be most valuable to find in the next 90 days — not for permission or validation, but for concrete intelligence about what the path actually requires — and what specific questions would make that conversation worth your time and theirs?
8. **The calling that precedes both careers**: What if "burnout" and "calling" were not opposites on a spectrum but signals pointing toward the same thing — a kind of work that doesn't currently have a name or an institution — and if you set aside both nursing and law entirely and asked what problem you were uniquely built to solve for immigrant patients in impossible situations, what shape would that work take if you could design it from first principles with no legacy constraints?
