# Brainstorming Questions from The Accountant: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## True Cost of Consolidation vs. Status Quo

1. **Savings vs. transition costs timeline**: The $1.2M in estimated annual administrative savings sounds compelling, but what are the one-time transition costs — severance for displaced administrators, contract buyouts, legal fees for union negotiations, technology system migration, and governance setup — and how many years does it take for cumulative savings to exceed those upfront costs?

2. **Deferred maintenance as a negotiating variable**: The $4.2M in deferred building maintenance sits across seven buildings in three districts, but this liability is almost certainly unevenly distributed — which buildings carry the largest deferred backlog, and does consolidation give the unified district leverage to close the worst-deferred buildings rather than inherit and fund their repair?

3. **Structural deficit trajectory without consolidation**: What does the financial model look like if consolidation does not happen? Given that the smallest district has a two-year fund balance and all three are running structural deficits, what is the expected sequence of fiscal failure — forced closure, state receivership, emergency state takeover — and what are the associated financial and governance consequences for the county compared to a managed consolidation?

---

## State Funding Formula and Revenue Restructuring

4. **Per-pupil funding gain mechanics**: The 12% increase in per-pupil allocation from the state formula is the headline revenue gain, but what is the actual dollar amount at current enrollment, and how does that figure change under the 15%-further-decline scenario projected by 2035? If enrollment falls faster than expected, does the formula gain shrink in ways that erode the consolidation's financial rationale?

5. **Property tax base equalization costs and winners/losers**: Consolidating three districts with different property tax rates and assessed valuations means some taxpayers will pay more and some will pay less after equalization — what is the estimated redistribution, which district's taxpayers face the largest increase, and what political and legal mechanisms exist to phase in rate changes rather than imposing them immediately?

6. **Bond obligation allocation across the new entity**: Each district likely carries its own outstanding bond debt tied to specific facilities and paid by its own tax base — how does consolidation legally restructure those obligations, which taxpayers become responsible for debt that funded buildings they don't use, and what is the total consolidated debt load the new district inherits?

---

## Operating Economics of the Consolidated District

7. **Transportation cost modeling for geographic expansion**: Consolidation almost certainly increases average student travel distances across a rural county, and transportation is one of the largest variable costs in rural district budgets — what is the estimated annual transportation cost increase under a consolidated model with centralized schools, compared to the current three-district configuration, and does that cost partially or fully offset the administrative savings?

8. **Staffing transition economics beyond administration**: The $1.2M administrative savings estimate likely captures superintendent, HR, and transportation director positions, but what happens to teaching staff ratios across consolidated grade levels, and are there scenarios where student-to-teacher ratios at surviving schools worsen in ways that require backfilling positions, partially negating the savings?

9. **Hidden ongoing cost structure of fewer, larger schools**: Closing or repurposing underutilized buildings reduces some costs but concentrating students in remaining facilities increases others — what is the total cost model for utilities, maintenance, custodial staff, and capital improvements at the surviving buildings after consolidation, and how does that compare to the distributed cost of running the current seven?

---

## Long-Term Financial Sustainability

10. **Break-even analysis under enrollment decline scenarios**: If combined enrollment continues declining from ~1,800 to the projected ~1,530 by 2035, at what enrollment floor does the consolidated district's cost structure stop making financial sense — meaning fixed costs per student rise to the point where the state formula gain no longer offsets operating losses — and what contingency triggers should be built into the consolidation plan?

11. **Facility repurposing as a balance sheet lever**: Closed or excess school buildings represent capital assets that could be sold, leased, or converted to generate revenue — what is the realistic market value or rental income potential for each of the seven current buildings in a rural county context, and how should proceeds be structured (endowment, capital reserve, one-time debt paydown) to maximize long-term fiscal stability for the new district?
