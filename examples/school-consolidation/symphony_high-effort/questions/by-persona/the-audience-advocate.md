# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## Children's Daily Experience and Learning Continuity

1. **Disruption of student relationships**: When consolidation reshuffles grade configurations and peer cohorts, how will students — especially those in middle and high school who rely on stable social networks for belonging and mental health — experience the loss of longstanding friendships and familiar teachers, and what transition supports would actually reach the most vulnerable students rather than those whose parents already advocate effectively?

2. **Transportation burden and learning readiness**: Students who currently walk or ride short bus routes to neighborhood schools may face 45-to-60-minute bus rides each way in a consolidated district — what does that daily time cost mean for homework completion, participation in extracurriculars, and fatigue-related learning outcomes, and how would those effects fall differently on students in different parts of the county?

3. **Extracurricular access under consolidation**: Small schools often allow students who would never make a varsity team in a larger district to participate in athletics, drama, or music — how will the consolidated district ensure that participation breadth does not collapse into a purely competitive model where students from previously smaller schools lose their spots, and what obligations should be written into the consolidation plan?

4. **Special education and IEP continuity**: Students with individualized education plans depend on relationships with specific aides, therapists, and teachers — how will the transition protect the continuity of those relationships and placements, particularly for students with complex needs who are most harmed by disruption, and who will be accountable if a placement lapses during the administrative transition?

---

## Family Experience: Voice, Access, and Trust

5. **Whose voices shape the process**: The feasibility study was commissioned by a county board, but the people most affected — families with children currently enrolled — may have no structured role in shaping what is studied or what tradeoffs are weighted — how can the consolidation process be redesigned so that community input from parents, students, and caregivers goes beyond public comment periods and actually shapes the plan?

6. **Language and literacy barriers to participation**: In a rural county, some families may include non-English speakers, adults with limited formal education, or households without reliable internet access — what would it take to ensure these families are genuinely informed and heard in the consolidation process rather than nominally notified, and how would their participation be measured?

7. **Trust deficits and historical grievances**: Families in the smallest, most financially precarious district may have a history of feeling overlooked by county-level decisions — how will planners surface and address existing distrust before asking those families to vote yes on a consolidation that effectively ends their district's independent existence?

---

## Economic and Logistical Barriers for Families

8. **Work-schedule conflicts with school hours**: If consolidation changes school start times, bell schedules, or transportation routes, working parents — especially those in hourly jobs without schedule flexibility — may face new childcare gaps at the margins of the school day — what commitments will the consolidated district make about schedule predictability, and how will early-morning or late-afternoon care be addressed for families who cannot absorb that cost?

9. **Fee and cost creep in larger districts**: Larger consolidated districts sometimes introduce activity fees, supply lists, or pay-to-play sports requirements that smaller community schools informally waive for families in need — how will the consolidated district track and address the financial accessibility of all student activities, and what accountability mechanisms will prevent the gradual exclusion of low-income students from full participation?

---

## Community Identity, Belonging, and the "Our School Is Our Town" Reality

10. **What the school building actually holds**: For communities where the school gymnasium hosts town meetings, the sports teams define local pride, and the school calendar structures community life, closing or repurposing a building is not merely a facilities decision — it is the dismantling of a civic anchor — how should the consolidation plan be evaluated not only on financial grounds but on what it does to the social fabric of each community, and who decides how much that is worth?

11. **Identity and dignity in a merged institution**: Students from the smallest or lowest-performing district may arrive at a consolidated school carrying stigma — either feared as a burden or dismissed as coming from a "lesser" school — how will the consolidated district build a shared identity that does not simply absorb the smaller community into the larger one's culture, and what would early signals of that failure look like?

12. **Community use of school facilities after consolidation**: If a school building is closed as a facility but the community still needs a gathering space, a gym, a library annex — what obligations does the consolidation plan carry to ensure repurposed buildings remain accessible to the community that built and paid for them, and how would that be governed?

---

## Long-Term Accountability to the Students Who Remain

13. **Measuring student outcomes, not just financial outcomes**: Consolidation plans are evaluated heavily on savings — but the people the school serves are the students — what student-outcome metrics (graduation rates, chronic absenteeism, college and career readiness, belonging surveys) will be tracked post-consolidation, with what baseline, and what would trigger a reassessment of the consolidation decision if those metrics decline?
