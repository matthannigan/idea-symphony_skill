# Brainstorming Questions from The Devil's Advocate: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## The Savings Mirage: Are the Financial Projections Trustworthy?

1. **Transition costs vs. stated savings**: The feasibility study projects $1.2M in annual administrative savings, but what are the one-time transition costs — severance for displaced administrators, system integration, legal fees for contract reconciliation, bond restructuring — and how many years until the consolidated district breaks even on those upfront expenditures?

2. **Enrollment decline as the real driver**: If consolidated per-pupil funding increases by ~12% but enrollment is projected to fall another 15% by 2035, what is the net revenue trajectory for the unified district, and does administrative consolidation actually prevent the structural deficit or merely delay it by a few years?

3. **Deferred maintenance accountability**: The $4.2M in deferred building maintenance exists under the current three-district structure; what prevents a consolidated board from inheriting all of it while adding new deferred maintenance liabilities as it decides which facilities to "keep open for now," effectively kicking the problem down the road at greater scale?

4. **Transportation cost underestimation**: Consolidation of a geographically spread rural county likely means significantly longer bus routes and higher per-mile costs — what rigorous transportation modeling has been done, and could transportation cost increases partially or fully offset projected administrative savings?

---

## The Vote Math: Can a Coalition Actually Win in All Three Districts?

5. **The asymmetric losers problem**: In any consolidation, at least one (and likely two) of the three communities will lose their local school building or administrative presence — how does a campaign simultaneously promise enough to the losing community to win their vote while not undermining the perceived benefits for the community that appears to "win"?

6. **Sequential vs. simultaneous approval risk**: Requiring voter approval in all three districts creates a veto point at each — what is the contingency plan if one district votes yes and two vote no, or if one district strategically delays its vote to extract concessions, and how does the county board prevent a race-to-the-bottom negotiation dynamic?

7. **The union contract reconciliation trap**: Reconciling three separate teacher contracts into one typically results in workers migrating to the most favorable terms from each contract — what is the realistic cost of a "highest common denominator" union settlement, and has that cost been factored into the $1.2M savings estimate?

---

## Governance Risks: Who Controls the New District?

8. **Board representation imbalance**: A unified board elected from a county-wide constituency will likely be dominated by the largest community's voters — what governance structures, such as district-based seats or weighted representation, would prevent the two smaller communities from feeling permanently disenfranchised in the entity they were asked to join?

9. **Leadership vacuum during transition**: The consolidation process will likely take 2-4 years from vote to full implementation; during that period, with three superintendents in nominal authority and unclear decision rights, what prevents operational dysfunction, talent flight, and a race to encumber the new district with legacy contracts and commitments?

10. **The "unified in name only" failure mode**: Have we considered the scenario where consolidation passes the vote but results in a district that operates as three feuding sub-systems — each community protecting its programs, staff, and building — without achieving the structural integration needed for real efficiency? What governance mechanisms make genuine unification more than symbolic?

---

## Community Identity and the Hidden Costs of Losing

11. **School closure as economic catalyst for decline**: One potential challenge is that closing a school building in a small rural community does not merely affect education — it removes a community anchor, reduces property values, and can accelerate the very population decline that consolidation is meant to address. How does the feasibility study account for the downstream economic impact on towns that lose their school?

12. **The successful alternative never seriously tested**: Before committing to consolidation, have the three districts genuinely explored shared-services agreements — joint purchasing, shared superintendent, regionalized special education — that could capture some savings without requiring full consolidation and voter approval? What specific savings threshold would a shared-services model fail to reach that justifies consolidation's political cost?

---

## Institutional Failure Modes After Consolidation

13. **The insolvency backstop assumption**: The smallest district faces insolvency within two years — but consolidation itself may take longer than that to complete. What is the actual contingency if that district reaches fund exhaustion mid-process, and does the county or state have a credible backstop that does not involve simply absorbing its debt into the consolidated entity on unfavorable terms?
