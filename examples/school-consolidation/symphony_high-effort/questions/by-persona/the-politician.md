# Brainstorming Questions from The Politician: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## Stakeholder Power Map and Gatekeepers

1. **Who holds veto power before the ballot**: Beyond the formal voter approval requirement in each district, which individuals or bodies — school board members, county supervisors, teachers' union presidents, local newspaper editorial boards — have the informal authority to sink the consolidation effort before it ever reaches voters, and what does each of them need to see before they move from neutral to supportive?

2. **Opposition type diagnosis**: The resistance from "our school IS our town" advocates likely blends principled community-identity concerns, territorial anxiety about which town loses its school, and simple institutional inertia — how should the feasibility study process be designed to distinguish between these three types of opposition, since each requires a fundamentally different response strategy?

3. **The smallest district's political leverage**: The district facing insolvency in two years holds an unusual position — it has the most to gain from consolidation but also the most credible threat to derail it by going bankrupt and forcing a chaotic state-managed dissolution rather than a negotiated merger; how should the other two districts and county officials treat this asymmetric leverage when structuring the consolidation terms?

---

## Coalition Sequencing and Momentum

4. **Early-ally credibility chain**: Which constituency, if it publicly endorses consolidation first, creates the most credibility with the next tier of persuadable stakeholders — and what specific ask, concession, or structural protection would need to be offered to secure that first endorsement without overcommitting resources that are needed to win later groups?

5. **Winning the teacher vote before the public vote**: Teachers are simultaneously among the most influential voices in their communities and among those most anxious about contract reconciliation and job security; what sequence of union-engagement steps — early information access, seniority protections, salary floor guarantees, representation on the transition committee — would be necessary to move the three union leaderships from likely opposition to at minimum public neutrality before the voter campaign begins?

6. **The fiscal conservative coalition problem**: Fiscal conservatives are a natural constituency for consolidation on cost-savings grounds, but they may also be the loudest voices demanding that any projected savings be locked into tax relief rather than reinvested in programming; how should the consolidation's financial case be framed to hold this coalition together without alienating parents and educators who see reinvestment in expanded offerings as the point of merging?

---

## Governance Design as a Power-Balancing Mechanism

7. **Unified board composition and town representation**: The new unified board structure will be read by each community as a signal of whether they "won" or "lost" the consolidation — how should the board's size, election geography (district-based seats vs. at-large), and initial term staggering be designed to give each of the three towns meaningful representation without giving any single town's bloc the ability to dominate decisions about facility closures and budget allocation?

8. **Facility closure decision authority**: The politically most explosive single decision — which school buildings to close or repurpose — will land on the new unified board shortly after consolidation, before trust between the three communities has been established; what governance mechanism (independent facilities committee, binding feasibility criteria, community input process with structured weighting) could be designed now and written into the consolidation agreement to depoliticize that decision enough to survive the early years?

---

## Voter Campaign Strategy

9. **Three-district approval threshold as a structural trap**: Requiring majority approval in all three districts simultaneously means that a motivated minority in any single district can block a deal the other two have accepted — how should the campaign's targeting strategy and the consolidation agreement's terms be calibrated to ensure that the district where opposition is strongest still reaches majority support, without making commitments to that district that undermine the overall fiscal case or generate backlash in the other two?
