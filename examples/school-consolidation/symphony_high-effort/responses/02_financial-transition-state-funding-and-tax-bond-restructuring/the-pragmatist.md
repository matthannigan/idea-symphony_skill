# Financial Transition, State Funding, and Tax/Bond Restructuring - The Pragmatist

## Break-Even Timeline Including Illusory Savings

* **Front-load severance and integration realistically.** The $1.2M annual administrative savings is attractive, but transition costs are likely $2.5M–$3.8M (severance for 3 superintendents, HR/transportation directors, IT system buyouts, legal fees for union and bond restructuring, and interim governance setup). If bundled into year 1, the district breaks even in year 3–4 on administrative savings alone. However, this assumes no re-emergence of coordination overhead. Practically, a larger district will need additional administrative layers (assistant superintendents, curriculum directors, audit/compliance staff) that eat 20–30% of the projected $1.2M, pushing break-even to year 4–5. The smallest district's 24-month insolvency cliff is a hard deadline; consolidation must vote and close legal structuring within 18 months to land within that window.

* **Phase the severance costs by role urgency.** Rather than absorbing all severance in year 1, negotiate staggered exit timelines for roles that can overlap (especially transportation directors and HR). This spreads costs across years 1–2 and reduces cash-flow shock. Focus year-1 severance on the superintendent role (highest salary, quickest replacement needed) and defer transactional HR staff until the central office proves functional. This pragmatic sequencing may reduce year-1 cash outlay to $1.8M–$2.2M, letting the district show positive net savings by year 3, before the insolvency cliff becomes acute for the smallest district.

* **Account for re-emerging hidden costs candidly.** Larger districts incur overhead that small districts don't: additional compliance (federal child nutrition audits, special education due-process administration), union grievance processing, and expanded facilities-management coordination across 7 buildings. A practical estimate is 30% of the $1.2M clawed back by year 2–3, reducing net savings to $840K annually. Real break-even occurs in year 4–5 if transition costs land at $2.5M; if they exceed $3.5M (bond restructuring gets litigious), break-even slips to year 5+, which overshoots the insolvency horizon significantly.

* **Test break-even against pessimistic enrollment scenarios.** If the 15% enrollment decline through 2035 accelerates (recession, out-migration), state per-pupil funds may plateau or decline despite the 12% consolidation boost. A 3-year break-even calculation assumes stable enrollment. If enrollment drops an additional 5% unexpectedly in years 2–3, the per-pupil allocation gain evaporates, and administrative "savings" become salary reductions. A practical move: build a sensitivity model showing break-even under base-case (14% decline) and pessimistic (19% decline) enrollment scenarios. This grounds the board's conversation in realistic cash positions rather than best-case assumptions.

## Deferred-Maintenance Accountability

* **Conduct a building-by-building triage audit before voting.** The $4.2M deferred-maintenance figure is an aggregate; it masks which of the 7 buildings are sinks vs. candidates for modernization. A practical first step is a third-party facilities audit ($15K–$25K) that ranks buildings by: (1) structural/safety deficiencies requiring immediate remediation, (2) systems near end-of-life (HVAC, roofing), and (3) code compliance gaps (ADA, fire safety). This inventory gives the consolidated board a concrete decision framework: which buildings can limp through 5 years with tactical repairs, which must be extensively renovated, and which should be closed. Without this clarity, the new board inherits the accumulated burden with no strategic rationale.

* **Tie maintenance decisions to program consolidation.** Rather than asking "which buildings do we close?" first, ask "where do we put the K-2, 3-5, 6-8, 9-12 programs geographically?" Once that program footprint is decided (driven by transportation efficiency and enrollment concentration), maintenance liability follows: buildings housing active programs get maintenance dollars; underutilized buildings become closure candidates. This pragmatic sequence prevents kicking the problem down the road because the program decision creates immediate maintenance priorities.

* **Create a maintenance reserve fund from year-1 savings.** If $500K–$600K of the $1.2M administrative savings goes into a dedicated capital reserve (not general operations), the consolidated district builds a 7-year runway to address the $4.2M deferred maintenance without issuing new debt. This is a practical alternative to perpetuating deferred maintenance: it commits a portion of the benefit to the burden, rather than treating maintenance as a future problem. Frame it as "consolidation savings fund the facility strategy," not "savings go to operations while buildings deteriorate."

* **Establish a sunset rule for maintenance decisions.** The consolidated board should adopt a policy: "No building remains in the district after year 5 in deferred-maintenance status above $X (e.g., $200K structural/safety deficit)." This creates accountability; either the board funds the maintenance or closes the building by a hard deadline. Without this rule, deferred maintenance metastasizes as enrollment continues to decline and maintenance needs intensify.

## Per-Pupil Funding Step-Change in Absolute Dollars

* **Translate 12% per-pupil allocation into dollars against real enrollment curves.** Current combined enrollment is ~1,800 students. If the state per-pupil allocation today is $14,000 (rough order of magnitude), the 12% increase means an extra $1,680 per student, or ~$3M in additional annual revenue if enrollment holds. However, projected 15% decline by 2035 reduces that enrollment to ~1,530 students, so even with the 12% boost, the consolidated district has $25.6M in operating revenue by 2035 vs. the current $25.2M—a modest gain that disappears into enrollment loss. A practical calculation shows the $1.2M administrative savings only marginally offset declining per-student dollars; the district's long-term solvency depends on controlling costs through facility consolidation and staffing efficiency, not on the per-pupil formula gain alone.

* **Identify the enrollment floor that preserves funding tier eligibility.** State funding formulas often have thresholds (e.g., districts under 500 students in a tier qualify for supplemental funding; 500–1,500 are standard; above 1,500 lose certain allowances). If the consolidated district's future enrollment stability hovers near a threshold (say, 1,200–1,400 students by 2032), small fluctuations in enrollment could drop the district below threshold and trigger a sudden funding cut. A pragmatic move is to map the state's funding tiers and identify the enrollment floor the district must maintain; then model what grade-cohort sizes and student-retention efforts are required to stay above that floor.

* **Monitor state formula revision risk actively.** REAP (Rural Education Achievement Program) funding and Small Rural School Achievement designation carry substantial bonuses for small, rural districts. If these programs are defunded or consolidated district eligibility changes (consolidation disqualifies the district from "small school" bonuses), the 12% consolidation boost could evaporate. A practical safeguard is to budget conservatively, assuming the per-pupil gain holds at only 8%, and earmark the difference ($2.4M projected) as a contingency reserve. This hedges against formula revisions that could occur during the 3–5 year transition window.

* **Run a 10-year cash-flow model under three enrollment scenarios.** Best case (8% decline), base case (15% decline), and pessimistic (22% decline). In each scenario, model the per-pupil revenue assuming the 12% boost holds, then subtract realistic operating costs (salary escalation 2–3% annually, utilities inflation, debt service). This forces the board to see whether the funding boost is actually transformative or merely delays the structural problem. If the consolidated district is still running a structural deficit under base-case enrollment in year 7, the board needs a different story (service efficiency, program consolidation, shared services with other districts) to justify consolidation.

## Property Tax Equalization Winners and Losers

* **Map the assessed-value-per-pupil disparities explicitly.** If District A (wealthy suburb-adjacent) has $8M in assessed value per 300 students ($26,667 per pupil) and District C (rural) has $3M assessed value per 600 students ($5,000 per pupil), equalization means pooling assessed values and distributing tax capacity proportionally by enrollment. Under equalization, all three districts operate under the same levy rate against a blended assessed-value base. This benefits District C (its residents pay less in property tax relative to other districts) but hurts District A residents, who effectively subsidize rural students. A pragmatic communication strategy is to name this explicitly: "Equalization means higher-property-value areas contribute more proportionally. Here's who pays more, by how much, and why it's fair under consolidation."

* **Propose a multi-year phase-in to avoid rate shock in the highest-rate districts.** Rather than immediate equalization, adopt a "glide path": year 1, blend 80/20 (20% equalized, 80% old rates); year 2, 60/40; year 3, full equalization. This softens the political blow in high-property-value districts where property taxes would spike. The tradeoff is that low-property-value districts get less immediate relief, but a 3-year phase-in buys political cover in the highest-rate jurisdiction, which is critical for cross-district voter approval. This is a classic pragmatic compromise: distribute the pain over time rather than absorbing it in year 1.

* **Calculate the redistribution arithmetic in dollars for each household.** Generic statements ("equalization is fair") don't survive scrutiny. Do the math: if the average household in District A pays $1,800/year in school property tax today and faces a 15% increase under equalization, that's an extra $270/year. In District C, the average household saves $180/year. Present this in campaign materials; voters need concrete numbers to assess whether they're winners or losers. The political feasibility of consolidation depends on whether the losers (District A) are outnumbered by the winners and the genuinely persuaded, and whether the phase-in buys enough credibility to survive that tough conversation.

* **Identify if any district can opt out or negotiate alternative structures.** Some state laws allow a single district to exempt itself from equalization if voter-approved. This gives the wealthiest district a release valve: "You can stay in the old structure and fund only your students, but you lose access to shared services and the per-pupil allocation boost." This pragmatic alternative may actually improve political feasibility; it tells District A residents they have a choice, which often reduces political backlash even if most choose to stay in the unified district. Check state statute before proposing, but it's worth exploring as a negotiation point.

## Bond Obligation Allocation Across the New Entity

* **Determine which district's bonds are secured by which facilities and if that changes under consolidation.** District A may have issued bonds in 2015 for a high school renovation; those bonds are paid by District A's tax base. Under consolidation, if the unified district keeps that high school operational, the question becomes: do all unified district residents pay to retire that debt, or only the former District A residents? The practical answer depends on state law and how the consolidation restructures bonded indebtedness. A pragmatic starting point is a legal review of each outstanding bond covenant; some bonds may have language preventing assumption by other parties. If a $5M bond is secured only by District A property and requires approval from District A bondholders to transfer, the unified district may need to retire it early or negotiate a release.

* **Calculate per-pupil debt burden under consolidation.** If the three districts carry $18M in combined outstanding bonds and the consolidated district serves 1,800 students, that's $10,000 per pupil in debt. If enrollment declines to 1,530 by 2035, debt per pupil rises to $11,765 unless bonds are retired or refinanced. The practical concern is that declining enrollment erodes the property tax base paying the same debt, creating a debt-spiral risk. A pragmatic response is to explore debt consolidation and refinancing: if interest rates have dropped since the bonds were issued, the unified district may refinance at lower rates and extend the amortization, reducing annual debt service.

* **Identify which taxpayers benefit from vs. absorb legacy facility debt.** This is the hardest political problem. If District C bonds funded a school building that was closed in 2010 and no longer serves students, the new consolidated district still owes the debt; current residents of Districts A and B effectively subsidize a facility they don't use. A pragmatic mitigation strategy is to accelerate retiring debt on closed facilities (using year-1 consolidation savings and state grants for facility remediation) rather than spreading that burden across the unified entity. This frames it as "consolidation pays off old debt faster" rather than "consolidation makes us all liable for other people's buildings."

* **Propose a debt-allocation formula tied to facility utilization.** For active buildings, all unified-district residents share debt retirement proportionally; for closed buildings, retire bonds quickly using reserve funds and consolidation savings. This hybrid approach is pragmatic: it acknowledges that some legacy debt is legitimate (active facilities benefit the whole district) while ringfencing closed-building debt for accelerated retirement. It reduces the political sting of "who pays for a school they don't use?"
