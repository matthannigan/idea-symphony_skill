# Strengths, Hidden Assets, and First Experiments - The Devil's Advocate

## Peak Moments of Cross-District Collaboration

* **Collaboration as exception, not default.** Have we considered that successful collaborative moments might be outliers precisely *because* they require overcoming structural friction? If athletic competitions worked smoothly, that's because they're temporary and voluntary—consolidation is permanent and compulsory. We might address this by auditing which collaborative moments involved genuine power-sharing versus unequal benefit (one district hosting, others traveling), then designing the consolidated structure to replicate the mechanics of successful ones, not just the goodwill.

* **Existing structures as gatekeepers.** One challenge: the trusted relationships that made past collaboration work might be led by people who resist consolidation (school board members, union leaders, town advocates). These aren't neutral bridges—they're stakeholders defending their turf. We could mitigate by identifying collaborative successes that emerged from *younger* teachers, student leaders, or parents, and elevating them as models rather than deferring to institutional gatekeepers.

* **Cross-district collaboration masking internal competition.** Regional events often pit the three districts against each other athletically or academically. Have we considered whether the "natural collaboration" people remember is actually rivalry dressed in positive language? We might address this by distinguishing between competitive structures (sports leagues, academic contests) and truly integrated collaboration (shared art programs, combined AP offerings), then designing the consolidated district to build more of the latter.

## Talent and Community Assets the Consolidated District Could Amplify

* **Retention narrative may hide burnout.** Teachers who stayed despite 14 vacancies might be there because of inertia, family ties, or reduced job options—not passion. Consolidation might prompt these teachers to leave once the disruption creates a natural exit window. We should address this by interviewing retained teachers about what kept them, then protecting those specific conditions (autonomy, subject specialization, community relationships) rather than assuming staying equals thriving.

* **Hidden costs of amplifying strengths in a larger system.** A talented principal in a small district has deep community relationships and decision-making autonomy. In a consolidated district, that talent gets absorbed into a larger bureaucracy where their influence dilutes. Have we considered that the strengths we're trying to amplify might not scale or might be crushed by consolidation itself? We could mitigate by designing leadership structures that preserve pockets of autonomy and recognizing that some strengths (intimacy, trust, quick decisions) are fragile in larger systems.

* **Asset identification without equity analysis.** Which communities are the "asset-rich" ones and which are the "asset-poor" ones? If we amplify existing strengths, we might reinforce existing inequalities. We should address this by auditing which district contributed which identified strengths, then ensuring the consolidated district invests in building missing assets in the weaker communities rather than doubling down on existing advantages.

## Successful Rural Consolidations Elsewhere

* **Survivorship bias in "success" stories.** Have we considered that we'll find plenty of consolidated districts that avoided immediate insolvency, but few that genuinely strengthened community identity? The benchmark we should examine is whether those communities say consolidation enhanced their school *and* their town identity—not whether it survived. We might mitigate by insisting on tracking long-term outcomes (enrollment stability 10+ years post-consolidation, community sentiment in year 3-5, teacher retention) rather than relying on press releases from year 1.

* **Different rural contexts, different outcomes.** A successful consolidation in a Plains state with dispersed small towns might fail in a Appalachian county where each town has deep identity ties to its school. Have we considered whether "successful elsewhere" means anything here? We should address this by categorizing comparison cases by geography, demographics, and identity attachment, then honestly assessing which of those conditions match or differ from this county.

* **Hidden costs in successful cases.** Successful consolidations often involve facility closures that displaced communities lived through quietly. Have we considered that the "success" was built on political decisions that created long-term resentment in losing towns? We might mitigate by asking about facility closure decisions in comparison cases and looking for symptoms of lasting community fragmentation (civic participation, local pride) rather than just accepting the consolidated district's narrative.

## A First Experiment That Builds Confidence Before the Vote

* **Pilot programs often fail to scale.** A successful shared program might work because it's optional, limited, and managed by enthusiasts. Once mandated across all three districts, it might collapse under administrative overhead. Have we considered that a positive pilot could backfire if the full implementation disappoints? We should address this by designing the pilot to test not just the program's value but the scaled version's administration—include bureaucratic layers, enrollment conflicts, transportation logistics in the pilot itself.

* **Selection bias in volunteer participation.** The teachers, students, and parents who opt into a pre-vote experiment are likely the most consolidation-friendly subset. Their enthusiasm might not represent the skeptical majority who will vote. We could mitigate by requiring broad participation (not just volunteers) or by creating a "control" group that doesn't participate and comparing their views afterward to see if pilots actually shift skeptics or just energize existing supporters.

* **Experiment success might create false confidence.** One shared extracurricular or facility working well doesn't mean the hard parts (union contracts, facility closures, curriculum alignment) will work. Have we considered that a successful pilot could lead the board to oversell consolidation as lower-risk than it actually is? We might address this by explicitly discussing what the pilot does and doesn't prove, and by running parallel research on the unsolved problems (Which facilities will close? How will contracts reconcile?) so voters aren't seduced by soft success into voting for hard unknowns.

## The First Day at Unified

* **Identity rituals can feel forced or exclude existing traditions.** A brand-new unified identity symbol might alienate people deeply attached to their original district colors, mascots, or traditions. Have we considered that the new unified identity could feel like erasure rather than synthesis? We should address this by co-designing new rituals with representatives from all three communities and explicitly preserving the old traditions within the larger structure (e.g., homecoming competitions between old district cohorts, retaining school colors in sub-branding) rather than replacing them entirely.

* **First day logistics could undermine belonging.** If transportation is chaotic, facility assignments are unclear, or students from different districts don't know where to go, the first day becomes an exercise in confusion rather than belonging. Belonging requires clarity and smooth experience. We might mitigate by doing a detailed dry run of the first day's logistics (busing routes, cafeteria capacity, class assignments, bathrooms) with all three communities, then fixing failures *before* the actual day, not after.

* **Belonging won't happen unless the consolidated district visibly serves all three communities equally.** If one town's school closes and its students are bused to another town's facility, "feeling they belong" is asking a lot. Have we considered that belonging is downstream of closure decisions—students feel like they lost their school even if the new one is nicer? We should address this early by making facility decisions before the first day and explaining why, rather than letting first-day experience bear the weight of fixing a fundamentally divisive decision.
