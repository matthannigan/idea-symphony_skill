# Coalition Sequencing and Stakeholder Power - The Politician

## 41. The Asymmetric-Losers Problem and the Multi-Jurisdiction Veto

* **Differential campaign messaging by facility outcome.** Run three separate campaigns: Town A (loses admin but keeps K-5 campus, paint it as "preserved identity"); Town B (gets consolidated admin, frame as "leadership opportunity"); Town C (closes one building but consolidates into one, offer guaranteed programming equivalency). Each district sees itself as acceptable-loser while another takes the hit. Contingency: if one votes no, immediately announce state receivership risk for the "no" district—fear works faster than promises.

* **Advance territorial concessions through a facility committee.** Create a 9-person joint commission (3 from each district) before the campaign, let them design the facility master plan themselves, and publicly commit to honoring committee recommendations. This locks three stakeholder groups into owning the outcome (they can't campaign against themselves), and delays the "who loses their school" perception until after coalition is formed.

* **Lead with the smallest district's insolvency.** Use District C's two-year deadline as your opening argument in all three communities: "We can choose consolidation now with District C at the table, or we can watch insolvency force a chaotic takeover that nobody controls." This reframes the vote from "redistribution of losses" to "coordinated rescue," making the losers feel like they're choosing stability over chaos.

* **Tiered concession architecture with firewall.** Commit to retention promises (building Y stays open 10 years) separately from operational promises (staffing levels, programs). Fund retention commitments from state facilities grants, not local operating budgets. This keeps fiscal promises tied to real dollars, preventing the "I promised you X but the budget won't allow it" death spiral.

* **Set the race-to-the-bottom tripwire in advance.** Publicly declare before any campaign: "Concessions are off the table after one district votes." This reverses the incentive—strategically delaying your vote no longer extracts more; it costs you the deal's protections.

## 42. Coalition Fragility and the Sequence of Conflicting Concessions

* **Hierarchy of commitments: bind down hardest commitments first.** Get the union protections on paper through a memorandum of understanding with all three union locals before the voter campaign begins. Union approval is your lowest-risk lock-in because unions care more about contracts than communities do about identity—they'll evangelize. Once unions are bound, everything else flows from that credibility.

* **Fiscal reality is your boss, not your problem.** Don't frame concessions as "what we're promising"; frame them as "here's the fiscal baseline from the state auditor, here's the savings we've modeled, and here's what stays sacred (buildings, union jobs, local board representation). Everything else is derived from real numbers, not political wishes. When groups conflict, show them the math, not the politics.

* **Information asymmetry as a feature, not a bug.** Give each district a tailored feasibility report showing *their* specific benefits (District C sees insolvency-escape math, District A sees admin-cost savings, District B sees program-expansion funding). They don't compare notes until the campaign is live, by which time they've each decided their interests are served. Risk: backfires badly if they compare and feel deceived.

* **Bind the opinion leaders, not the general public.** Map the first-mover opinion influencers in each district (board members, union reps, newspaper editors, church leaders) and secure their private endorsement before any public campaign. Once these figures go public, their reputational investment makes backpedaling costly. General voters follow established opinion leaders—move the influencers, the votes follow.

* **Create cross-district task forces to pre-commit stakeholders.** Form joint implementation committees (teachers, parents, administrators from all three districts) to design the consolidated operations *before* the vote. As members see merged departments working together and solving real problems, they stop seeing consolidation as abstract threat and start seeing it as inevitable process they're shaping. Self-interest shifts from "kill it" to "make sure I'm heard in design."

## 43. Who Holds Veto Power Before the Ballot

* **The three-superintendent gate.** Each superintendent can kill consolidation by refusing to submit feasibility analysis to their board, or by publicly warning their district about risks. Secure each one's private commitment to neutral or supportive public stance *before* the formal feasibility study launches. If one is opposed, remove them from the study-design process and bring in an interim superintendent who's consolidation-agnostic.

* **The teachers' union presidents are joint gatekeepers.** If one local president opposes, they can coordinate a "no" campaign that reaches every household (union member families = voter universe in a rural district). Get all three union presidents to sign a joint endorsement of the "consolidation principle"—not the specific plan, but the idea that merger is better than insolvency. Once they're publicly aligned, they can't campaign against you.

* **County board and county supervisor alignment is the political weather.** If the board of supervisors is fractured or opposed, every local town council gets political cover to vote no. Get three of five supervisors to publicly endorse consolidation as a county priority; this signals to town mayors that opposition is politically risky. Supervisors matter because they control state-legislative relationships and facilities funding.

* **The newspaper editorial board in the largest town.** That paper's endorsement reaches all three districts. Editorial boards care about fiscal responsibility and local stability. Build the case that consolidation prevents state takeover, stabilizes property taxes, and preserves local school identity (through facility retention). If the editorial board endorses, it becomes "responsible leadership"; if they oppose, it becomes "risky experiment."

* **School board members in each district are the formal gate.** Board members must vote to move consolidation to referendum. Unlike voters (who are passive until the campaign), board members must actively say yes. Individual board members can be lobbied, influenced, or converted. Map their ideological positions (fiscal conservative vs. community-identity defender) and secure 4 of 5 in each district before you launch. If you have the boards, the voters follow.

## 44. Opposition Type Diagnosis

* **Separate surveys by opposition type: identity vs. territorial vs. inertia.** Ask targeted questions: "How much does keeping our school in town matter to your family?" (identity); "Which other town should close their school?" (territorial anxiety); "Do you oppose consolidation because you've thought through it, or because change feels risky?" (inertia). Responses tell you who to persuade (identity folks—hard; territorial anxiety—solvable through facility decisions; inertia—low-hanging fruit if you lower activation energy).

* **Run a structured focus-group process with opt-in skeptics.** Invite 8-10 known consolidation skeptics from each town to a moderated session where you present the feasibility study and ask: "What would need to be true for you to support this?" Track what matters to each person (financial security, preserved local school, union protections, lower taxes). Pattern-match to opposition type and design targeted response strategies.

* **Use facility tours as opposition-type diagnostic.** Offer tours of the consolidated district's proposed school locations, asking parents and community members what they fear losing. Listen for nostalgia and identity language (opposition type 1: "I went to this school, my kids should too"), competitive anxiety ("Why should Town B's school be the hub?"), or vague discomfort ("Seems like a big change"). Each type signals different response strategy.

* **Create a rapid-response evidence brief for each opposition type.** For identity opposition: show how local school identities and traditions survive consolidation (separate school names, community advisory committees, local athletic conferences). For territorial opposition: show the facility master plan clearly, describe what stays open where. For inertia opposition: produce simplified one-pagers with before/after budgets, enrollment trends, and staff stability. Different messages, delivered through different channels.

* **Track opposition hardness through behavioral signals, not just surveys.** Opposition that appears at board meetings, writes letters, or organizes petitions is real and requires substantive response. Opposition that exists but isn't mobilized yet is soft and responds to low-activation information (a postcard, a town hall, a local leader's endorsement). Separate your response effort accordingly—spend persuasion energy on soft opposition, spend containment energy on hard opposition.

## 45. The Smallest District's Political Leverage

* **Use D's insolvency as a forcing mechanism, not a threat to weaponize.** Publicly acknowledge District C's fiscal timeline but frame it as opportunity: "District C's situation gives us three years to plan consolidation properly, rather than lurching into crisis management." This neutralizes District C's leverage (they can't threaten chaos if you're publicly accepting their timeline) while making consolidation feel inevitable to Districts A and B (better to do this now than manage a collapse later).

* **Offer District C leadership roles in consolidated structure.** District C's superintendent becomes consolidated assistant superintendent or interim COO during transition; District C board members get 4 seats on consolidated board (proportional to enrollment). This converts District C's desperation into political capital—they become architects of their own integration rather than victims of a takeover, and they advocate aggressively to sell consolidation because their board's future depends on it.

* **Lock in District C's union concessions first.** District C's union is most desperate to avoid layoffs, so they'll agree to more flexible work rules, salary restraint, or transition provisions. Once District C union agrees, you can tell Districts A and B unions: "District C has already agreed to these terms; you can match or negotiate something equivalent." District C's desperation becomes your negotiating leverage against the other two.

* **Set a hard bankruptcy date and stick to it.** If District C's insolvency is real on a two-year timeline, state that publicly and never extend it. This prevents District C from repeatedly extending the deadline as leverage for more concessions. "If consolidation isn't approved by [date], District C enters state receivership" becomes your strongest forcing mechanism—it makes failure real and visible, not theoretical.

* **Use state education agency as honest broker.** Get state officials to publicly affirm that District C's insolvency scenario is real and that consolidation is the recommended path forward. When state actors say the math is real, District C can't use insolvency as a political bluff, and Districts A and B can't ignore it as irrelevant to them.

## 46. Early-Ally Credibility Chain

* **The teachers' unions are your first dominoes.** Get all three union locals to endorse the "consolidation principle" before any public campaign. Unions don't care about town identity; they care about job security and contracts. They reach every household through union families. Once unions are public supporters, consolidation stops feeling like a chaotic reorganization and starts feeling like a labor-backed stability plan. Next tier unlocked: parents who trust their union reps.

* **Use parent-led program advocates as second-wave credibility.** Once unions are supportive, highlight which programs expand under consolidation (advanced math, music, special ed services, athletics). Find parent advocates for those programs (band parent association, special ed parent council) and spotlight the consolidation benefits to them. These parents are credible with other parents because they care about children, not politics. Third tier unlocked: general parents now see tangible child benefit.

* **Fiscal conservatives are your third credibility wave.** Once unions (labor credibility) and parents (family credibility) are supporting consolidation, use their support to persuade fiscal conservatives that consolidation isn't a left-wing restructuring but a fiscally responsible necessity. Show the savings math, the facility efficiency plan, the administrative reduction. Fiscal conservatives who've seen union + parent support go public now feel permission to support consolidation on cost grounds. Final tier: broad community alignment.

* **County business leaders as capstone.** Get chamber of commerce, business roundtable, and major employers to publicly support consolidation as a "regional economic stability initiative." Businesses care about property-tax stability and workforce development. Once they're supporting consolidation, you've locked in every major stakeholder category (labor, families, fiscal conservatives, business). Holdout "community identity" opposition now looks isolated.

* **The smallest district's superintendent is your first-wave credential.** If District C's superintendent publicly urges their district to support consolidation (because insolvency is real), they become your first credible authority figure. They're local, they know the numbers, and they have institutional legitimacy. If District C's superintendent endorses consolidation for survival reasons, Districts A and B superintendents face pressure to not be left behind the curve.

## 47. The Fiscal Conservative Coalition Problem

* **Distinguish between two fiscal conservative subgroups: tax-relief conservatives vs. sustainability conservatives.** Tax-relief conservatives want budget cuts and lower taxes. Sustainability conservatives want a financially stable district even if it costs money. You need the latter, not the former. When fiscal conservatives ask about savings, emphasize sustainability language: "Consolidation stabilizes the district for the next 20 years" rather than "Consolidation cuts taxes immediately." Sustainability conservatives will evangelize this; tax-relief conservatives will grumble but accept it.

* **Lock savings into mandatory structural mechanisms, not discretionary decisions.** Instead of "savings will be reinvested in programming" (discretionary), commit to "administrative costs will be capped at 8% of budget, with any spending above that level triggering a board vote and public review." This satisfies tax-relief conservatives (they see fiscal control mechanisms) while protecting education spending (the cap is set at a reasonable level).

* **Create three-year revenue/reinvestment plan with explicit tradeoff choices.** Show Year 1: $1.2M in savings from eliminated duplicated admin. Detail where it goes: $400k restores teacher salary competitiveness (addresses staffing crisis, appeals to educators), $400k goes to special education and transportation (appeals to parents), $400k goes to contingency reserve (appeals to fiscal conservatives). This isn't "reinvestment vs. tax relief"; it's "here's where the savings actually go, and here's why each category matters."

* **Make the anti-consolidation scenario's fiscal case explicit.** If consolidation fails, show the 5-year budget under decline-only scenario: enrollment drops 15%, state per-pupil funding continues penalty for small districts, three districts now have no administrative efficiency. Fiscal conservatives see that staying separate is *more* expensive per child, not less. Consolidation becomes the fiscally conservative choice.

* **Find the fiscal conservative thought leaders who understand education economics.** There are conservative accountants, small-business owners, and council members who understand that school stability is good for property values and economic development. Get them to explain (not you explaining to them) why consolidation is the fiscally conservative move. Peer credibility with fiscal conservatives matters more than external advocacy.
