# Coalition Sequencing and Stakeholder Power - The Pragmatist

## Question 41: The Asymmetric-Losers Problem and Multi-Jurisdiction Veto

* **Guarantee the "winning" community and secure a binding timeline.** Rather than making equal promises to all three, publicly designate one community's school as the consolidated district's flagship campus before the vote. This eliminates the suspense that fuels opposition in that district and redirects energy toward securing the other two. The "losing" communities get a contractual facility-reuse commitment (recreation center, community college satellite) signed before consolidation takes effect, making the loss concrete and offset.

* **Decouple the building question from the consolidation vote.** Require a separate 18-month facilities task force after consolidation is approved, but binding arbitration (not board discretion) if communities disagree. This postpones the zero-sum conflict and reframes consolidation as "let's merge operations and let an impartial process decide buildings" rather than "vote yes to close your school." The psychological shift is significant: voters accept losing a building to arbitration more easily than to administrative choice.

* **Build a fallback coalition of the two most stable districts.** If the smallest (insolvency-facing) district looks like it will vote no, shift strategy: pursue consolidation of only the two larger districts, which preserves 60% of savings and sidelines the smallest district's bankruptcy threat. This is unpopular but practical. The smallest district then faces the genuine choice—join the consolidation at a disadvantage, or go bankrupt independently. The pressure reverses.

* **Embed a "protection period" for displaced school buildings.** Commit that the losing districts' buildings cannot be sold or significantly repurposed for 10 years without community board veto, and establish a reserve fund from consolidation savings specifically for alternative community use (library expansion, medical clinic, job training). This converts the building closure from permanent loss to temporary displacement and signals reinvestment in the affected community.

## Question 42: Coalition Fragility and Conflicting Concessions

* **Create a "concession register" and publish it early.** Before you campaign, write down which concessions you're offering to which groups, identify the overlaps and conflicts, and decide—explicitly—which concessions are flexible and which are non-negotiable. Then publish the register as part of the feasibility study. This eliminates the surprise-grievance dynamic: voters see the trade-offs upfront rather than learning via rumor that another district got a better deal.

* **Separate the union negotiation from the consolidation vote.** Don't try to resolve three separate union contracts as part of winning the vote. Instead, commit to a structured, independent labor negotiation 60 days post-consolidation approval, with a neutral mediator if parties can't agree within 90 days. This removes an unpredictable variable from the campaign and lets union leadership credibly tell members, "We're negotiating after consolidation is decided, not holding it hostage beforehand."

* **Appoint a "Chief Integration Officer" before the vote.** Hire a specific person (publicly named) responsible for managing the transition and trade-off conflicts. This person becomes the decision-maker for inevitable disputes—not the superintendent or board, but a dedicated neutral. Knowing a conflict has a clear resolver (and one who isn't serving a particular district's interests) reduces anxiety among all groups that they'll be blindsided or cheated.

* **Create an "early wins" protocol for post-vote integration.** Identify 2–3 consolidation benefits you can deliver within the first 90 days after the vote (e.g., filling teacher vacancies in the most understaffed district, reopening a popular program cut by financial pressures). When you campaign, commit to these specific wins and publish a timeline. Once voters see quick progress, confidence in the consolidation leadership rises and grievances from early concessions are less volatile.

## Question 43: Who Holds Veto Power Before the Ballot

* **Map the three school boards and identify the pivotal member in each.** In each district, one board member likely controls 2–3 votes through influence and institutional credibility. Before going public, secure confidential buy-in from these three pivotal members. If they're neutral or supportive, their public silence on a sensitive topic signals acceptance. If they're against it, they'll sink the entire campaign at the district level before voters even hear the case.

* **Engage the three union presidents individually and in writing.** Don't try to build consensus among all three at once; that creates a collective veto. Instead, have separate conversations with each union leader: "Here's what consolidation means for your members' contracts, job security, and compensation. We're going to move forward. Will you publicly oppose or stay neutral?" Once one leader commits to neutrality or support, the others face pressure to match rather than be the outlier opposing jobs and benefits.

* **Secure the county superintendent's active endorsement before the boards.** In many rural systems, the county superintendent has informal authority over the three district superintendents and boards. If the county superintendent says, "Consolidation serves our region's future; my office is supporting this process," it shifts the framing from district-level political threat to county-level policy direction. A weak county superintendent endorsement is worthless; you need active, public support.

* **Identify and pre-brief the three local newspaper editorial boards.** Rural newspapers have outsized influence. Schedule editorial board briefings with the chief proponent (not a consultant), present the full financial case, and ask directly, "Will you support this in your editorial coverage?" A single hostile editorial board can tank the campaign in one district. Getting an explicit editorial board commitment before the campaign announcement prevents surprise opposition during the critical middle phase.

* **Build a "physician's compact"—silent commitment from health/human services leaders.** Doctors, hospital administrators, and county health officers often carry quiet credibility in rural communities. If they say consolidation is economically sound, it carries more weight than politicians saying it. Pre-negotiate their willingness to make brief public statements ("Fiscally stronger schools help health outcomes"). This isn't a veto, but silent opposition from health leaders would undermine trust.

## Question 44: Opposition Type Diagnosis

* **Run three separate opinion surveys before the feasibility study goes public.** Use a focus-group hybrid: ask respondents to choose between "community identity concerns," "worried my town loses its school," and "skeptical of big government / prefer small schools." Separate the principled from the strategic from the inertial opposition. Then design responses accordingly: the identity group gets cultural messaging, the territorial group gets facility-reuse commitments, the inertia group gets pilot programs and quick wins. Mixing responses wastes effort.

* **Structure the feasibility study in three consecutive phases, each public.** Phase 1 (6 weeks): Confirm the financial case—can consolidation really work? Let fiscal conservatives and administrators lead. Phase 2 (6 weeks): Explore facility and service scenarios—what do communities get to keep? Involve building community leaders and union reps. Phase 3 (6 weeks): Governance and identity—what does the new district look like culturally? Involve history teachers, librarians, and longtime administrators. Each phase surfaces a different opposition type naturally, and each phase can be addressed with tailored solutions before it hardens.

* **Deploy "opposition samplers" to document actual objections during preliminary meetings.** Rather than assume opposition types, send trained facilitators to town halls and ask "Why are you concerned about consolidation?" in open-ended terms. Cluster the actual responses (not your predictions of resistance types) and respond to those clusters specifically. Communities often surprise you—the real concern might be "Will the new superintendent understand rural issues?" not "Our school is our town." Tailored responses are far more effective than generic rebuttals.

## Question 45: The Smallest District's Political Leverage

* **Offer the smallest district's superintendent a specific role in the consolidated district.** The fastest way to neutralize the smallest district's bankruptcy leverage is to give its leader something to protect—a deputy superintendent role, a campus principal position, or leadership of a key initiative. Once the superintendent has committed to a future in the consolidated system, their incentive to derail consolidation evaporates. This works only if the offer is genuine and lucrative enough to offset the political cost of backing consolidation in their district.

* **Structure the consolidation terms to favor the smallest district's fiscal recovery.** Don't try to distribute savings equally across three districts. Instead, commit that 60% of consolidated savings go directly to the smallest district's priority needs (teacher salaries, building repair, program restoration) for the first three years post-consolidation. This makes the smallest district's voters see tangible benefits quickly and neutralizes the bankruptcy leverage: they're better off consolidating than going bankrupt, and they get the biggest slice of the prize.

* **Pre-negotiate the insolvency timeline as a formal constraint.** Work with the state education office to establish that if the smallest district reaches insolvency before consolidation takes effect, the state steps in to manage its affairs. This removes the smallest district's implicit threat to force chaotic bankruptcy. The smallest district then faces the genuine choice—voluntary consolidation with favorable terms, or state-managed receivership. Consolidation becomes the less-disruptive option, removing leverage.

* **Create a "smallest-district bond" mechanism.** If the smallest district votes yes but one of the larger districts votes no, commit that the smallest district will be fast-tracked into the consolidated district even if the full three-way consolidation fails. This removes the domino risk: the smallest district can't be stranded if it votes yes and the others don't. It also removes the largest district's leverage to demand concessions under threat of "the smallest district will go bankrupt anyway, so vote yes for them." Each district becomes independent in its choice, eliminating leverage games.

## Question 46: Early-Ally Credibility Chain

* **Secure the primary teachers' union endorsement first.** If the largest district's union president publicly supports consolidation (ideally with a written agreement protecting member jobs and contract status), it legitimizes the effort for the broader parent community and teachers in the other two districts. Union leaders are credible to their members in a way politicians are not. The specific ask to the union president: written job security for current members, union recognition and contract renewal rights in the consolidated district, and a seat on the consolidation transition committee. This is expensive but foundational.

* **Get the county school board chair to endorse next, followed by all three district boards.** After the largest union commits, the county school board chair's endorsement makes consolidation feel "official" rather than experimental. Then pursue individual district board endorsements district-by-district, largest to smallest. Each board endorsement creates peer pressure on the next board ("if the larger district's board is backing it, we should too"). The smaller districts' boards follow, not lead.

* **Recruit a retired superintendent from a neighboring county to be the lead spokesperson.** Someone who has already successfully consolidated districts elsewhere carries immediate credibility. They don't face the suspicion that they're protecting their own job. Offer them a modest consulting contract and have them lead the public case. When they say "I've done this twice and it works if you do it right," rural communities listen more than when a current superintendent says it.

* **Identify the three most respected community figures in each district (teacher, pastor, business owner) and secure their verbal support before the public campaign.** These figures aren't on the school board or city council; they're trusted by neighbors. Once they agree to say publicly, "I support consolidation because it protects our children's future," it shifts the social math. Opposition then has to argue against the barber, the pastor, and the retired principal—not just politicians.

## Question 47: The Fiscal Conservative Coalition Problem

* **Separate savings messaging by audience.** In fiscal-conservative settings, lead with "Consolidation saves $1.2M annually in administrative overhead—that's money for tax relief or debt repayment, your choice." For parent/teacher audiences, lead with "Consolidation frees up $1.2M to restore teacher salaries, technology, and Advanced Placement courses that budget cuts eliminated." Don't try to hold both groups with the same message. Run two separate communications tracks targeting each coalition with the appeal that matters most to them.

* **Establish a transparent "Savings Allocation Committee" before the vote.** Don't promise that all $1.2M goes to tax relief (fiscally conservative) or all of it goes to programming (parent/educator coalition). Instead, commit that post-consolidation, an appointed committee (with fiscal conservative and educator representatives) will recommend how each dollar is allocated, with public voting required for any allocation over 10% of the savings. This delays the conflict and gives both groups a stake in the outcome. Fiscal conservatives see structural cost control; educators see reinvestment potential.

* **Lead with a "Consolidation Baseline Budget" that assumes zero reinvestment.** Show the three districts' budgets merged without any savings reallocation—just pure operational efficiencies and administrative reduction. Then say, "This is the floor. Any savings beyond this can be allocated by the community—tax relief, expanded programming, facility repair, or bonuses to retained teachers. But the cost-cutting commitment is firm regardless of what you vote for." This reassures fiscal conservatives that savings are real and aren't a hidden platform for spending.

* **Create a binding "tax levy cap" as part of the consolidation agreement.** Commit that the consolidated district's tax levy cannot increase for seven years unless voters specifically approve it in a referendum. This locks fiscal conservatives in: they know consolidation won't become a vehicle for tax increases. Simultaneously, commit to a "reinvestment floor"—at least 40% of year-one savings must go to teacher salary restoration to retain staff. Both groups get a binding structural protection and a floor of predictability.
