# Facilities, Transportation, and Operating Economics - The Accountant

## Facility Scoring Matrix and Irreversibility

* **Weighted scorecard driven by long-term occupancy and fixed-cost obligation.** I'd weight the matrix heavily toward utilization (current % capacity, 5-year projected enrollment trend at that site, grade-span fit) and 30-year occupancy cost obligation. A building that costs $400K/year to operate and maintain but would serve only 300 students post-consolidation is a fiscal anchor. Secondary weight to geographic centrality only insofar as it reduces transportation cost (measurable delta) and community-anchor function only if the building has documented alternative revenue (community rentals, county services co-location). The irreversibility trigger is after a closure, monitor building deterioration cost. If preventive maintenance drops to $5K/year and deferred work accumulates to $50K+, the "value destroyed by delay" signal is clear before it becomes a full gut-renovation at $2M+.

* **Scorecard minimalism—avoid the 47-factor trap.** I've seen facilities matrices with 30+ criteria that produce pseudo-precision and mask political decisions as technical ones. For 7 buildings and declining enrollment, three drivers matter: current utilization (surveyed capacity with actual enrollment), 10-year fixed operating cost per pupil (utilities, custodial, core maintenance), and closure ripple cost (bond acceleration, community litigation insurance, transition severance for custodial staff at that site). Weight them 50%-30%-20%. Geographic centrality is included only as a transportation-cost proxy (calculate route-miles saved). Everything else is politics dressed as analysis.

* **Real-time occupancy and asset condition as the reversibility monitor.** Close a building only if you've instrumented it with clear stop-loss metrics: e.g., "if average daily enrollment at this site falls below 250 students, closure triggers automatically." This removes discretion mid-consolidation and lets you reverse within 2-3 years if enrollment stabilizes. Pair it with mandatory annual condition assessments (deferred maintenance, roof/HVAC remaining life); if a "closed" building's deferred work exceeds annual operating cost, the decision has likely become practically irreversible because remediation now costs more than reopening.

* **Risk-weighted framework for politically fragile decisions.** The real irreversibility isn't the building—it's community trust. A closure decision that survives legally but fractures the new unified board's legitimacy has destroyed future governance capacity. I'd embed a "reversibility option" cost into each scenario: calculate the 5-year cost of keeping one borderline building open as "insurance" against political backlash, then compare it to estimated litigation and staff turnover costs if closure destroys coalition. Often that insurance is cheap relative to the risk.

## Total Cost of Ownership and Bond-Acceleration Risk

* **Explicit 10-year TCO model with contingent-liability reserve.** For each facility configuration, model: utilities (heating/cooling per square foot in rural context, roughly $1.20-$1.80/sq ft/year), custodial (usually $0.40-$0.60/sq ft/year, scaled to building age and complexity), maintenance (preventive 1-2%, emergency/deferred 0.5-1%, higher for older buildings), capital improvements at surviving buildings to backfill closure impact (e.g., if you close a north-county school, surviving school in that region needs added capacity investment—gymnasium, cafeteria—roughly 10-15% of building value over 5 years). Now calculate bond acceleration: most rural school bonds include change-of-control or facility-disposition clauses. If you close a school on a facility bond, you may trigger prepayment requirements or lose bond reserve funds. Request legal review of all 3 districts' bond documents; the acceleration risk could be $2-5M of unbudgeted payoff. Reserve 15-20% of claimed savings for contingent litigation and transition costs.

* **Operating-cost reality check: small districts have load-leveling costs hidden in depreciation.** The $1.2M admin savings is real, but facilities don't scale linearly. If you close 2 of 7 buildings, you don't save 29% of utilities and custodial; you save maybe 20-25% because consolidated schools run at lower utilization (you can't perfectly pack kids into 5 buildings). A 1,500-student district in 5 buildings, assuming 70% average utilization, has consolidated fixed costs of roughly $6-7M/year (utilities, custodial, core maintenance, district-wide FM staff). Model the full range: close 1 building (~4% cost reduction), close 3 (~8-10%), consolidate to 2 sites (~15%). The difference between these scenarios is material and often determines whether consolidation pencils out.

* **Bond risk is binary; model the two scenarios separately.** Some rural consolidations trigger bond prepayment; others don't (depends on your specific indentures). Don't hedge—calculate two 10-year TCO models: "acceleration triggered, $X million prepayment required immediately" and "no acceleration." The financial case for consolidation might work in scenario 2 and fail in scenario 1. Get legal certainty before finalizing the case.

## Repurposing as Community-Anchor Preservation and Balance-Sheet Lever

* **Repurposing is financially viable only if it replaces revenue and reduces vacancy-holding costs.** The anchor-institution reframe is intellectually honest, but it's also emotionally comforting in ways that obscure the hard numbers. A closed school building held vacant costs $100-150K/year (utilities on skeleton crew, roof maintenance, property tax, insurance). A repurposed building used for adult learning or early childhood can cover that only if it generates positive cash flow or saves the district money elsewhere. For rural areas, the realistic revenue models are: (1) early childhood / pre-K hub (district subsidizes or uses state pre-K funding—requires growth in that population), (2) adult learning / workforce training (joint venture with community college—revenue share on tuition, but execution risk is high), (3) community center leased to county or nonprofits (rent typically $15-30K/year for rural spaces—barely covers 20% of holding costs). The honest bottom line: repurposing is worth exploring if a partner (county, nonprofit, community college) commits to a lease with escalation clauses, but don't count the revenue in core consolidation savings until the lease is signed.

* **Adaptive reuse works when the building's fixed assets—size, location, parking—have secondary-use economics.** A closed elementary school (15,000 sq ft, built-in cafeteria, gym, parking for 400+) repurposed as a regional pre-K hub (capacity 150-200 kids) is a good match. A closed rural middle school repurposed as a "workforce training center" with declining county population is speculative. I'd require a partner letter of intent (county health department, community college, nonprofit) before committing the building. Without a partner, mothball (minimal spend—$40-50K/year, including inspections and roof management) rather than invest in repurposing.

* **Disposition value is lower than you expect in rural markets.** A closed school building might sell for $500K-$1M depending on local real estate markets, but disposition costs (environmental audit, remediation if any, listing, realtor fees) eat 15-25% of sale price. The net proceeds are modest. More important: who buys a 40,000 sq ft rural school? Prison, mental-health facility, immigrant detention (politically radioactive), or speculative real estate that may never develop. I'd model disposition as generating minimal net cash; treat any proceeds as a bonus, not a budget requirement.

* **Endowment or debt reduction is the only durable capital structure.** If you do generate proceeds from repurposing or sale (rare), don't allocate to one-time capital improvements. Establish an endowment fund (modest target: $200-400K, generating $8-16K/year in perpetuity for building maintenance on surviving schools) or dedicate to bond paydown. One-time capital repair is tempting but locks you into ongoing maintenance burden.

## Transportation Cost Delta and Student-Experience Burden

* **Route-mile cost is measurable; student burden is not, and that's the real risk.** Transportation cost in rural districts runs $1.20-$1.80 per mile driven. If consolidation adds 500 miles/day to your fleet (plausible for a geographically spread county), that's $600-900/day, or $110-165K/year in incremental routing costs. This number is real and should appear in the financial model. The trickier piece: longer bus rides (45-60 min each way vs. current 20-25 min) affect homework completion, extracurricular participation, and late-arrival tardiness rates. These outcomes vary by part of the county. A north-county student now facing 50-minute rides might drop sports/clubs (loss of enrichment, lower college competitiveness), skip homework (academic friction), or exhibit fatigue-related behavioral issues. These don't appear in the budget, but they're real costs—to families, to future achievement, to your consolidated district's academic reputation.

* **Transportation is where consolidation savings get clawed back.** The $1.2M admin savings sounds like the justification for consolidation. But as you close schools and consolidate transportation, fleet utilization improves, but so does per-pupil transport cost. A disaggregated estimate: (1) close 2 schools, consolidate to 5 sites: roughly +$80-120K/year in routing. (2) Close 3 schools, consolidate to 4 sites: +$140-180K/year. By the time you realize schedule flexibility is lost (can't offer early/late start times because you're running a unified bus schedule), the transportation tail has begun to wag the facilities dog. I'd mandate that transportation cost increases cannot exceed 25% of claimed administrative savings without a re-evaluation trigger.

* **Route-mile costs hide driver shortage and wage pressure.** Rural districts are struggling to fill bus-driver positions; consolidation reduces the number of distinct routes, but it increases per-route complexity and driver fatigue (longer days). You'll likely need to pay drivers more to retain them post-consolidation. Budget an additional 5-10% on driver wages as a "consolidation friction cost."

* **Differential impact across geography demands subsidized equity model.** If consolidation imposes 50-minute rides on low-income families in geographically distant towns, and 20-minute rides on families near the new central sites, you've created a hidden equity problem: students in remote areas have lower extracurricular access, lower college-prep rigor, and lower completion rates. The solution is either (1) keep a satellite academic hub in remote areas (adds cost), (2) fund after-school programs near remote communities (adds cost), or (3) accept the outcome and budget for lower achievement in affected areas. The financial model should name the choice explicitly.

## Hidden Ongoing Operating Cost Structure

* **Grade consolidation creates teaching-position backfill costs not captured in "reduce administrative overhead."** Here's the real structure: you save 3 superintendent positions (~$400K), 3 HR directors (~$240K), 3 transportation directors (~$180K) = ~$820K in direct admin savings. But consolidate grades 6-8 from two schools into one, and that one school must now serve 800 kids across 6-8 core periods per day with more diverse skill levels. Class sizes increase, elective options decrease, or you need 2-3 additional teachers to maintain class size and program breadth. That backfill (2 teachers @ $60K/year + benefits = ~$150K/year) partially offsets admin savings. The net is now ~$670K/year, not $1.2M. I've seen districts model this incorrectly by treating teaching costs as fixed, when they're actually variable with enrollment and program structure.

* **Custodial and utilities load concentrates differently.** Consolidating 7 schools into 4-5 reduces total custodial headcount, but the remaining buildings are older, larger, and more complex. A 40,000 sq ft rural elementary can be serviced by 3 custodians; a consolidated K-6 building with 1,200 students might need 5-6 (older buildings are less efficient, more surface area, more systems to maintain). Utilities don't scale linearly either. A half-empty 30,000 sq ft building costs roughly the same to heat as a 35,000 sq ft building at 80% capacity. The consolidation "efficiency gain" is modest—maybe 15-20% reduction in custodial FTE and 10-15% in utilities, not 30-40%.

* **Facilities management overhead often increases, not decreases, post-consolidation.** A superintendent of a 600-student district manages 2-3 buildings; a superintendent of a 1,500-student consolidated district manages 4-5 buildings with more complex interdependencies. The district-level FM director role doesn't shrink—it expands, because now there's more scheduling complexity, more preventive maintenance coordination, and more capital-project management. Budget an increase of 10-15% in FM staff time even as overall district overhead shrinks.

* **Year-one transition costs are large and often under-budgeted.** Consolidation requires IT infrastructure integration ($50-100K), student information system migration ($30-50K), staff training on unified procedures ($20-40K), legal/consulting fees for governance and contract reconciliation ($100-150K). These are one-time, but they pile up. The full year-one cost to integrate three districts operationally is often $250-400K.

## Facility Closure Decision Authority

* **Independent facilities committee with pre-negotiated criteria removes the political target.** Here's the structure I'd recommend: before consolidation vote, all three communities agree to establish a Facilities and Long-Range Planning Committee (7-9 members: 2 reps from each original district, 1 superintendent, 1 parent representative). This committee is tasked with evaluating all 7 schools against a pre-agreed scorecard (utilization, 10-year fixed cost per pupil, transportation impact, bond implications). The committee has 6 months post-consolidation to present closure recommendations. Crucially, the criteria and weighting are set now, not after consolidation, so the new board is executing a pre-negotiated plan, not deciding on the fly. This removes the optics of "board created by consolidation now closes our school"—it's the agreement you made upfront.

* **Binding feasibility criteria with built-in reversibility options.** Rather than leaving closure authority to the board, embed it in the consolidation agreement itself: e.g., "Any school with average daily attendance below 250 students in year 2 post-consolidation is recommended for closure, with a 6-month reversibility window if enrollment rebounds." This creates legal clarity and reduces the number of ad-hoc decisions the new board must make. It also gives communities a concrete metric: "If we stay above 250 ADM, our school stays open for the first five years." The reversibility window is key—it signals you're not abandoning communities permanently, just reallocating temporarily.

* **Weighted input process with published criteria, not consensus.** Don't solicit "community feedback" on closure decisions; it won't produce consensus. Instead, publish the scorecard, gather structured input (each community submits 3-5 data points or narratives in response to specific criteria), and let the committee deliberate in public. Make the reasoning transparent, but recognize that each community will advocate for its school. The process legitimacy comes from the criteria being clear and applied consistently, not from everyone being happy.

* **Budget for transition support in affected communities to separate the political blast.** If you close North School (400 students), plan 18-month transition support: bus pickups at the closed site for 6 months, a community task force to plan the mothballed building's future (repurposing options), and a $50K allocation for community events that acknowledge the change. This is cheap relative to the legal risk and staff morale damage if you just close the school and move on.

## The School-as-Anchor Parallel and Community Economic Development Reframing

* **Anchor institution framework is accurate but its economic implication is stark: closure threatens secondary activity.** Urban planners are right—when a hospital closes, surrounding businesses (cafes, dry cleaners, pharmacies) lose foot traffic and often close within 18-24 months. A school closure works the same way in small towns: teacher spending ends, student-activity-related spending (gas, food, services) evaporates. The secondary economic impact can exceed the school's direct payroll. A 400-student school with 50 teachers and staff generates roughly $3-4M in direct district spending; secondary multiplier effects (teachers buying groceries, gas, services locally) add another $1-2M in local economic activity. If closure eliminates that $5-6M circulation, you're asking the town to absorb a measurable economic contraction. The financial model for consolidation should quantify this: if you're saving $1.2M in administrative overhead but destroying $2M in secondary economic activity in affected communities, the net case is weaker than it appears.

* **Adaptive reuse and co-location are the economic-development strategy.** Rather than mothballing a closed school, the consolidation agreement should earmark capital dollars ($100-200K per closed building) to recruit community partners: county health clinic (40-50 jobs, $2M annual payroll), adult education provider (12-15 jobs), preschool hub with slots for 120+ children (12-15 jobs). A closed school converted into a multi-use community facility retains some of the secondary spending multiplier and gives families who lost the school anchor a reason to see consolidation as a net benefit, not a loss. This is harder than just closing the building, but it's the mitigation strategy that works economically.

* **Alternative anchors and distributed service models might be more resilient than fighting closure.** Some rural communities are experimenting with moving away from single-anchor dependence. Rather than one school, one hospital, one main street, they're investing in broadband, enabling remote work, and distributing services (telemedicine, online education, co-working hubs). A consolidated district might position itself as the hub for a county-wide tele-tutoring network or workforce-training platform, rather than doubling down on geographic centralization. This is a 10-year play, not a consolidation payoff, but it's worth naming in the long-range financial plan.

* **The anchor-reframe puts consolidation in civic development context, not just budget-cutting.** Boards and unions resist consolidation because it reads as "we're cutting costs by cutting services." Reframing as "we're consolidating administration so we can invest in community anchors and economic resilience" is different. It means: we save $1.2M, we invest half in teacher compensation and enrichment (making the remaining schools more robust), and half in adaptive reuse and community partnerships. This narrative works politically and creates an actual economic case for communities to accept closure of their immediate school building.
