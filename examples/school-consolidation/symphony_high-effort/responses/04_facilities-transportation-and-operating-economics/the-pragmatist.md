# Facilities, Transportation, and Operating Economics - The Pragmatist

## Facility Scoring Matrix and Irreversibility

* **Start with a three-factor system now, expand later.** Rather than waiting for the perfect 6-factor matrix, use condition + utilization + geographic centrality as your initial decision framework. This can be documented in 6-8 weeks with existing asset data; add transportation-cost and community-anchor layers once the unified board is operational. The first decision (which 2-3 buildings to retain immediately) doesn't require perfection—it needs defensibility.

* **Monitor occupancy trends, not just enrollment.** Set a clear trigger: if a building's annual per-student utilization drops below 60% and stays there for two consecutive years, that signals an irreversibility risk. Attach this rule to the consolidation agreement now so future boards can't second-guess the decision later. Once you've closed a building and shifted transportation patterns, reopening it costs 3-4x the savings you achieved.

* **Commission a facility conditions audit within the first 90 days of consolidation.** A $15k-25k independent engineering assessment of all seven buildings (HVAC, roof, electrical, ADA compliance) gives you objective cost-of-operation data, not assumptions. This removes the "we didn't know the costs" excuse later and anchors facility decisions in hard numbers rather than politics.

## Total Cost of Ownership and Bond-Acceleration Risk

* **Build a transparent 10-year model by month 4 of consolidation.** Break down utilities (by building and usage pattern), custodial staffing (FTE per building), routine maintenance reserves, and deferred-maintenance catch-up. Include the cost of mothballing (utilities reduction, winterization, maintenance skeleton crew) for each building variant. This model doesn't predict the future perfectly, but it prevents decisions driven by guesswork. Budget $8k-12k for a consultant to build this if in-house capacity is thin.

* **Identify bond-obligation triggers in writing before the consolidation vote.** Consult the district's bond counsel now: which facility closures trigger acceleration clauses? Which require bondholder notification? Which property sales would flow to bond debt reduction versus operational reserve? Get this in writing and share it with the board during the campaign—transparency on debt risk actually builds trust rather than eroding it.

* **Reserve $200k-400k of year-one operational savings for one-time closure costs.** Stripping HVAC systems, environmental remediation, foundation winterization, and property disposition aren't free. If you run out of money mid-closure, you end up with a half-abandoned building and damaged credibility. Setting this aside upfront signals competence to bond ratings agencies and communities alike.

## Repurposing as Community-Anchor Preservation and Balance-Sheet Lever

* **Identify the three buildings with best adaptive-reuse potential immediately.** A building close to a town center, with parking, and decent envelope condition has genuine market value as a community center or early-childhood hub. A building 3 miles from town on a dead-end road does not. Be realistic: in a rural context, you're looking at $50k-150k annual operating subsidy for a true community hub, not a revenue generator. The value is political stability and long-term community investment, not cash flow.

* **Pilot a community-center model with one building in year one.** Rather than closing and mothballing, immediately transition one underutilized K-12 facility to a multipurpose community hub with ECE (early childhood education), adult literacy, and meeting space. Seek $150k-250k in grant funding from state education or workforce agencies (many have rural consolidation support programs). This buys time on irreversibility decisions while building trust that the unified district isn't just ripping facilities out of communities.

* **Structure proceeds from any building sales strategically.** If you sell a building, don't dump the proceeds into the general fund—this creates a one-time revenue bubble that masks structural deficits and is politically harder to defend later. Instead, establish a $250k-500k capital-maintenance endowment fund that generates $12k-25k annually for deferred-maintenance work across surviving buildings. This locks savings into facility quality and signals 30-year thinking rather than short-term budget relief.

## Transportation Cost Delta and Student-Experience Burden

* **Map current routes and actual ride times before consolidation.** You need a baseline: how many K-5 students currently ride 30+ minutes, and what's the effect on homework, extracurricular participation, and fatigue? A 45-60 minute ride each way is real burden on elementary students and a recruitment risk for teachers who want to offer after-school clubs. Quantify this now so you can set realistic targets and defend transportation investments later.

* **Budget for a 15-20% increase in transportation cost, not the headline 8-12% savings.** The $1.2M administrative savings looks good until you realize that consolidating 7 buildings into 4 adds 180 miles of daily routes, fuel costs up 22%, and you need two additional bus drivers. A practical approach: assume transportation eats $150k-200k of your administrative savings in year one. Plan for this in your budget modeling or you'll face a crisis by October.

* **Prioritize feeder patterns that keep elementary kids on buses under 35 minutes.** Don't solve the math problem of "minimize miles" without asking "what's educationally defensible?" Consolidating middle schools across 25 miles is manageable; putting a 5-year-old on a 50-minute bus creates parent rebellion and fatigue-related learning losses you can't measure in the budget. Set transportation ride-time targets in policy now, before routing becomes a battle.

## Hidden Ongoing Operating Cost Structure

* **Model staffing-ratio impacts by grade span before consolidation.** If you're consolidating three K-12 districts into one K-5, 6-8, 9-12 structure, you may need to backfill teaching positions at middle and high school (special education, math, AP courses) because you now have critical mass for courses that were ghosted before. This isn't an administrative saving—it's a necessary investment. A pragmatic approach: factor in $80k-120k of "new staffing to enable consolidation quality" in your budget for years 1-3, then phase it out as you achieve economies of scale.

* **Separate the facilities-management cost of one large building from three small ones.** A 400-student elementary school needs one full-time custodian; a 150-student one needs 0.5 FTE minimum (you can't really hire 0.5, so costs are lumpy and inefficient). When you consolidate, you gain efficiency but you also concentrate risk: one HVAC failure at your single high school affects 800 students, not 250. Budget $40k-60k annually for a facilities manager role that doesn't exist in three-district model—this person pays for themselves in preventive maintenance alone.

* **Track utilities consumption by square footage, not by building count.** Closing two small schools and concentrating students in one medium-sized building often costs more per student in utilities because you're now running one larger HVAC system instead of three smaller ones. Your year-one utilities bill may actually go up. This is counterintuitive but real—plan for it and don't be blindsided by the operational reality in November of year one.

## Facility Closure Decision Authority

* **Create an independent Facilities Committee with binding criteria, seated in the consolidation agreement itself.** This committee should have 5-7 members: two board members (rotating), two facility-management professionals from outside the district, and one community representative (rotating). Give them authority to recommend closures, but require the full board to vote on actual closure (maintaining political accountability). The key: write their decision criteria into the agreement now, before consolidation votes, so nobody can claim they didn't see how decisions would be made.

* **Use a third-party consultant to author the facilities decision framework.** Don't have the unified superintendent or board write this—it will be torn apart as biased. Instead, hire an education facilities consultant ($25k-40k) to author a neutral, publicly-available decision matrix before consolidation. This person becomes the arbiter when debates heat up: "We're following the framework that was commissioned before consolidation, not making it up as we go." This is an investment in political legitimacy, not just a technical exercise.

* **Phase facility decisions: announce the 3-year strategic vision but defer the irreversible closure call.** In year one, retain all seven buildings but run tight controls: consolidate food service, reduce custodial hours at underutilized buildings, and create clear data on utilization and costs. In year two, announce which buildings will close in year three, using data not ideology. This gives communities time to adapt, secures buy-in through observation rather than announcement, and reduces the initial political shock that kills consolidation votes.

## The School-as-Anchor Parallel

* **Reframe facility decisions as community-development strategy, not cost-reduction exercise.** The headline "Close two schools, save $300k" triggers identity-based resistance. The same consolidation framed as "Consolidate K-12 sites into four regional hubs, invest $150k in turning the now-empty elementary building into a community learning center and childcare hub, partner with the county on co-location of job training" creates stakeholder coalition. This isn't spin—it's honest reframing that extends the consolidation's value beyond budget neutrality.

* **Identify what replaces the school as a community anchor before you close the building.** Urban planners have learned the hard way: closing an anchor without a succession plan leaves a vacuum that gets filled by decline, not by healthy alternatives. Concrete approach: before closing a rural school building, secure commitments—in writing, with funding—for its next use. This might be a community center, a health clinic, an adult learning center, or a county workforce office. The building stays alive; the community stays anchored; the consolidation stays politically sustainable.

* **Seek county/state partnership funding for anchor-replacement projects.** Most state education agencies have rural consolidation support budgets. Some have economic development programs that fund adaptive reuse of public buildings. Practical approach: as part of your consolidation feasibility study, reach out to state education department and economic development office now and ask, "If we consolidate and want to preserve three buildings as community anchors, what grant streams exist to support that?" You may discover $200k-500k in external funding that changes the entire economics and politics of the decision.
