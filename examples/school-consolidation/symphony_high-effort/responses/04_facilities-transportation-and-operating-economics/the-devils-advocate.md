# Facilities, Transportation, and Operating Economics - The Devil's Advocate

## Facility scoring matrix and irreversibility

* **Weighted criteria alone won't capture community attachment.** A numeric matrix might rank the oldest or smallest school for closure, but that building may be the only community gathering point in a town of 400. Have we considered that the irreversibility signal—litigation, rapid property deterioration, or a sudden revenue shortfall—will arrive only *after* closure? We could address this by requiring a 2-year post-closure monitoring period with explicit buy-back rights if a closure threshold is breached (e.g., if consolidated transportation costs exceed projections by 20%), and by building community identity mapping into the weighting rubric itself, not just facility metrics.

* **Condition scores degrade unpredictably.** The framework assumes facility condition is stable and measurable, but aging rural buildings often experience sudden roof failures or heating-system collapses. What if a "viable" secondary school building fails a critical safety inspection 18 months after consolidation? We could mitigate this by obtaining current structural engineering assessments, setting aside a 10% capital reserve specifically for surprise facility interventions, and establishing a "condition improvement fund" that retroactively improves secondary buildings before closure decisions are locked in.

* **The framework may systematically favor urban/central buildings over rural ones.** Geographic centrality favors the mid-county location, but transportation cost implication likely favors the same building. Rural satellite buildings lose on every dimension. Have we considered whether a weighted system inherently guarantees the closure of the most geographically isolated—and community-dependent—schools? We should introduce a "rural equity coefficient" that explicitly weights community anchor function at 40%+ of the total score, ensuring at least one satellite facility survives the first round of closures.

## Total cost of ownership and bond-acceleration risk

* **Mothballing costs exceed mothballing assumptions.** The phrase "mothballing the rest" glosses over property taxes, liability insurance, and periodic safety inspections for closed buildings. Rural counties often carry closed school buildings for 5-10 years; the cost of maintaining a building in non-operational state may approach 30-40% of operating cost. Have we costed out the realistic mothballing liability per building? We should model a year-by-year P&L for each facility configuration including property taxes, insurance, and caretaker costs, then stress-test the 10-year horizon with one facility emergency (roof, foundation) that requires $150k-$500k intervention.

* **Bond acceleration terms are district-specific and poorly understood.** The feasibility study likely treats all bonds as passive, but rural district bonds often carry unusual covenants—facility-pledge requirements, enrollment triggers, or revenue-pledge language that could activate with facility closures. Have we obtained the full bond indentures from all three districts and requested legal review of closure-triggered acceleration? We could hire bond counsel now (cost: $3-5k) to map trigger points, then structure closure decisions to avoid the highest-risk bonds in years 1-3, buying time for market refinancing.

* **Contingent liabilities hide in facility disposition.** Selling a closed school building to a private buyer seems like revenue, but rural school buildings often sit on contaminated land (old boilers, asbestos, underground storage tanks). Environmental remediation can cost $50-200k and may trigger seller liability for decades. Have we commissioned Phase I environmental assessments on all seven buildings? We should establish a facility disposition reserve (2-3% of sale value per building) held in escrow, and require Phase II assessments on any buildings targeted for sale or community repurposing.

## Repurposing as community-anchor preservation and balance-sheet lever

* **Repurposing success rates are much lower in true rural areas than suburbs.** Early childhood hubs and community health outposts work well when they're 10 minutes from dense population; in a county where towns are 20-30 miles apart, a health clinic in a former school building faces transportation barriers and low utilization. Have we audited similar repurposing attempts in truly rural districts (not just exurban ones near metro areas)? We should require a market feasibility study per building—interviewing potential partners (health departments, workforce agencies, nonprofits)—before committing to repurposing. Buildings that fail the feasibility test should be candidly earmarked for long-term mothballing or sale, not speculative "community asset" framing.

* **Rental-income projections assume stable tenants.** A community center or workforce training hub sounds viable, but rural nonprofits are fragile—a single grant loss or director departure destabilizes them. What if a repurposed building loses its primary tenant within 3 years? We could address this by structuring partnerships as shared-facility agreements (not single-tenant leases), diversifying revenue across 2-3 community organizations per building, and requiring a 5-year operating reserve ($50-100k per building) to weather tenant transitions without immediately resorting to property sale.

* **Endowment framing masks ongoing operating burden.** Putting sale proceeds into an endowment (say, $200k per building at 4% yield = $8k annual income) sounds sustainable, but closed school buildings still need utilities, property insurance, and custodial care—$30-50k annually. The endowment covers 16-26% of ongoing costs; the repurposing partner bears the rest. Have we confirmed that community partners can absorb that burden? We should reframe the question: instead of "How do we preserve community anchors via repurposing?", ask "What community partners have demonstrated financial sustainability for 5+ years and can absorb facility operating costs?" Only buildings with credible long-term tenants should be repurposed; others should be honestly sold or mothballed.

## Transportation cost delta and student-experience burden

* **Ride duration varies wildly by facility configuration—the model may hide this.** A 45-60 minute bus ride assumes a single facility configuration, but rural districts with multiple small facilities might achieve shorter average rides. Have we modeled the transportation impact per configuration, showing how ride duration changes for different student cohorts? We could address this by requiring a GIS routing study for each facility scenario, mapping actual bus routes and ride-time distributions. If any configuration pushes more than 15% of students over 50 minutes one-way, that configuration should be rejected or mitigated (e.g., offering in-district student transportation funding to families who home-school or hire alternative carpool arrangements).

* **Homework completion and achievement gaps widen with longer commutes.** The prompt notes this outcome but treats it as informational; it's actually a deal-killer for some configurations. Rural students already face economic disadvantages; longer commutes mean less homework time, fewer after-school activities, and earlier graduation rates among families without in-home academic support. Have we costed out the academic intervention required to offset a 45-minute commute (tutoring, study halls, online resources) per student? We should model consolidation scenarios with a constraint: no configuration that increases the mean ride duration for any school by more than 10 minutes is acceptable without paired academic support funding. If the savings model doesn't allow for that support, the consolidation calculus changes fundamentally.

* **Bus scheduling inflexibility locks in inefficiency.** Rural consolidation assumes a fixed transportation cost per route-mile, but consolidated routes become more complex and less efficient. A route that now visits three satellite schools might become one long loop; drivers work longer days; fuel and wage costs per student-mile climb despite economies of scale. Have we modeled dynamic routing (staggered school start times, multi-school pickup clusters) or does the baseline assume fixed 7:30 and 2:30 dismissal for all buildings? We could reduce transportation cost growth by introducing a 30-minute "dismissal window" (7:20-7:50 for different grades/buildings) allowing optimized route clustering. This requires modest operational change and no facility closure, yet could save 8-12% of the transportation cost increase.

## Hidden ongoing operating cost structure

* **Consolidated grade levels create hidden staffing backfill costs.** The model assumes consolidation saves on administration but doesn't account for teaching position creep. A rural district with 150 students per grade level across three schools might run one teacher per grade per school (3 teachers per grade). One consolidated school still needs 1.5-2 teachers per grade, plus specialized staff (counselor, special ed coordinator, assistant principal) that weren't counted in the original headcount. Have we modeled teaching positions, not just administrative positions, for each facility configuration? We should run a staffing model by configuration showing 5-year salary and benefits projections, assuming attrition recovery at full cost, not at savings.

* **Larger facilities require different operational staffing ratios.** A small rural school building (300 students, 1 principal, 1 custodian, 1 secretary) differs fundamentally from a consolidated building (1,200 students, 1 principal, 2-3 assistant principals, 3-4 custodians, 2-3 secretaries). The operational multiplier—staff per student—actually climbs with facility consolidation. Have we accounted for the rise in administrative overhead to manage 1,200 students vs. 300? We could address this by modeling "operational overhead per student" across configurations and building in a 10-15% contingency for unexpected staffing needs at consolidated sites.

* **Utilities and caretaking scale non-linearly at consolidated facilities.** Heating, cooling, and custodial care for a 60,000 sq ft building don't scale down proportionally when that building serves 1,200 students instead of 300. Summer maintenance, emergency repairs, and energy inefficiencies in older buildings often spike with intensive use. Have we obtained detailed utility and custodial cost histories from similar-sized consolidated districts? We should benchmark against 2-3 comparable consolidated districts (similar climate, building age, enrollment), establish year-one baseline projections with 15% variance bands, and build escalation clauses into facility budgets that reflect historical patterns, not idealized linear scaling.

* **Year-one honeymoon masks year-three drift.** The first year after consolidation, morale is high and staff work extra hours to prove the consolidation "works." By year three, normalcy returns; staff takes their earned leave, attrition picks up, and hidden costs surface. Have we stress-tested the consolidation budget for years 3-5 when staffing attrition and deferred maintenance compound? We should model 5-year financial projections with conservative assumptions on attrition (20% of administrative staff move on, requiring expensive replacement training) and a facilities reserve that grows from 2% to 5% of operating budget by year three to absorb emerging needs.

## Facility closure decision authority

* **Deferring closure decisions to the post-consolidation board is a timebomb.** The prompt correctly identifies this as "the politically most explosive single decision." But the feasibility study will recommend closures, and communities will campaign for years against the unified board's anticipated decisions. The moment of psychological trust is *now*, before consolidation; after consolidation, any closure is perceived as a betrayal by the merged-away districts. Have we considered that delaying closure decisions to the unified board essentially guarantees polarized politics and possible board recall elections? We could address this by conducting the closure decision process *now* using an independent facilities committee drawn from all three districts plus neutral experts. The committee publishes its recommendation, embeds it in the consolidation agreement as a binding schedule, and voters see what they're voting *for*, not what they fear will happen later.

* **Binding feasibility criteria can paralyze or trigger litigation.** If the consolidation agreement states "the unified board shall not close any building with >70% capacity utilization or <3 years to debt repayment," communities that don't meet those criteria will litigate. And any criteria will eventually be gamed (manipulate enrollment, defer maintenance to trigger closure clauses). Have we considered that written criteria create false certainty that will collapse when circumstances change? We could instead establish a "Facilities Committee Charter" that outlines process (annual review, community input structured via surveys/town halls with explicit weighting, 18-month notice before closure, post-closure community benefits fund) rather than binding numeric thresholds. Process legitimacy matters more than criteria rigidity in politically fraught decisions.

* **Community input weighting is easily captured.** A "structured weighting" process sounds fair, but if one town controls the district board or the facilities committee is dominated by one region's representatives, the input process becomes theater. Have we thought through how to ensure the three rural communities have actual decision weight, not token representation? We could mandate that any closure recommendation require two-thirds agreement from the committee, must include at least one representative from each original district, and must be accompanied by a community benefit plan (workforce retraining support, business development funds, alternative facility use) that runs for 5 years post-closure. The benefit plan signals that closure isn't abandonment—it's managed transition with investment.

## The school-as-anchor parallel

* **Anchor institution frameworks don't transfer cleanly to rural school closure.** Urban planners study hospital and university closures in dense neighborhoods where nearby institutions can absorb some functions and populations have alternative job/service options. Rural schools are anchors in a different sense: they're often the largest employer, the main gathering space, and a symbol of town viability. Urban closure research doesn't answer what happens when the anchor leaves and there are few alternatives. Have we studied actual rural school closures in comparable counties and measured economic/demographic impact 5-10 years out? We should commission a brief case-study research project on 2-3 completed rural consolidations (Ohio, Iowa, Maine) interviewing community leaders, tracking property values, and measuring migration patterns before assuming adaptive reuse or ownership models will work here.

* **Community ownership models require sustained organizational capacity.** Urban planners often suggest community nonprofits take over closed anchor buildings as social enterprises. But rural communities often lack the nonprofit infrastructure, board depth, and grant-writing capacity to run a building long-term. Have we assessed whether the three districts' communities have credible nonprofit leaders who could form a community ownership entity? If not, a well-intentioned community ownership clause in the consolidation agreement becomes a liability trap—a nonprofit forms optimistically, secures a 10-year lease, then collapses in year four, and the unified district inherits a stranded asset. We should require that any closure paired with community adaptive reuse must be preceded by a 12-18 month "community readiness assessment" led by a university extension office or nonprofit development consultant, determining whether local organizations exist to sustain the building. Only if that assessment is positive should closure proceed with community ownership pairing.

* **Alternative anchors require different economic development strategy.** The urban framework assumes new anchors emerge (a health clinic, a workforce hub, a food co-op). But these don't arise spontaneously in declining rural areas. Have we asked what economic anchors the county *needs*, and whether any of them could plausibly locate in repurposed school buildings? A county nursing shortage might support a nursing education program; a farm labor shortage might support an agricultural extension hub; a childcare crisis might support an early education center. We could reframe the facilities question: instead of "preserve the school building as-is via repurposing," ask "What county economic needs exist, what partners would address them, and which school buildings are suited to that purpose?" This ties facility disposition to economic development strategy rather than treating closure mitigation as a real-estate problem divorced from county growth. If no credible economic anchor opportunity emerges for a building, honesty suggests a planned long-term mothballing or sale, not hopeful adaptive reuse framing.
