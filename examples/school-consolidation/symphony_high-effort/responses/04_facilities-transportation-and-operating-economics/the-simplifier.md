# Facilities, Transportation, and Operating Economics — The Simplifier

## Facility Scoring Matrix and Irreversibility

* **The one-question approach.** What if instead of a weighted scoring matrix across six dimensions, you asked a single question: "Which buildings are already so old or poorly used that keeping them open costs more than closing them?" Identify the obvious closures first, then hold the rest. The complexity of a matrix exists to feel thorough; the signal already exists in the data.

* **Irreversibility solved before it happens.** Forget the "monitoring signal that indicates a closed building should have stayed open" — that's planning for regret. The question to ask now is: "If we close this building in year one, what community cost becomes irreversible?" If you don't know, leave the building open. Simplicity means: only close buildings where the closure is reversible or where the cost of staying open is inarguably larger.

* **Build utilization as the true rule.** Consolidation means some buildings are underutilized. Close the ones running at 40% capacity or lower, and keep the ones that work. That's it. The other five dimensions (condition, geography, community anchor) are valuable, but they should inform which full schools survive — not create a matrix of half-schools.

## Total Cost of Ownership and Bond-Acceleration Risk

* **Stop modeling for 10 years; start with year one.** The 10-year total cost of ownership calculation will be massively speculative (enrollment projections fail, utility costs shift, maintenance emergencies arrive). Instead: calculate the honest costs of the first three years — transition expenses, building maintenance at kept schools, utilities, closure/mothballing of the rest. If the model fails at year three, the 10-year forecast is fantasy.

* **The bond question is simpler than it sounds.** Ask the county finance director directly: "Which closure decisions trigger bond acceleration?" Write that down. Don't bury it in a financial model. If a closure voids a bond or triggers a lawsuit, you don't close that building. The decision rule is binary, not analytical.

* **Mothballing is often the hidden cost.** Closing a building is free. Mothballing it (maintaining a vacant structure for a decade) is expensive. What if you didn't mothball? Sell the buildings, demolish them, or let another entity take them over. The "preserve optionality" instinct is tempting but it costs money — make that trade-off explicit.

## Repurposing as Community-Anchor Preservation and Balance-Sheet Lever

* **Repurposing sounds good until you price it.** Community centers, early childhood hubs, and workforce-training centers are all real uses — and they all require operators, funding, and ongoing management that the school district shouldn't carry. If a building has a genuine second life, a nonprofit, municipal agency, or private operator will find it. If not, there's no hidden opportunity; repurposing just means you own a vacant building longer while looking for a tenant.

* **Don't solve the "school closure grief" with an alternative mission.** The communities losing their schools are grieving the loss of identity and local anchor, not because they secretly want a senior center. Repurposing doesn't solve that problem — it just defers the closure. If the goal is to keep the community building alive, say that. If the goal is to reduce district financial burden, sell or demolish the buildings.

* **Rental income from rural buildings is minimal.** The district is rural. Market rent for a vacant school building in a rural county is low or nonexistent. Endowments funded by one-time building sales of $200K or $300K buildings are modest and volatile. Don't count on this as a major fiscal lever.

## Transportation Cost Delta and Student-Experience Burden

* **45-minute bus rides are inevitable in rural consolidation; don't engineer around them.** Longer rides are a cost of geography, not a cost of bad planning. The real question: do you lengthen rides to keep two or three small schools open, or do you consolidate and accept longer rides? That's the actual trade-off. Model the route costs under the facility configurations, but don't treat transportation as a constraint to solve — it's a parameter that shifts.

* **The homework and fatigue question is real but not solvable by facilities decisions.** Students on long buses don't complete homework as well. That's true. But it's also true for rural students in a non-consolidated system. The question isn't "How do we avoid this?" — it's "Is consolidation still worth it despite this?" If the answer is yes, then you accept the trade-off. Don't add complexity (tutoring, technology, alternative schedules) to solve a problem that's baked into rural geography.

* **Where transportation truly matters: meal timing and extracurriculars.** A student on the bus for two hours has no lunch break flexibility and can't attend a 3 p.m. basketball practice. If consolidated schools shrink extracurricular options, that's a real cost. But if they expand them (larger schools field more teams), the longer bus ride might pay for itself. Model only those tradeoffs.

## Hidden Ongoing Operating Cost Structure

* **The "hidden costs" you list aren't hidden — they're negotiable.** Staffing-ratio shifts, utilities, and custodial loads are real but malleable. Instead of projecting them, use consolidation as a moment to reset staffing models. Consolidate and accept that per-building overhead increases at fewer, larger schools. But don't add layers of facilities-management overhead to manage that increase — use existing district staff to cover the larger footprint.

* **Year-one through year-five is the frame where you actually know anything.** Beyond year five, enrollment projections become guesses. Model the first three years honestly (know your transition costs, staffing gaps, and utilities), then stop. The administrative savings of $1.2M are real and knowable. The "second-order operating costs" are hunches. Don't let hunches offset a real $1.2M in annual savings.

* **Watch for the phantom cost: organizational complexity added to manage consolidation.** Districts often create new positions (consolidation managers, facilities coordinators, transition directors) to administer the merger. That burns savings. What if you didn't? Assign consolidation tasks to existing staff, set a deadline, and move forward. Simpler organizations cost less.

## Facility Closure Decision Authority

* **Don't create a new governance mechanism; use the one you have.** The unified board will make the facility decisions. Yes, it's politically explosive. But that's why they're there. Binding criteria (e.g., "buildings running below 50% capacity close unless the board votes unanimously to keep them") reduce discretion and speed decisions. You don't need a separate committee — you need a rule.

* **The "early years" problem is real; the solution is to frontload closure decisions.** Rather than letting the board decide buildings later, consolidate with the facility decisions already made. Make the hard calls in the consolidation proposal — three districts agree to close buildings X and Y and merge into Z. The referendum is "Do you support consolidation as proposed?" not "Do you want to consolidate, and we'll figure out closures later?" Transparency now, not surprises then.

* **Depoliticization doesn't come from more process; it comes from clarity.** The most explosive closures are the ones that feel arbitrary. A clear rule (highest-cost facilities close first, lowest-cost-per-student-served stay open) removes politics. A committee with "structured weighting" adds perceived politicking. State the rule publicly, apply it, and move forward.

## The School-as-Anchor Parallel

* **The anchor institution framework is useful but doesn't change the math.** Yes, schools anchor communities; yes, their closure affects neighborhood identity and economic vitality. That's real. But the question is still: what does consolidation cost, and what does it save? The "anchor" frame is powerful for understanding community loss — it's not a tool for deciding closures. Once you've decided to consolidate (for financial reasons), the anchor framework helps you communicate the tradeoffs, not avoid them.

* **Urban planners' adaptive reuse strategies are expensive and often fail in rural contexts.** Converting a closed school into a community center, maker space, or small-business incubator sounds transformative. In practice, rural adaptive reuse projects struggle with funding, governance, and demand. They're often showcased as "success stories" precisely because they're rare. Don't build the consolidation case on the assumption that every closed building becomes an adaptive reuse success.

* **The real mitigation for anchor loss is direct and local.** If closing a school destabilizes a town's identity, the mitigation isn't turning the building into something else — it's recognizing what you're losing and deciding whether the consolidation benefits (budget stability, better teaching) are worth it. If they are, invest the savings into that community in other ways (youth programming, local business support). If they aren't, don't consolidate. Don't use the anchor framework as cover for consolidation while simultaneously pretending to preserve anchors through repurposing.
