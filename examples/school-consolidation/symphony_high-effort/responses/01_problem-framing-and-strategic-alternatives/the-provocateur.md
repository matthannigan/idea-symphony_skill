# Problem Framing and Strategic Alternatives - The Provocateur

## Root-Cause Framing: What Problem Are We Actually Solving?

* **Reverse the causation entirely.** What if declining enrollment isn't *caused by* inadequate funding and poor staffing, but is the *rational market response* to those conditions? The real disease is that three small districts spent decades trying to mimic comprehensive K-12 models designed for 5,000 students, forcing unsustainable overhead onto populations that never needed it. The insight: before consolidating to preserve a structure no one asked for, ask *what these 1,800 families actually want to buy*—maybe it's not a full-service district at all, but micro-schools, hybrid learning, trading state mandates for parent choice. Consolidation might be the system's last-ditch effort to protect a product line the customer already abandoned.

* **Eliminate the teacher shortage by eliminating teaching.** What if the teacher-staffing crisis is unsolvable because we're trying to staff positions that don't need humans? Replace 30% of classroom instruction with adaptive AI tutoring, asynchronous video, and project-based learning with minimal supervision. The consolidated district becomes a logistics hub for learning materials and assessment, not a labor-intensive classroom machine. The real insight: rural education's cost structure assumes classroom scarcity (you need a warm body), but rural *connectivity* is now abundant. Stop trying to attract teachers to dying towns; build a model that doesn't require them at scale.

* **Consolidation as treating the symptom, not the disease.** The real problem isn't district inefficiency—it's that the state education funding formula systematically punishes rural districts by design. Consolidation buys maybe 12% more per-pupil allocation, but doesn't touch the formula that *created* the crisis. The insight: before merging, what if the three districts jointly lobbied the state to change the funding model to rural-density adjustment (+20% for dispersed populations), property-tax equalization, and per-student special-education weighting? That's structural problem-solving; consolidation is rearranging deck chairs on a sinking ship designed by Sacramento.

* **Treat educational outcomes, not costs.** Flip the frame: none of these three districts are consolidating because *test scores are bad*—enrollment decline and budget pressure are proxies for community confidence. What if the root cause is that parents have lost belief in what these schools deliver? Consolidation that just makes the same curriculum cheaper doesn't fix that. The real insight: what if we should ask "what would a genuinely different educational model look like that would *attract* families back?" (project-based learning, vocational tracks, dual-enrollment with community colleges) instead of asking "how do we keep the current one solvent?"

## Hybrid Governance and Shared-Services Alternatives

* **Start with the $1.2M savings and work backward to structure.** The stated benefit is eliminating three superintendents and administrative duplication. But what if you could capture $800K of that (67%) through a shared-services cooperative—shared superintendent reporting to all three boards, consolidated HR/payroll/purchasing, but *each district keeps independent curricula, culture, and governance*? Then the real question becomes: would the community coalition that defeats consolidation at the ballot box *accept* this? The insight: the political resistance to consolidation isn't really about money; it's about identity and control. A hybrid model might deliver 2/3 of the financial benefit while avoiding the 2/3 voter turnout requirement that kills the full merger.

* **What if the shared service agency *competes against* the districts?** Imagine a regional educational cooperative that offers full operational services—transportation, food service, special education, substitute teaching, professional development—priced at market rates to the three districts. But districts aren't forced to buy from it; they can outsource to private vendors if the coop's pricing drifts. The insight: the current discussion assumes consolidation is the only way to achieve scale economies. But competitive regionalization might achieve the same efficiency without the political death spiral of "who loses their school building."

* **The federated district that still feels local.** Create one legal entity with one superintendent, one budget, one bus fleet, but decentralized *curriculum decisions and hiring*, so each of the three towns still has a "their school" that feels autonomous. This requires giving up some operational efficiency (you can't fully rationalize transportation if each building has hiring flexibility), but you keep the coherence for voter approval. The insight: what specific operational decisions *must* be centralized to capture savings (transportation routing, facility maintenance, central purchasing), and which decisions can stay distributed to preserve buy-in? That's where the engineering is.

## The No-Consolidation Baseline: What's the Cost of Inaction?

* **Model the domino sequence with dates.** Smallest district runs dry in 24 months (known fact), then what? Does the county take emergency receivership, forcing consolidation anyway on worse terms? Do parents pull kids into private/charter options, accelerating the other districts' decline? Does state superintendent intervene? Map this domino sequence month-by-month with decision points, so the board sees *which consolidation scenario beats which failure scenario*. The insight: "do nothing" isn't actually a static option—it's a cascade with irreversible points-of-no-return. Consolidation's real competitor isn't status quo; it's managed failure under receivership.

* **Calculate the transition costs of delayed consolidation.** If consolidation happens now at district negotiations, infrastructure synergy is high. But if the smallest district goes insolvent first, then the surviving two districts negotiate merger from a position of desperation—they'll accept worse terms, lose leverage on labor contracts, and may have to absorb the insolvent district's debt at 100 cents on the dollar instead of restructuring it. The insight: consolidation has a *time value*—waiting costs money, not just debt service but negotiating power and optionality.

* **What happens to the buildings if we do nothing?** Seven school buildings exist. In inaction scenario, the financially strongest district eventually swallows the other two, but maybe only four of the seven buildings stay open. Now you have three empty rural school buildings. Someone must maintain them (property taxes + liability), or they become liability havens. Quantify this shadow cost: $200K/year in perpetuity for building maintenance and liability insurance on shuttered assets. The insight: "keeping current structure" actually means paying to keep buildings we can't afford to use. Full consolidation lets you decisively close redundant buildings and redeploy them (community centers, municipal offices, sold for tax base diversification).

## Solving the Wrong Crisis: What If Decline Is Success, Not Failure?

* **The county is successfully *completing its demographic transition*—and we're panicking about it.** Rural America's structural shift from agricultural/extraction to knowledge work means fewer young families stay put, more remote work means population is *choosing* to concentrate in cities. What if a 15% enrollment decline by 2035 is the *correct equilibrium*—the market is clearing correctly, not breaking down? The insight: what if the right solution isn't consolidation but *smaller, deeper schools*—maybe 300-student schools instead of 600, with intense community connection, arts/maker spaces, agricultural education, family involvement? You'd have *more* schools per capita but *fewer* kids total, reversing the bloat-and-decline cycle. Consolidation is the logical response if you assume you *must* operate to 1800-student scale. But what if that assumption is wrong?

* **Plan for a different county, not a stable one.** What if instead of projecting "1,800 students by 2035," you ask: *what if this county attracts rural-seeking remote workers and the population *stabilizes*—or even grows—but in an entirely different demographic shape*? More 35-55 year-olds, fewer families with children, more multi-generational homes. The education system that works for that county isn't smaller versions of the current system—it's evening/adult education, grandparent-as-caregiver support, skills retraining for job transitions. The insight: consolidation planning assumes the only variable is student count; but the *composition* of the county is transforming. What if we should build for what's coming, not the ghost of what was?

* **Stop trying to save the districts; save the educational *outcomes* instead.** What if students would be better served by a 60% reduction in facility footprint, closing rural school buildings entirely, and funding $10K/student stipends for families to choose learning pathways—hybrid learning hubs in the county seat, distance learning through state virtual school, private tutoring, homeschool co-ops? Yes, you lose the school-building-as-community-anchor. But outcomes and equity might improve. The insight: we're optimizing for institutional survival (the districts), not learning effectiveness. Those aren't the same. Decline is a crisis only if you assume the institution itself is the goal.

## The Merger as the Disease: What If Consolidation Compounds Failure?

* **What if three small districts are failing because they've converged to the same *wrong model*?** All three run traditional classroom-based instruction, college-prep curricula, hire teachers trained in the same credential programs, use similar accountability frameworks. They're not failing because they're small—they're failing because they're all identical small copies of a system designed for different conditions. Merging them creates a *bigger version of the same identical failure mode*. The insight: what if one district pivoted to project-based learning, one to dual-enrollment tech focus, one to agricultural/vocational pathways? They'd compete for enrollment, drive differentiation, and actually serve the county's actual economic needs. Consolidation kills that possible experiment by forcing homogenization.

* **Consolidation eliminates the *only competitive pressure* the system has.** Parents with choice-leverage (ability to homeschool, private school, move districts) are already gone. Consolidation removes the last check on decline: now there's one district with even more monopoly power and zero incentive to respond to changing community needs. The insight: what if the *only reason* to keep three districts is that they provide just enough competitive friction to stay responsive? Merge them and you guarantee institutional sclerosis at larger scale.

* **The failure isn't the districts—it's the talent pipeline.** All three districts can't attract qualified teachers because no one wants to live in these towns, rural credential programs aren't producing enough teachers, and compensation is uncompetitive. Consolidation might save $1.2M in overhead, but that money doesn't fix *why there are 14 vacancies*. You still can't attract talent. The consolidated district will have the same 14 vacancies, just at larger scale. The insight: what if you should stop trying to solve a *staffing problem* with *organizational restructuring*? The real solution is remote teaching (hire certified teachers anywhere, they teach from their urban centers), blended learning (reduce classroom hours per student), or radical compensation increases. Consolidation doesn't solve the actual constraint.

## Run It Like Municipal Bankruptcy: Restructuring Instead of Merger

* **Strip away the democratic pretense and acknowledge insolvency.** The smallest district has 24 months of solvency. That's not "we're considering options"—that's *insolvency in slow motion*. What if the county reframed this using Chapter 9 bankruptcy language: a structured process with defined timelines, creditor negotiations (unions, bond holders, state), and a bankruptcy judge *imposing outcomes* instead of asking voters? The emotional cover of "community choice" is obscuring the reality that choice is already foreclosed. The insight: honest insolvency framing might actually *unlock permission* that merger framing never does—people accept restructuring under bankruptcy but resist "consolidation" as community-erasure. Same outcome, different narrative frame.

* **Separate the insolvent district's assets and liabilities from the solvent ones.** Instead of merging three districts into one, what if insolvent district #1 enters supervised restructuring (state receivership, 3-year recovery plan, board takeover), while districts 2 and 3 *optionally* consolidate if they choose? This keeps the political poison off the solvent districts' vote and gives district 1 a clear rescue plan with state resources. The insight: "three into one" feels like *everyone loses*; but "one gets help, two choose partnership" redistributes the pain and might get voter approval.

* **Make the consolidation unequal and explicitly hierarchical.** What if instead of "three equal districts merge into one," the structure is "smallest district enters receivership and becomes a campus of the larger district," creating clear winners and losers? Yes, it's brutal for the smallest town. But it's *honest*. And the two remaining districts get to vote on whether they want to partner, without being yoked to the sinking third. The insight: forcing equal treatment in unequal situations creates political failure; explicit hierarchy is more defensible.

## The Hospital Merger Parallel: Learning from Consolidation Failures

* **Rural hospital consolidations that *preserved* local emergency capacity while centralizing specialty care kept community trust.** The pattern: one main hospital becomes the regional hub for cardiology/trauma/ICU; smaller satellite hospitals become urgent-care/observation facilities staffed by physician assistants and nurses. This maintains *immediate access* while achieving economies of scale on expensive specialist care. Translate this: consolidated district keeps school buildings in each town open, but with *differentiated missions*—one becomes the hub for AP/honors/college-prep, others focus on career technical, special education, early childhood. Same consolidation; different perception because kids still "go to school locally." The insight: the closure anxiety driving voter opposition might dissolve if *consolidation didn't mean building closures*.

* **Hospital mergers that closed rural facilities triggered population collapse—families moved to be near medical centers.** The same risk applies to school consolidation: close the neighborhood school, families move or choose private school, the county loses population and tax base, making the situation *worse* than before consolidation. The insight: any consolidation plan must front-load how it keeps educational access *geographically distributed*, or it risks accelerating the very demographic collapse it's trying to arrest. A consolidated district with one high school and three closed buildings may be financially optimal but fiscally suicidal.

* **The hospital parallel suggests "forced consolidation" loses, but "collaborative regional networks" can win.** Rural health systems that *succeeded* framed consolidation as voluntary partnership (all boards kept autonomy, decisions were consensus-based) rather than top-down merger. It took longer but survived political backlash. Translate: what if the consolidation process itself was multi-year, consensus-driven governance integration before any operational consolidation? Districts learn to trust each other, then merge from a position of relationship rather than desperation. The insight: the *process of consolidation* determines success more than the *financial structure* does.
