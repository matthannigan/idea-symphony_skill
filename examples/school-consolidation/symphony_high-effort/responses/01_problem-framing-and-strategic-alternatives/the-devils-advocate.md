# Problem Framing and Strategic Alternatives - The Devil's Advocate

## Root-cause framing

* **The real problem might not be consolidation-solvable.** All three districts are caught in a structural funding trap created by the state per-pupil formula, not by their own governance. The 12% per-pupil boost from consolidation is real, but it assumes state allocations don't shift—and if the state notices consolidation as a "success," it may reduce baseline funding to other small districts to maintain budget. Have we considered that consolidation merely papering over a systemic under-funding problem that should be solved at the state advocacy level? We could mitigate this by building a strong coalition with other rural districts to lobby for per-pupil floor minimums before consolidation, so gains aren't reversed later.

* **Teacher shortage might persist post-consolidation because it's not about district size.** The 14 vacancies exist because rural teaching is hard—isolation, lower salaries than metro areas, limited professional community, spouse employment constraints. A bigger district doesn't solve any of these. The $1.2M in saved admin overhead is real, but have we stress-tested whether those savings actually flow to classroom teacher salaries or disappear into larger system overhead? We could address this by committing 60-70% of admin savings to rural teacher incentives (housing assistance, signing bonuses, spouse job placement support) and measuring vacancy rates 18 months post-consolidation.

* **Declining enrollment is the constraint nobody can control.** Even if consolidation is perfectly executed, the county is losing 1,800 students and heading toward 1,530 by 2035. The per-pupil funding gain doesn't change the math—total revenue still falls 15% unless per-pupil funding rises dramatically. Have we modeled what happens to consolidation economics if the state reduces per-pupil rates as rural enrollment declines statewide? We should run sensitivity analysis: what if per-pupil rates drop 5% over 5 years? Does consolidation still pencil out, or do we end up with a single consolidated district in worse fiscal shape than three separate ones today?

## Hybrid governance and shared-services alternatives

* **A regional service agency doesn't require a merger, but the board may have already discounted it without data.** Shared superintendent ($200k instead of $300k), centralized HR and transportation, joint purchasing—these could yield $600–800k in savings with zero political capital spent on facility closures or identity loss. Have we modeled what 80% of consolidation's financial benefit would cost as a phased regional shared-services contract? We could test this by hiring a consultant to price out a 3-year regional services pilot (HR, transportation, purchasing) with an opt-out clause at year 3, so the board has real data on whether shared services achieve most benefits without irreversible decisions.

* **Federated districts with independent curricula might be better for 1,800 students than one unified system.** Each district keeps its K-12 continuity and local identity, but they share procurement, transportation routing, HR compliance, and central warehouse functions. The capital-cost barrier is low (it's a shared IT backbone, not a building closure). Have we explored whether federated structures preserve the community cohesion that makes schools magnets for young families? We could prototype this by proposing a "federation lite" where districts stay separate K-12 units but merge transportation and purchasing for 18 months, measuring school enrollment stabilization and parent satisfaction.

* **"Seriously evaluated" may not mean quantified.** If regional service agencies and federated districts were dismissed in prior studies with phrase like "too complex" or "insufficient savings," that's not rigorous. Have the board commission side-by-side financial modeling: full consolidation vs. regional services vs. federation, all three with the same assumptions, costed out to fiscal year +3 and +10? We could insist on this modeling as a precondition to moving forward, with each model including transition costs, staffing reductions, and risk factors. Without apples-to-apples comparison, any recommendation will feel predetermined.

## The no-consolidation baseline

* **The board is likely conflating "worst case" with "realistic case."** If the smallest district hits insolvency in 24 months, that's a trigger for state intervention, not automatic closure. State receivership typically means austerity (facility mothballing, hiring freeze, emergency tax increase), not liquidation. Have we mapped the actual statutory sequence—what does county education law say about receivership powers, authority for emergency levies, asset seizure timelines, and employee protection? We should commission a specific legal analysis: what are the real 24-month and 60-month consequences of doing nothing, with concrete timelines and state regulatory steps?

* **The "cost of inaction" assumes current decline continues linearly, which it might not.** Declining enrollment often plateaus—rural counties stabilize at a lower equilibrium once they stop losing young families to distant jobs. Have we examined whether this county has stabilization indicators—local job creation, return migration, young families moving in—that might bend the enrollment curve before the smallest district hits the wall? We could model three scenarios: (1) continued decline, (2) stabilization at 85% of today's enrollment, (3) modest growth from new employment. Only scenario 1 requires emergency consolidation; scenarios 2 and 3 suggest alternatives.

* **Fiscal contagion is a real risk, but it's preventable without consolidation.** If the smallest district fails, creditors pursue the county; the county likely absorbs debt rather than watch the default spread. Have we quantified the true fiscal contagion—what debts would the county be forced to assume, what legal claims would arise—versus the cost of a targeted bailout ($5–10M emergency state loan to the smallest district to buy time for alternatives)? We could model a "smallest-district intervention" strategy: a 5-year state loan with conditions (austerity, shared services, enrollment recovery targets), freeing the other two districts to explore federation while the smallest district stabilizes.

## Solving the wrong crisis

* **Consolidation assumes stability is recoverable; what if the county is in structural transition, and the real opportunity is designing education for decline.** If the county has lost 22% of students, that's not a temporary recession—it's permanent loss of young-family housing or local employment. Trying to operate seven buildings and three separate systems for 1,800 students is indeed wasteful, but so is a consolidated district designed for 1,800 if the real steady state is 1,200 students by 2040. Have we asked: what would a genuinely right-sized education system look like for a county heading toward 1,200 kids? A regional hub-and-spoke model with one modern central building and satellite learning centers? A K-8/9-12 split? A charter-district partnership? We could commission an "education-for-decline" design study: start from 1,200-student target, design backward to today's 1,800, and see what institution emerges. It might not be full consolidation.

* **The psychology of defending declining institutions is that every efficiency intervention feels like defending the indefensible.** Consolidation sounds rational, but it's still trying to preserve a 3-district educational model for a county that can no longer sustain it. What if the right frame is not "consolidate" but "transform"—embrace smaller schools, rural-specific curriculum (agricultural science, land management, water policy), hybrid in-person/remote teaching, and partnerships with regional universities for vocational programs? Have we considered that resistance to consolidation isn't necessarily irrational—it might be resistance to the wrong intervention? We could reframe the public conversation around transformation: smaller, more specialized, deeply rooted in county assets, rather than "bigger is better."

## The merger as the disease

* **All three districts likely share the same pathologies: aging infrastructure, limited advanced programming, small staff capacity for innovation, weak recruitment.** A consolidated district will inherit all three problems at scale. If District A is struggling to offer Advanced Placement, District B has deferred maintenance, and District C has weak STEM recruiting, consolidating into District D just means one larger system with those exact same inherited weaknesses. Have we identified what's genuinely broken about each district's governance, curriculum, or community relationship? We could mitigate this by requiring a pre-merger diagnostic: independent audit of each district's instructional practices, governance effectiveness, and community trust. Only if the consolidation plan shows how the new structure will *fix* specific pathologies (not just save money) should it move forward.

* **Economies of scale in a single district might lock in outdated models faster than three separate districts can evolve.** Three smaller systems can pilot different things—one focuses on vocational pathways, one on dual enrollment with community colleges, one on project-based learning. They're natural experiments. A single consolidated system flattens that diversity; the superintendent's choice about curriculum becomes the only choice for 1,800 kids. Have we considered that three "failing" districts might actually be more innovative than one "efficient" one? We could preserve the experimental advantage by structuring consolidation as a federation with shared business services but independent K-8 units or curriculum tracks.

* **The real question is: what would a genuinely *different* institution look like?** If three consolidated schools aren't it, what is? Remote-first schooling? Year-round calendar? Competency-based graduation? Portfolio assessment instead of grades? Aggressive partnership with county libraries, museums, farmer extensions? Have we stepped back from "consolidation yes/no" to ask what institutional form would actually serve rural students better than what exists? We could commission a radical redesign workshop: start with zero buildings, 1,800 students, unlimited pedagogical freedom, and no budget constraints. The output might be a hybrid model that neither the current three nor a traditional consolidated district would create.

## Run it like a municipal bankruptcy

* **The "emotional framing" of consolidation is actually obscuring the real fiscal insolvency in plain sight.** The smallest district has 24 months of fund balance remaining. That's not a "challenge to solve cooperatively"—that's the threshold for state receivership. Reframing this as a bankruptcy proceeding (transparent accounting of liabilities, supervised restructuring, binding timelines) might actually unlock political permission that "merger" framing cannot. Have we considered that parents and teachers might prefer transparent insolvency proceedings to the slow-motion ambiguity of a voluntary "merge-or-watch-us-fail" ultimatum? We could shift the frame: instead of "Consider consolidation," propose "Trigger Chapter 9-style restructuring with binding mediation, defined timelines, and protected stakeholder interests." Bankruptcy has legal teeth and clear procedures; consolidation is consensus-seeking theater.

* **Stripping away voluntarism and community-feelings language might actually build more trust than preserving it.** Voters will know consolidation is forced, not chosen. Being honest about that ("Two districts are fiscally insolvent; this restructuring is mandatory under state law") is more credible than "Let's come together as a community" when two-thirds of the community doesn't want it. Have we modeled what voter approval rates look like if the message shifts from "community choice" to "structured insolvency resolution with court-like process"? We could test message framing in focus groups: does "This consolidation is mandatory bankruptcy restructuring" perform better or worse with voters than "This consolidation is a community decision"?

* **A bankruptcy frame comes with procedural protections that voluntary consolidation lacks.** Creditor treatment is defined. Pension obligations are negotiated in supervised mediation. Asset sales follow clear rules. Voluntary consolidation leaves all these questions ambiguous. Have we considered that teachers' unions might actually prefer bankruptcy restructuring—it guarantees negotiation of contract terms with a mediator—to negotiating in the shadow of voluntary merger where one district might simply capitulate? We could propose a pilot: file for Chapter 9 status (or equivalent state-level insolvency procedure) for the smallest district, enter supervised restructuring with the other two, and work through consolidation via formal mediation. It's more procedurally robust than good-faith negotiation.

## The hospital merger parallel

* **Rural hospital consolidation has a mixed track record: some models preserved community access while centralizing specialty care; others gutted local capacity and triggered population flight.** The successful models kept emergency departments open in smaller towns while moving surgery/oncology to a regional hub. The failed models centralized everything, forcing 1-2 hour ambulance rides for any acute care. Have we studied which sequencing and facility design preserved community trust in hospital consolidation? We should commission a comparative analysis of 5-10 rural hospital mergers from the past 15 years: which ones maintained community support (measured by physician retention, population stability, enrollment in insurer networks)? Which ones triggered exodus? What was different in their communication and facility-closure decisions? We could then apply those lessons directly to school sequencing—e.g., if successful hospital mergers kept *some* local capacity, the equivalent for schools might be retaining K-8 buildings in smaller towns while centralizing secondary education.

* **Hospital consolidation success correlated with honest transparency about closures and strategic rebuilding.** The health systems that preserved trust said: "We're closing the maternity ward here because the regional hospital has better outcomes, but we're *expanding* emergency capacity and keeping a 24-bed acute-care unit." They didn't say "We're consolidating everything and hoping it works out." Have we studied the communication playbooks from successful rural hospital mergers—the press releases, community meetings, timeline announcements—to understand what transparency actually looks like? We could hire a health-system communication consultant to help the school district draft an analogous transparency strategy: "We're consolidating administration and secondary education in [Town A], but we're investing $2M in renovating elementary buildings in [Towns B and C] as community anchor institutions."

* **Hospital mergers faced massive population flight when they underestimated the symbolic value of "our local hospital."** Rural identity is tied to institutional presence. But successful health systems rebuilt that symbolism through things like residency programs, community clinics, and regional laboratory hubs in smaller towns. Have we considered what the school equivalent might be—not just keeping buildings open, but making them centers of adult education, community gathering, or career training? We could mitigate identity loss by proposing that consolidated district elementary schools become community anchor institutions: adult literacy programs, community college satellite campuses for vocational training, public library expansions, youth development center. If "our school" becomes a genuinely multipurpose community institution, the symbolic loss of K-12 in-building might be offset.
