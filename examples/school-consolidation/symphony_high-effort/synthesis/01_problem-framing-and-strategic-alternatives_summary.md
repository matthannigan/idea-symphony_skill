# Summary: Problem Framing and Strategic Alternatives

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension across this cluster is not "consolidate or not" but "how much to consolidate, and toward what future." All seven perspectives agree the $1.2M in administrative savings is real, yet all seven also agree it addresses a symptom rather than the disease. The load-bearing constraints—a state per-pupil formula that penalizes small rural scale and a teacher-staffing shortage rooted in isolation and pay—are largely untouched by merging governance. Consolidation pulls the financial lever cleanly but does little for staffing or educational outcomes on its own.

That gap reframes the decision. A shared-superintendent and shared-services model, the strongest convergence in the cluster, captures an estimated 60-85% of consolidation's savings (roughly $700K-$1.0M) while preserving local boards and identity and can begin immediately without a ballot. The key question is whether closing buildings is worth the marginal $200-300K it adds beyond what shared services already deliver. The recommended sequence: pilot shared services first, treat full consolidation as a finishing move, and close buildings last, by mutual agreement, after the model has proven itself.

Two reframes raise the stakes. First, the no-consolidation baseline accelerates: the smallest district exhausts its fund balance around month 24, triggering state receivership, after which the state consolidates on its own terms and chooses which buildings close. Controlled consolidation by choice costs much less than forced consolidation under receivership—the latter runs an estimated $3-4M more over five years and eliminates local agency. Second, the math assumes a population that is disappearing.

A productive dissent runs underneath the cascade: the 24-month runway is a worst case, receivership means austerity rather than liquidation, decline often plateaus, and a targeted state loan could stabilize the smallest district more cheaply than a merger. That argues for a legal and fiscal diagnostic before any merger commitment, not after.

## Key Themes

**A genuine categorical reframe surfaced and survives scrutiny: this is a right-sizing problem, not a consolidation problem.** Consolidating to serve 1,800 students optimizes for a population projected to fall to 1,200-1,400 by 2035-2040, leaving the district still oversized. All seven perspectives independently reached this conclusion, which changes the priority: design backward from the future steady state rather than preserving current scale efficiently. Consolidation built for 1,800 merely delays the hard facility decisions by five to seven years, then forces them under worse conditions.

### Separate the financial problem from the educational problem
Consolidation reliably improves financial sustainability but not educational sustainability, which depends on teacher recruitment and pedagogy that scale alone cannot deliver. Several perspectives insist on testing whether administrative savings reach classrooms (via committed teacher incentives and measured vacancy rates) rather than becoming absorbed in larger-system overhead.

### The hybrid spectrum captures most of the savings at a fraction of the political cost
Shared superintendent, central HR, joint purchasing, and regionalized special education emerge as the practical middle path, estimated at 60-85% of full-consolidation savings. Pure federation or joint purchasing alone reaches only ~$300-400K, enough to buy a 3-5 year bridge but not to prevent the smallest district's insolvency. This sharpens where the hybrid threshold actually binds.

### Differentiation as the antidote to "merging identical failures"
If the three districts fail because they are identical (same governance, curriculum, talent constraints), merging them scales the pathology. The alternative is deliberate differentiation: distinct missions per school (classical, project-based, STEM, CTE) atop shared services. A pre-merger diagnostic would require the plan to fix specific failure modes, not just save money.

### Reframe the politics: insolvency, not merger
A Chapter 9-style framing (neutral trustee, binding timeline, protected stakeholders) may unlock political permission that "merger" framing forecloses. Voters accept structured necessity but resist community erasure. Binding deadlines end the negotiate-forever stalemate.

### Sequence and communication over structure
From the rural-hospital parallel: keep local elementary anchors, centralize secondary and specialized programs into a hub, consolidate administration first and buildings last, and frame the message as student gains rather than streamlining. Sequencing and communication matter more than financial structure.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Commission a single side-by-side financial model of full consolidation vs. regional services vs. federation on identical assumptions, costed to FY+3 and FY+10, so no option is dismissed without data.
- Commission a legal analysis of the actual statutory receivership sequence (powers, emergency-levy authority, asset and employee-protection timelines) to ground 24- and 60-month consequences in reality rather than worst-case assumptions.
- Map the insolvency cascade month-by-month with explicit dates and a hard internal decision deadline (~month 15), including human-cost indicators (parent exodus per quarter, staff departures, population trajectory).

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Stand up a shared superintendent and central HR/recruitment function (roughly $150K to implement) to prove governance consolidation without legal merger, targeting $900K-$1.1M in savings and a measured vacancy rate within 12 months.
- Commission a facilities study modeling 1,500 / 1,200 / 1,000-student scenarios to identify which two to three buildings close and on what timeline. This shifts the question from "consolidate?" to "right-size how?"
- Run a community "design for decline" engagement that asks what size and shape of system the county can sustain indefinitely, building buy-in before any consolidation pressure.
- Test the insolvency-versus-merger framing in focus groups, and draft the protected-stakeholder list (teacher pay, transportation, special education, debt service) before going public.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Phase the transition on the proven hospital-merger sequence: administration and shared services first, transportation and facilities next, specialized services after, and selective building closures last, by mutual agreement.
- Adopt a hub-and-spoke design that keeps a K-5/K-6 anchor school in each town and consolidates grades 7-12 with advanced and specialized programs at one campus.
- Pursue differentiated school missions (and the pre-merger diagnostic that justifies them) so the new structure fixes failure modes rather than scaling them.
- Build a rural-district coalition to lobby the state on the per-pupil formula and a funding floor, so consolidation gains are not clawed back later.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- A shared-services hybrid can capture most of the savings without a ballot fight and decouple cost efficiency from cultural homogenization.
- Right-sizing by intentional design rather than crisis closure lets the county brand itself as "rightsizing to excellence." A distinctly small, specialized school can attract families from surrounding counties as a revenue strategy.
- Repurposing closed buildings as community anchors (adult education, community-college satellites, clinics, maker spaces) can offset the symbolic loss that drives opposition.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- Consolidation may leave the two real constraints untouched (funding formula and teacher attraction), producing a larger entity with the same 14 vacancies.
- Merging three structurally identical districts can scale shared failure modes rather than cancel them if pedagogy is not transformed alongside structure.
- Closing buildings can trigger population flight that erases the fiscal gains. A financially optimal single-campus district can be fiscally suicidal.
- If the state reads consolidation as a "success," it may cut baseline funding to other small districts, reversing the per-pupil gain.

**Trade-offs**:
- Capturing the final ~$200-300K in savings requires building closures, whose political and demographic cost may exceed the marginal benefit.
- Proactive consolidation preserves negotiating power and local control but requires spending political capital now. Waiting cedes terms to a state receiver later.
- Federation preserves identity and buys time but cannot reach the savings needed to prevent the smallest district's insolvency. It serves as a bridge, not a destination.
- Slower, consensus-built, phased integration lowers backlash and failure risk but costs more in transition time and redundant positions.
