# Brainstorming Synthesis: Problem Framing and Strategic Alternatives

---

## Synthesized Insights by Question

### Question 1: Root-Cause Framing

**Full question**: Is consolidation addressing the right underlying problem — insufficient revenue, inefficient cost structure, poor educational outcomes, the rural teacher-staffing shortage, or a symptom of structural underfunding of rural education — and what organizational structure would a from-zero institutional design recommend for delivering education to 1,800 students across this rural county, regardless of legacy district boundaries that were drawn under conditions that no longer hold?

* **Consolidation targets administrative cost but leaves the load-bearing constraints untouched: state funding formula and teacher attraction.** All seven perspectives converge that the $1.2M administrative savings is real but addresses a symptom rather than the disease. Two distinct root causes recur. A state per-pupil formula structurally penalizes small rural scale (a policy problem, not a governance problem). A teacher-staffing shortage driven by rural isolation, pay, and spouse-employment constraints is an attraction problem that a larger payroll does not by itself fix. The financial lever that consolidation pulls is separate from the educational levers. Test whether savings actually reach classrooms rather than being reabsorbed as larger-system overhead.

* **A from-zero design points toward minimal shared central services plus distinct, technology-enabled local schools, not a fourth uniform district.** Centralize the back office and specialist pools. Keep teaching and identity rooted in place. The recurring structure is a cooperative or accelerator backbone (common curriculum bank, shared credentialing, technology infrastructure, asynchronous specialized instruction) serving distinct community-facing schools. This replaces one homogenized K-12 entity.

### Question 2: Hybrid Governance and Shared-Services Alternatives

**Full question**: Beyond the binary of "three districts vs. one," what other structures (regional service agencies, federated districts, joint purchasing, shared superintendent, regionalized special education, educational cooperatives) have been seriously evaluated, and what specific savings threshold would those models fail to reach to justify full consolidation's political cost?

* **A shared-superintendent / shared-services model captures roughly 60-85% of consolidation's savings at a fraction of the political cost; this is the single strongest convergence in the cluster.** All seven perspectives support a hybrid that removes the duplicated top layer (three superintendents, three HR/transportation functions) while keeping local boards or curriculum. Estimates cluster at roughly $700K-$1.0M against the $1.2M full-consolidation target. The key question is whether closing buildings is worth the marginal ~$200-300K it adds beyond what shared services already capture. Start with shared services (shared superintendent, central HR, joint purchasing, regionalized special education) and measurable savings targets. Treat full consolidation as a finishing move, not the opening one. Pure federation or joint purchasing alone yields only ~$300-400K, enough to buy a 3-5 year bridge but not enough to prevent the smallest district's insolvency.

* **Shared-service models can compete or be made contestable so efficiency does not depend on monopoly.** Price shared functions (transportation, food, special education, substitutes) at market rates. Let districts outsource to private vendors if the cooperative drifts. This allows scale economies without the political fight over which town loses its building.

### Question 3: The No-Consolidation Baseline

**Full question**: What is the quantified cost of inaction — the sequence of fiscal failure (forced closure, state receivership, emergency state takeover), the smallest district's 24-month fund-balance runway, debt absorption, fiscal contagion to the other two districts — so the board compares consolidation against a realistic alternative rather than a frozen status quo?

* **"Do nothing" is not static; it is an accelerating cascade whose true cost is loss of local control, not just dollars.** All seven perspectives reject the frozen-status-quo framing and reach the same endpoint: the smallest district exhausts its fund balance around month 24, triggering state receivership. The state then consolidates on its own terms and chooses which buildings close. The real comparison is "controlled consolidation by choice vs. forced consolidation by receivership." Five-year cost of inaction reaches roughly $3-4M including receivership fees, teacher turnover, and emergency repair premiums, plus the loss of negotiating power. Model the sequence month-by-month with explicit dates and a hard internal decision deadline (around month 15-20) well before the receivership threshold. Include human costs in that baseline: parent exodus per quarter, staff departures, county population trajectory.

* **The 24-month runway is a worst case that may be mitigable short of full consolidation; insolvency is a receivership trigger, not automatic closure.** The analysis conflates worst-case and realistic-case scenarios. Receivership usually means austerity, not liquidation. Decline often stabilizes rather than continuing forever. And contagion is preventable without merging. Commission a legal analysis of statutory receivership powers and timelines. Model multiple enrollment scenarios—only sustained linear decline forces emergency consolidation. Consider a targeted $5-10M state loan to the smallest district paired with austerity and recovery conditions. That's a cheaper bridge while the other two districts explore federation.

* **Stranded buildings carry a perpetual cost the inaction baseline usually omits.** Under inaction, the strongest district eventually absorbs the others while several buildings sit empty, costing roughly $200K per year indefinitely to maintain and insure. Full consolidation lets the entity close those buildings decisively and redeploy the assets.

### Question 4: Solving the Wrong Crisis (Plan With Decline)

**Full question**: What if declining enrollment isn't a problem to fix but a signal that the county is successfully transitioning to a different kind of community — and consolidation is the system desperately trying to preserve an institution that should be shrinking? What would it look like to plan *with* decline rather than against it?

* **Right-size to the real steady state (~1,200) now, rather than optimizing for an 1,800 that is already disappearing; "plan with decline" reframes closure from crisis to design.** All seven perspectives converge that consolidating to serve 1,800 optimizes for a population that will not exist by 2035-2040, leaving a still-oversized footprint. Design backward from a ~1,200-student target with fewer buildings, leaner administration, and specialized programs that make small schools excellent and attractive. Consolidation preserves current scale efficiently but merely delays hard facility decisions by 5-7 years. Right-sizing builds for the future scale and forces these decisions now, under better conditions. Closing buildings by design rather than by failure also dissolves much of the identity backlash. A small, specialized school can become a revenue strategy by drawing families from surrounding counties.

* **The county may stabilize or grow in a different demographic shape, requiring a different system rather than a smaller one.** If rural-seeking remote workers arrive (more 35-55-year-olds, fewer child-rearing families, multi-generational homes), the system should shift toward evening classes, adult education, caregiver support, and skills training. Consolidation planning assumes student count is the only variable. In reality, the county's composition, not just its size, is transforming.

### Question 5: The Merger as the Disease

**Full question**: Imagine that the three failing districts are failing precisely *because* they are already too similar — same governance philosophy, same curriculum model, same relationship to their communities. What if consolidation into a fourth, larger version of the same thing accelerates failure rather than preventing it? What would a genuinely different institutional form look like?

* **The three districts share identical failure modes, so merging them risks a larger version of the same dysfunction; the antidote is deliberate differentiation, not homogenization.** All seven perspectives agree. Identical governance, curriculum, and talent constraints mean consolidation repeats the same problems at scale. A better move: let schools differentiate. One could offer classical/liberal-arts, another project-based, another STEM, another CTE/vocational. Keep shared back-office services. You get choice, schools can attract teachers with distinct missions, and the system becomes less fragile. If merger makes sense, first run a diagnostic to identify what's actually broken in each district. Then require the plan to fix those problems, not just move everyone into a bigger container. A capability matrix—comparing whether each district's strengths are unique or duplicated—should shape both the merger design and how you pitch it.

### Question 6: Run It Like a Municipal Bankruptcy

**Full question**: What if instead of framing this as an educational merger — a story of loss — the county reframed it using the template of Chapter 9 municipal bankruptcy: an honest acknowledgment that the current structure cannot service its obligations, a supervised restructuring with protected stakeholders, and a court-like process with defined timelines and binding outcomes? What would it mean to treat this as a structured insolvency?

* **Honest insolvency framing may unlock political permission that "merger" framing never will, by replacing voluntarism with binding, time-bound, stakeholder-protected restructuring.** All seven perspectives converge on the reframe: name that the structure cannot service its obligations, install a neutral trustee (the state commissioner), set an 18-36 month binding timeline, and protect defined stakeholders. Voters resist "merger" as community-erasure but accept structured restructuring as necessity. Binding deadlines end the negotiate-forever dynamic in which each district waits for the others to cave. Test this framing directly through focus groups and message testing. Design the protected-stakeholder list before going public (teacher pay, transportation, special education, debt service). Use binding arbitration to reconcile the three union contracts quickly.

* **Decouple the insolvent district from the solvent ones rather than yoking all three into one vote.** "Three into one" feels like everyone loses. "One gets help, two choose partnership" redistributes the pain and keeps the political poison off the solvent districts' ballot. Put the insolvent district into receivership while the other two optionally consolidate. Make the hierarchy explicit rather than forcing equal treatment on unequal situations.

* **Bankruptcy framing can dissolve the unanimous-vote trap that makes voluntary consolidation structurally fragile.** Consolidation legally requires a yes in all three districts. An insolvent district in receivership can be consolidated by a receiver even if it votes no. This paradoxically makes consolidation more palatable because the alternative is plainly worse.

### Question 7: The Hospital Merger Parallel

**Full question**: What structural lessons from rural hospital consolidation (including which merger designs succeeded in maintaining community trust versus which triggered population flight) should guide how this district consolidation is sequenced and communicated?

* **The hub-and-spoke design (keep local elementary anchors, centralize secondary/specialized programs) is the dominant lesson, and sequence plus communication determine success more than financial structure.** All seven perspectives converge on the same translation of rural hospital experience. Successful systems preserved local primary/emergency capacity while centralizing specialty care. Failed ones closed satellites first and triggered population flight. For schools, this means keeping an elementary school (K-5/K-6) in each town as a community anchor, consolidating grades 7-12 and advanced programs into a central hub. Sequence administrative consolidation first and building closures last, only by mutual agreement after the model is proven. Communicate gains for students (better programming, pooled facilities) rather than "streamlining" losses. Repurpose closed buildings as community anchors for adult education, community-college satellites, or clinics to offset symbolic loss.

* **Collaborative, consensus-built integration beats top-down merger even when slower.** Rural health systems that survived backlash framed consolidation as voluntary partnership with retained board autonomy and consensus decisions. Sequence multi-year governance integration and trust-building before operational consolidation. The process of consolidating determines success more than the financial structure does.
