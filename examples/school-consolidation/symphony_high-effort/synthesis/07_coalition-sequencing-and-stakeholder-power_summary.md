# Summary: Coalition Sequencing and Stakeholder Power

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension of this cluster is that consolidation requires a simultaneous yes from three communities, but every concession that secures one community's vote tends to undermine the fiscal case for merging or the promises made to another community. Guarantee one town its building, and the next town demands the same. Promise teachers full job protection, and the administrative savings that justify consolidation evaporate. Tailor a different pitch to each district, and the moment voters compare notes the whole effort looks dishonest. The personas did not resolve this trade-off so much as agree on how to manage it in the open, with published trade-offs, structural guardrails, and a deliberate endorsement sequence, rather than through hidden deals that unravel at the worst moment.

The strongest recommendation across responses is radical transparency about concessions. A published "concession register" or reconciliation memo that names who asked for what, what can be delivered, and where commitments conflict defuses the single most dangerous dynamic in a three-way vote: a community learning by rumor that its neighbor got a better deal. Pairing this with structural mechanisms (a neutral arbiter, binding facility-reuse commitments, tax-levy caps with reinvestment floors) converts fragile political promises into enforceable terms that both fiscal conservatives and educators can trust.

A second point of agreement concerns who actually decides whether voters ever see the ballot. Teachers' union presidents, pivotal school board members, the county superintendent, editorial boards, and county supervisors who control transition financing each hold an informal veto. The dominant move is to secure private, written buy-in from each before any public campaign, engaging them individually so they cannot form a collective veto.

The cluster's sharpest strategic insight is that the order of endorsements is itself a lever. If fiscal conservatives endorse first, every later audience reads the motive as "cuts, not improvement." Educators and parents must establish that consolidation improves schools before fiscal conservatives establish that the county can afford it. Reverse the sequence and each endorsement undercuts the next.

The critical risks are an all-or-nothing veto structure (a single early "no" collapses the whole effort), asymmetric leverage from the insolvency-facing district (which is double-edged because state receivership harms everyone), and an emotional irreversibility that no talking point overcomes. Voters weigh permanent, immediate loss against abstract future savings they will never feel in their own pocket.

## Key Themes

**Endorsement sequence is a strategic variable, not an afterthought.** A single-persona reframe corroborated by two others changes which actions get prioritized. Educators and parents must endorse before fiscal conservatives, because a fiscal-first sequence brands the entire effort as cuts rather than improvement and poisons every subsequent endorsement. This makes "who goes first" a deliberate campaign decision rather than a matter of who happens to be willing.

### Transparency as the critical tactic
Across nearly every question, the recommended move is to surface trade-offs and information in the open rather than manage them in the dark. A published concession register, itemized savings allocation, quarterly tracking reports, and honest naming of which communities face tax increases all serve the same function: defusing the betrayal dynamic that collapses fragile coalitions the moment one group suspects it is being managed. One genuine dissent surfaces whether tailored district-by-district messaging is a feature or a fatal liability, and even its proposer flags the backfire risk.

### Map and pre-commit the informal gatekeepers
Union presidents, pivotal board members, the county superintendent, editorial boards, and bond-approving county supervisors can each kill consolidation before voters see it. The strategy across responses is to engage each individually and privately, secure written commitments, and turn gatekeepers into co-owners by inviting them onto implementation task forces so their interest shifts from blocking to building.

### Convert the insolvency clock from threat into shared deadline
The smallest district's bankruptcy threat is real but double-edged, since receivership harms all three. The durable move is to reframe it as a forcing deadline that accelerates a shared timeline. Give that district front-loaded gains and leadership roles so it advocates rather than derails. Recast the public story from "bailout of a failure" to "shared regional adaptation" so larger-district voters accept favorable terms.

### Diagnose opposition by type before responding
"Our school is our town" resistance blends principled identity, territorial anxiety, and institutional inertia, each requiring a different response. The method across responses is to isolate the three types through targeted probes and behavioral signals, then match each to its remedy: cultural continuity for identity, facility and bus-route guarantees for territorial fear, pilots and quick wins for inertia.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Privately secure written buy-in to the feasibility-study scope from the county superintendent, then map and engage the pivotal board member and union president in each district individually, before any public announcement.
- Negotiate a memorandum of understanding with all three union locals (job security, contract-renewal rights, current wage scales, a transition-committee seat) and hold it as the first locked commitment.
- Draft and publish a concession register that names what each group has asked for, what can be delivered, where commitments conflict, and which are flexible versus non-negotiable.
- Commission opposition-type research using targeted probes and open-ended facilitation to separate identity, territorial, and inertia resistance rather than assuming the categories.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Brief each newspaper editorial board and a public majority of county supervisors with the full financial case and the prevent-state-takeover argument, securing explicit commitments before the campaign.
- Pre-negotiate the smallest district's insolvency timeline with the state as a formal constraint, set a hard public bankruptcy date, and structure terms that front-load its gains and offer its leadership genuine roles.
- Convert facility decisions into binding reuse commitments (innovation center, community hub, recreation/medical use) signed before the vote, and decouple remaining building decisions into a post-vote task force with binding arbitration.
- Run a small joint pilot (shared purchasing or professional development) to demonstrate competent cross-district collaboration and convert "risk of change" into proof of feasibility.
- Build the financial case around binding guardrails: a tax-levy cap requiring a referendum to exceed, an administrative-cost cap, and a reinvestment floor, with an itemized savings allocation published in advance.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Stand up a neutral arbiter (Joint Powers Authority or named Chief Integration Officer) and an appointed savings-allocation committee with both fiscal-conservative and educator representation to govern post-vote trade-offs.
- Deliver two to three concrete early wins within 90 days of approval (fill vacancies in the most understaffed district, restore a cut program) and publish a tracking report on savings allocation.
- Run a parallel identity-transition workstream (school histories, alumni networks, facilities rebranded as regional assets) so identity grief becomes stewardship rather than veto power.

### Endorsement sequence (cross-cutting)
- Sequence public endorsements deliberately: educators and parents first to establish "this improves schools," then fiscal conservatives to establish "we can afford it," with a low-self-interest outsider (a retired superintendent who has consolidated before) or trusted local figures as visible leads.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- The shared existential risk of insolvency and state receivership can unify all three districts around a single accelerating deadline rather than dividing them.
- A 12% per-pupil funding increase and $1.2M in administrative savings give a concrete, itemizable basis for showing each community what it gains.
- Gatekeepers can be converted from judges into co-owners by giving them implementation roles, turning potential vetoes into advocacy.
- Reframing closure as transformation (innovation center, community hub) offers losing communities a dignified gain instead of pure loss.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- The all-or-nothing veto structure means a single early "no" can collapse the entire effort and permanently change the calculus for the remaining districts.
- The cascade of concessions can destroy the fiscal logic for merging. Keep every building, honor every contract, and the efficiency case is gone.
- Tailored district-by-district messaging risks looking dishonest the moment communities compare notes, breeding quiet rage and defection.
- Emotional irreversibility (immediate permanent loss versus abstract future benefit) creates resistance no talking point overcomes. Close votes breed a decade of mistrust.
- The "efficiency versus opportunity" split may be genuinely incompatible visions. Splitting the difference after the vote can leave both fiscal conservatives and educators feeling betrayed.

**Trade-offs**:
- Transparency about concessions builds trust but surfaces conflict early. Strategic ambiguity buys short-term peace at the cost of a later betrayal narrative.
- Favorable terms for the insolvency-facing district make consolidation attractive to its voters but risk a "why bail them out" backlash in the larger districts unless reframed as regional adaptation.
- Locking savings into tax-relief caps reassures fiscal conservatives but constrains the reinvestment that parents and educators see as the entire point of merging.
- Front-loading union commitments secures the most credible early ally but risks the appearance of a secret deal if the timing is wrong. Both too-early and too-late endorsement carry distinct costs.
