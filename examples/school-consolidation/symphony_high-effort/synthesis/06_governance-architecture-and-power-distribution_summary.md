# Summary: Governance Architecture and Power Distribution

## Executive Summary

**The board must balance protection for smaller towns (to win their votes) against the decisiveness a district in fiscal crisis requires.** Every protection that reassures a small town (reserved seats, supermajority thresholds, town vetoes) also slows the body that has to close buildings and cut budgets. The personas rejected the false idea that this tension resolves cleanly, and converged instead on a structure that gains legitimacy at the margin while keeping a workable majority.

The structural consensus is unusually tight. All seven perspectives reject pure at-large elections and favor a small board (5-7 seats) that guarantees each town a named district seat, balanced by at-large seats so most decisions require a cross-town coalition. Six of seven add a supermajority or town-specific veto for the one class of decision that can detonate the whole project: facility closures. On the operational boundary, all seven draw the same line. Centralize budget, curriculum standards, hiring, transportation, and facilities (much of it legally compelled), and keep school culture, extracurriculars, and community relationships local.

Governance design alone cannot carry the political load. No board math survives the first school closure intact; whichever town loses its building will read the loss back into the board's composition. The personas therefore shift weight from structure to process: a public facilities review conducted before the consolidation vote with explicit criteria, so voters consent knowing the building-level consequences rather than discovering them afterward. Several pair this with an appointed or deferred-election first board that makes the hard early calls while identity wounds are fresh and politics are least tribal.

Two forward-looking threads round out the cluster. First, the structure should be built as a modular node in a future county service network, with delegation pathways and expansion slots written into an amendable charter now, so fire, library, or transportation consolidation in 5-10 years doesn't force a re-fight of 2026. Second, the "dissolve the districts" thought experiment reframes the district as a funding-and-governance container rather than an educational necessity. A sharp dissent warns it dodges the real problem: 1,800 students in buildings designed for 2,300 is an arithmetic problem no governance container solves. The strategic implication is to right-size the portfolio regardless of how the container is drawn.

## Key Themes

### Structure Sets the Floor; Process Carries the Weight
The board's composition is the entry ticket. Without guaranteed district seats and a closure supermajority, the smaller towns won't vote yes. But the personas are emphatic that representation design cannot by itself absorb the shock of facility closures. The durable legitimacy comes from a transparent facilities process with explicit criteria (ideally completed before the vote) and from rotating, visible signals of power-sharing like a chair that cycles through the towns.

### The Same Centralization Boundary, Drawn Seven Times
The clearest convergence in the cluster: centralize the expensive and compliance-bound functions (budget, curriculum standards, hiring, transportation, facilities) and keep the identity-bearing functions (culture, extracurriculars, community relationships) local. The unifying logic is the rural "death spiral" — fragmented districts lose teachers, outcomes fall, enrollment and budgets follow. Centralizing parity breaks the loop, but only if savings visibly return to schools as discretionary funds. Consolidation then reads as a local payoff rather than extraction.

### Build for the Next Consolidation, Not Just This One
Rural counties are consolidating fire, library, and municipal services on an accelerating curve. A board hardwired for education-only authority becomes an obstacle the moment the next merger arrives. The consensus is to define decision rights by function category, leave contingent expansion slots in an amendable (not voter-locked) charter, and establish a county-coordination body now to build collaboration before a crisis forces it.

### Legibility as Legitimacy
A recurring move is to make power visibly fair rather than merely fair. Named town seats, published rosters with photos and committee assignments, rotating chairs, published election data by origin-district, and a publicly negotiated seat-allocation deal locked in before the vote all matter. Boards are experienced as political symbols, not enrollment ratios, so the perception of equal partnership is itself load-bearing.

### The Demographic Floor Under Every Design (single-perspective dissent)
A pointed counter-argument cuts beneath the entire governance debate: the core problem is demographic collapse, not governance structure. Any container (unified district, university extension, hospital network, parent cooperative) inherits the same arithmetic of too many buildings for too few students. Governance design is necessary but insufficient. The portfolio (how many buildings, what partnerships) must be right-sized to actual enrollment no matter how authority is drawn.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Pull the state education statute to determine which board structures (at-large, district-based, hybrid) are permissible and what supermajority and amendment rules are within board authority — the design space is unknown until this is settled.
- Stand up a public Facilities Task Force with representation from all three towns to evaluate all seven buildings against explicit criteria (structural condition, enrollment, transportation efficiency, operating cost, community impact), with the aim of reporting before the consolidation vote.
- Negotiate and publish the seat-allocation agreement (how many seats per town, for how long, with an enrollment-based sunset) so it stops being a live grievance during the campaign.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Adopt a 5-7 seat board with one guaranteed named district seat per town plus at-large seats, and codify a 66%+ supermajority (or affected-town assent) for facility closures and major budget reallocations.
- Design term staggering plus a chair that rotates through the towns on a fixed cycle. Consider a lottery for which seats face the first election to signal procedural fairness.
- Write the charter in amendable, principles-based, function-category language with contingent service-sharing slots and sunset/review triggers, allowing board-level amendment once stable.
- Establish a quarterly county-coordination council (commissioners, library, public works, fire, school leadership) as a no-binding-authority forum to build cross-sector relationships.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Phase administrative centralization over ~5 years (payroll, finance, and purchasing first; curriculum later) with written-justification veto windows for principals, and route efficiency savings into visible per-school discretionary budgets.
- Right-size the facilities portfolio to actual and projected enrollment (potentially far fewer than seven buildings), paired with dual-enrollment or regional partnerships. No governance design makes 2,300-capacity infrastructure affordable for 1,800 and falling students.
- Pilot one unbundled-function or alternative-governance arrangement (e.g., university-extension curriculum, county-managed shared services, the smallest district transferring operations to a county education authority) for ~3 years, measure cost and satisfaction, then absorb, expand, or discard.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- Consolidation can break the teacher-loss death spiral by equalizing salary scales and hiring standards across the three towns, directly addressing the recruitment crisis.
- Administrative-duplication savings can fund visible per-school discretionary budgets, turning consolidation into a locally felt gain rather than a remote efficiency.
- Designing the board as a modular county-service node positions the district at the front of the rural-consolidation wave, retaining control over fire, library, and transportation sharing decisions later.
- A pre-vote facilities process lets the community consent to closures with eyes open, converting the most toxic decision into a legitimacy asset.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- Every small-town protection (reserved seats, supermajorities, vetoes) risks gridlock in a district that must act decisively under fiscal stress.
- The first facility closure will be read back into board composition no matter how the board is designed. Legitimacy is fragile precisely where it matters most.
- An appointed or deferred-election first board buys stability at the cost of democratic legitimacy and can be attacked as undemocratic.
- A wealthier town may view a single district seat as under-representing its tax contribution, demanding weighted voting that smaller towns will resist.
- Voter-locked bylaws or an education-only structure can ossify into a trap that forces a full re-fight when the next county consolidation arrives.

**Trade-offs**:
- Protection versus decisiveness: thresholds that reassure small towns also slow the body that has to close buildings and cut budgets.
- Centralization economics versus local engagement: centralizing saves money and equalizes quality, but taken too far it turns schools into sterile delivery nodes and drives educator disengagement.
- Electoral legitimacy versus transition stability: an elected first board is legitimate but vulnerable to capture and chaos. An appointed one is stable but contestable.
- Governance redesign versus demographic reality: the single-perspective dissent holds that no container (district or otherwise) escapes the arithmetic of too many buildings for too few students. Structural work must be paired with portfolio right-sizing or it solves the wrong problem.
