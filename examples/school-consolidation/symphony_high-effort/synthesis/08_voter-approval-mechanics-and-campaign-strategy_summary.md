# Summary: Voter Approval Mechanics and Campaign Strategy

## Executive Summary

The core trade-off is timing under a veto rule. The campaign must move fast enough to beat the smallest district's 24-month insolvency runway but slowly enough to build the cross-district trust that a unanimous, three-district approval requires. Six of seven perspectives converge on a roughly 18-month window (a vote with a ~6-month financial buffer) as the balance point. One adversarial voice disagrees, arguing that genuine coalition-building in a trust-deficit rural environment takes 12-18 months before a campaign can even start. An 18-month vote, by this logic, campaigns before the coalition exists and loses the hardest district. That dissent reframes the entire question: set the date by coalition readiness (month 24-30), not by the calendar, and accept the financial risk of waiting. The two camps agree on everything except whether the cliff or the coalition sets the clock.

Underneath the timing question sits a near-unanimous reframing of the whole effort. Advocates should stop selling "consolidation saves money" and start selling control: "you choose how we change, or change gets chosen for you." Insolvency produces identity loss regardless, on worse terms, through a state receiver who sells buildings and closes schools by formula. The job is to make that contrast concrete and personal in each town, using three tracks (quality for the wealthiest, survival-with-dignity for the smallest, leverage for the middle) that share one logic but never one script.

Two structural moves stand out as highest-leverage tactics. First, defeat the simultaneous-veto trap by staggering the votes (or simulating a sequential wave with reported straw polls), giving each district a specific, named, ballot-level win. Sign a binding consolidation agreement before any ballot so voters ratify a concrete deal rather than an open idea. Second, win the holdout district with structural protections (board seats, closure vetoes, transition guarantees) rather than money or kept-open redundant buildings. Resource concessions erode the very fiscal case the other two districts are buying.

Every perspective argues the feasibility study should follow political consensus-building, not precede it. Commissioned first, the study signals "experts will decide this technical problem" and triggers tribal defense. Communities become inoculated against its own conclusion before they can own it. The critical success factor across all six questions is the same: trust and felt agency must be built before facts are deployed, or the facts become ammunition for the opposition.

## Key Themes

**Sequence trust before analysis. Do the political consensus-building first and commission the feasibility study second, so the numbers validate a decision the communities already own rather than imposing a verdict they will reject.**

### Control, Not Cost, Is the Core Message
The persuasive frame across every question is agency: managed transition the community shapes versus forced surrender to a state receiver. This reframing defuses the "they're threatening us with crisis" objection by making identity loss the consequence of inaction rather than of consolidation. Pair it with a concrete preservation commitment every time, or it curdles into perceived emotional blackmail and provokes defiant no votes.

### Differentiate the Three Districts or Lose One
A single campaign pitch fails because the three districts carry different fears: the wealthiest fears tax burden, the smallest fears extinction, the middle fears being overlooked. The same underlying logic (local control, fiscal stability) must enter through three doors (quality, survival, leverage), with locally named guarantees published district by district. Because approval is unanimous, the campaign should concentrate disproportionate resources and a dedicated negotiating table on whichever single district is likeliest to veto.

### Structure Beats Persuasion on the Veto Problem
The simultaneous three-district threshold is best dismantled through architecture, not rhetoric. Use staggered or simulated-sequential votes for momentum, a signed binding agreement before the ballot, and per-district ombudspersons. Consider a legal shift to a 2-of-3 or supermajority rule. Multi-jurisdiction precedents (water districts, regional transit, annexations) consistently succeeded by giving each jurisdiction a tangible, named win rather than a shared abstraction.

### The Skeptic Is Won by Math She Does Herself, Delivered by a Peer
The fiscal conservative who flips does her own arithmetic on her own tax bill and is given permission by a trusted peer, not an official. She is relieved of a worse alternative rather than sold a vision, and the shift usually crystallizes around a personal stake (her building, her teacher, her grandchild). Peer-to-peer credibility outperforms institutional messaging by an order of magnitude, which makes the converted skeptic the campaign's single most valuable messenger.

### Honesty as a Trust-Building Instrument
In a trust-deficit environment, acknowledging consolidation's real costs (lost local autonomy, longer bus rides, centralized decisions) builds more credibility than minimizing them. Name the genuine identity loss in messaging, lock in specifics in the agreement so vagueness can't breed fear, and map trade-offs (not just finance) in the study. One hypothesis worth testing: needing an external study to decide at all may signal that the civic trust required for consolidation is already missing.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Run listening sessions in all three districts before any further analysis, documenting each community's specific fears and non-negotiables; scope (or re-scope) the feasibility study to answer those named questions.
- Identify the single likeliest-to-veto district through early, separate polling, and stand up a dedicated negotiating table for it.
- Reframe all public messaging from "consolidation saves money" to "controlled change versus forced surrender," and stop using the insolvency deadline as the stated reason for the vote.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Negotiate and sign a binding consolidation agreement (governance, budget formulas, facility decisions, multi-year tax and closure guarantees) before drafting any ballot language.
- Build a district-by-district "Consolidation Guarantee Sheet" with a specific, named win for each town, and structure holdout concessions as control levers (board seats, closure vetoes, transition periods) rather than resource transfers.
- Recruit and platform peer messengers (converted fiscal conservatives, parents, peer-district leaders) and lock in written superintendent and union endorsements across all three districts.
- Resolve the timing decision deliberately: commit either to the ~18-month-window logic or to the coalition-readiness logic, and gate the date on a defined readiness checklist rather than the calendar.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Place the vote in an off-cycle, lower-turnout, season-appropriate window once the coalition is demonstrably ready, releasing the study in phases (finance, then staffing, then facilities) with engagement periods between releases.
- Pursue a legal change to a 2-of-3 or supermajority approval threshold if available, or implement a staggered/sequential vote structure to convert the veto trap into cascade momentum.
- Pre-build and publicize a credible Plan B (regional cooperative or state-forced consolidation on worse terms) so voters understand a no is not a costless return to the status quo.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- The ~12% per-pupil funding increase and ~$1.2M administrative savings let advocates show many tax-sensitive voters a flat or lower personal bill. This is the single most persuasive data point for the wealthiest district.
- Multi-jurisdiction precedents offer a tested playbook (sequencing, binding pre-agreements, named per-district wins) that translates directly to the three-district vote.
- A converted skeptic carries more persuasive weight than any official. Each won fiscal conservative becomes a peer messenger with outsized influence.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- Commissioning analysis before building trust can inoculate communities against the study's own conclusion, making the feasibility study an obstacle rather than a tool.
- Crisis framing without a paired preservation commitment reads as emotional blackmail and hardens identity-based opposition.
- Under-resourcing the hardest district (the common failure mode in multi-jurisdiction votes) lets a motivated minority veto a deal the other two have accepted.

**Trade-offs**:
- Speed versus readiness: the smallest district's 24-month cliff pushes for an ~18-month vote, but genuine coalition-building may demand 24-30 months. Campaigning before the coalition exists loses the holdout.
- Holdout concessions versus fiscal case: resources promised to the reluctant district weaken the savings story the other two are buying. Make concessions structural (control) rather than financial.
- Transparency versus control: publicizing the veto and a Plan B makes districts negotiate in good faith but cedes the ability to manage the narrative tightly. Most perspectives judge that cost worth paying.
