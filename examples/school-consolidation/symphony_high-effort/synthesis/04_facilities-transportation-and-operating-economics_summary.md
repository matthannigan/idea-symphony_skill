# Summary: Facilities, Transportation, and Operating Economics

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension of this cluster is that the headline savings barely survive contact with reality, while the decision that actually determines whether consolidation succeeds is political, not financial. The $1.2M in administrative savings is real on paper, but the perspectives converge that it nets to roughly $670-900K once teaching-position backfill (the single largest hidden cost), a 15-25% transportation increase, and non-linear utility and facilities-management costs are counted. The "obvious" facility savings evaporate when total cost of ownership is modeled configuration-by-configuration rather than at the district level.

Two financial risks can swamp the case entirely and must be resolved before the vote. Bond-acceleration clauses in any of the three districts' indentures could force $2-5M in unbudgeted prepayment, so the model needs two scenarios (acceleration triggered and not) with bond-counsel certainty in writing. Mothballing is the second underpriced cost. A vacant building runs 30-40% of operating cost, and the hedged "maintain it lightly" middle state is the most expensive option of all.

The strongest consensus across all seven perspectives is procedural: decide which buildings close *now* and bind the criteria into the consolidation agreement. The worst possible moment to make that call is month two post-merger, before the three communities trust one another. Voters should see what they are voting for, not fear what comes later. The mechanism splits between a binding pre-negotiated plan and a transparent rule set, but the timing principle is unanimous.

The genuine reframe of the cluster is that facility closure is a community economic-development decision, not a facilities-efficiency problem. A 400-student school circulates an estimated $5-6M locally. Saving $1.2M while destroying $2M in secondary economic activity weakens the net case. The actionable form of this insight is to secure a funded successor anchor before closing, written into the same agreement that authorizes closure. The disciplining counterpoint is equally important: rural adaptive reuse mostly fails, urban anchor research does not transfer cleanly, and the frame must not become cover for pretending to preserve anchors through speculative repurposing.

## Key Themes

**The genuine reframe:** Facility closure is a community economic-development decision, not a facilities-efficiency problem — and where the secondary economic loss exceeds the administrative savings, the apparent case for closure reverses.

### The savings are largely illusory until proven otherwise
The headline $1.2M shrinks to roughly $670-900K once teaching backfill, transportation, and non-linear operating costs are netted out. It can go negative once secondary community economic loss is counted. Every analytical perspective insists on configuration-by-configuration modeling and a phased year-one-through-five timeline. District-level averages and a single five-year figure both hide the year-three trough when backfill hiring and capital-replacement cycles hit.

### Pre-commit the closure decision or lose the vote
All seven perspectives converge that the closure decision must be designed now and written into the consolidation agreement, not deferred to a freshly merged board with no shared trust. The split is over mechanism (a binding pre-negotiated plan versus a transparent rule with reversibility windows), but the underlying logic is identical: depoliticize by deciding before the merger, with cross-district representation and supermajority rules to prevent capture.

### Transportation and student burden as the hidden equity cost
The transportation increase (15-25%, driven by per-student-per-mile distance) falls hardest on remote, low-income, and elementary students. It costs homework time, extracurricular access, and college competitiveness in ways that never appear in the budget. Several perspectives treat this as a near-deal-breaker for configurations that push young children onto 45-60 minute rides and recommend a ride-time policy ceiling set before routing becomes a political battle.

### Mothballing and contingent liabilities are the underpriced risks
Bond-acceleration clauses ($2-5M potential), environmental remediation ($50-200K with long-tail liability), and mothballing (30-40% of operating cost) are routinely buried in footnotes. The recurring recommendation is to surface and price them as named line items before the vote. The hedged middle state and the unmodeled bond trigger are exactly where surprise-free budgets fail.

### Repurposing only works with a signed, independently funded partner
Repurposing revenue is real only when an outside operator with independent funding commits in writing. Otherwise it is a future cost dressed as a community asset. Rural reuse succeeds far less often than suburban examples suggest. The honest path is a market-feasibility study per building, with buildings that fail it earmarked for sale or mothballing rather than speculative "community asset" framing.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Engage bond counsel for all three districts to map acceleration, change-of-control, and disposition triggers, and obtain the answer in writing before the consolidation vote.
- Commission a facility conditions audit and Phase I environmental assessments on all seven buildings ($15-25K) to ground decisions in objective cost-of-operation data.
- Convene an independent facilities task force with cross-district representation plus neutral experts, charged with producing a binding facilities plan in 4-6 months and writing its criteria into the consolidation agreement.
- Draft the lean scorecard (utilization ~50%, 10-year fixed cost per pupil ~30%, closure ripple cost ~20%) and reject the temptation of a 30-plus-factor matrix.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Build configuration-by-configuration 10-year TCO models with two bond scenarios, explicit mothballing line items, and a phased year-one-through-five savings timeline rather than a single average.
- Commission a GIS routing study per facility configuration, set a ride-time policy ceiling (K-5 under 20-35 minutes), and reject or fund-mitigate any configuration putting more than 15% of students over 50 minutes one-way.
- Run a market-feasibility study per candidate-repurposing building, requiring a partner letter of intent before any repurposing investment.
- For any building slated to close, secure a funded successor anchor commitment in writing and embed it in the same agreement that authorizes the closure.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Establish a dedicated facility-maintenance reserve or modest endowment ($200-400K) from any disposition proceeds, never one-time operations or debt-bubble smoothing.
- Institute a facilities review cycle (every 3-5 years) with independent external audit and reversibility provisions, so closure decisions are revisable rather than permanent.
- Pursue county and state economic-development and rural-consolidation grant streams to fund successor anchors and adaptive reuse, potentially $200-500K in external funding that changes the politics and economics.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- Consolidation can refinance all three districts' debt at once under the unified district's stronger credit, potentially netting 3-5% annual debt-service savings.
- A larger district can finally offer specialized courses and staffing (AP, special ed, music, art) that small rural schools cannot, an educational upgrade that can aid teacher recruitment and retention.
- Repurposed buildings matched to genuine demographic need (pre-K hubs, health outposts, workforce training) can become funded successor anchors that give towns a reason to view consolidation as net benefit.
- Entering consolidation with quantified, pre-funded contingent liabilities ("zero surprises") is a strong confidence signal to voters and bond-rating agencies.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- Bond-acceleration triggers could force $2-5M in unbudgeted prepayment and reverse the financial case entirely.
- Teaching-position backfill, non-linear utility/custodial costs, and rising facilities-management overhead cut the headline $1.2M to roughly $670-900K.
- Long bus rides impose unbudgeted academic and equity harms that fall unevenly on remote, low-income, and elementary students. This can trigger a self-fulfilling "the consolidated district is worse" narrative.
- A flat numeric scorecard systematically condemns the most isolated, most community-dependent schools. Closure can set off a reinforcing enrollment-decline and residential-relocation cascade.
- Rural repurposing and community-ownership models frequently fail. Fragile nonprofit capacity can leave the district holding a stranded asset.

**Trade-offs**:
- Efficiency versus community economic survival: the most cost-efficient configuration may destroy more secondary local economic activity than it saves in administration.
- Centralized megaschool versus distributed multi-hub: fewer buildings cut facility costs but raise transportation cost and student burden. Two dispersed schools can sometimes beat one central school once transport is included.
- Pre-committed binding closure plan versus revisable rule with community veto: the former maximizes vote-time clarity, the latter rebuilds post-merger trust but slows decisions.
- Preserving optionality (hot-standby, mothballing) versus committing to clean open-or-closed status: optionality has real holding costs. The hedged middle is the most expensive state of all.
