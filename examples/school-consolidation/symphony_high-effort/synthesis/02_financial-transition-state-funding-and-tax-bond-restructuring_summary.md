# Summary: Financial Transition, State Funding, and Tax/Bond Restructuring

## Executive Summary

The financial case for consolidation is real but slower-acting than the crisis it is meant to solve. Across all five questions, the seven perspectives converge on one uncomfortable finding: the headline numbers are softer and later than the feasibility framing suggests. The $1.2M in administrative savings erodes to roughly $600-900K once a larger entity rebuilds coordination, transportation, and facilities overhead. Cumulative net savings does not turn positive until year 3 at the earliest, more likely year 4-5, which arrives after the smallest district's 24-month insolvency cliff. The most critical operational consequence follows from this timing: the smallest district's survival cannot depend on consolidation savings. A state or county bridge loan, framed as transition insurance, must decouple fiscal survival from the merger's actual financial performance.

The organizing strategic choice centers on timing. Consolidating now captures a real but time-limited 12% per-pupil funding step-change before state formula revisions and REAP reauthorization can erode it. The tradeoff: it locks in today's debt levels against a property-tax base that is still declining. Delaying for property-value stabilization and a lower eventual debt burden carries the risk of the smallest district's insolvency and continued enrollment loss. The responses lean toward acting now while the funding advantage holds, provided the bridge financing and the facility and debt decisions are settled before any vote.

Two themes run through every question. The first is that this cluster is governed by redistribution, not arithmetic alone. Tax equalization, bond allocation, and facility closures all create explicit winners and losers. The documented failure mode is concealing that fact until year 2-3 when it surfaces as a surprise rate increase or a debt bill for a building someone doesn't use. Transparency, modeled in per-household dollars before any ballot language, is repeatedly named as the only viable path to approval and the best protection against post-vote litigation. The second is that the new entity gains optionality that it must deliberately give up. Left without direction, a consolidated board will defer maintenance across all seven buildings, half-fund every town's school, and let the $4.2M liability grow to $6-7M. The remedy across all cases is to precommit: binding facility master plans with named closures, debt allocation by benefit, and earmarked reserves, written into the consolidation agreement and the referendum rather than left to a board making decisions under political pressure later.

The strategic implication is that financial analysis is necessary but not decisive on its own. The money works eventually on a shrinking base, but only if the politics of redistribution are handled with pre-vote transparency and only if the highest-rate district, which holds an effective veto, accepts phase-ins and targeted offsets.

## Key Themes

### Redistribution Is the Real Subject, and Transparency Is the Only Defense
Tax equalization, bond allocation, and facility closures each create explicit winners and losers. The recurring failure mode is hiding that until it surfaces as a surprise. Every perspective on equalization and bonds independently lands on the same prescription: model the dollar impact per household and per jurisdiction before the vote, publish it, and choose the least-resentment structure openly. Concealment is named as politically fatal and as legal exposure for post-vote misrepresentation suits.

### Pre-Commitment Beats Discretion
The consolidated board inherits the freedom to defer hard decisions, and that freedom is the trap. Without direction, it grows the $4.2M maintenance liability toward $6-7M, keeps six or seven buildings open for a 1,530-student district, and lets bond obligations dictate which buildings stay open. The shared fix is to remove optionality up front: binding facility master plans with named closures and decision dates, voter-authorized closure triggers, debt allocated by facility usage, and reserves set aside before money can be raided for operations.

### Fund the Burden From the Benefit
A repeated structural move is to dedicate a defined slice of the consolidation upside to the liability it must offset. Year-one savings seed a capital reserve for deferred maintenance (a 7-year runway without new debt), a formula-contingency reserve buffers state-funding risk, and a tax-rate-stabilization fund softens equalization shock for the highest-rate district. This turns diffuse risk into managed, precommitted buffers.

### The 24-Month Cliff Is a Forcing Function, Not a Funding Source
Several perspectives reframe the smallest district's insolvency clock as what makes consolidation urgent and credible to voters, while insisting it be solved by bridge financing rather than by savings that arrive too late. Decoupling solvency from the merger's financial performance lets the campaign focus on governance benefits instead of fiscal panic.

### Real Numbers Are Softer and More Uncertain Than the Headlines
The $1.2M savings is partly illusory and partly legally unavailable (state minimum-staffing rules). The 12% funding gain shrinks in absolute dollars as enrollment falls and could be clawed back. Deferred maintenance carries non-discretionary compliance costs. The personas' own dollar estimates for the per-pupil gain range from roughly $500K to $3M depending on the assumed base rate. This underscores that the magnitude must be verified with the state before it anchors any decision.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Commission a third-party facilities condition assessment (FCI scoring) of all seven buildings and request a formal state funding-projection letter specifying the exact per-pupil allocation, tier effects, and any clawback or wealth-threshold conditions, with a make-whole clause if the projection falls more than 5% short.
- Obtain bond counsel review of each district's indentures (covenants, material-adverse-change and consent requirements) and a rating-firm letter projecting the consolidated entity's credit, so debt restructuring options are known before the vote.
- Build a year-by-year transition cash-flow model with phased severance and realistic re-emerging overhead, and a sensitivity analysis across base-case and pessimistic enrollment scenarios, to find the true break-even year against the 24-month cliff.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Negotiate a state or county bridge loan that covers the smallest district's solvency gap independent of consolidation savings, contingent on that district relinquishing autonomous budget authority on day one.
- Publish per-household, per-district tax-equalization projections for year 1, year 3, and year 5, and design a 3-4 year phase-in with a glide path plus a savings-funded rate-stabilization fund and offers (capital-priority guarantees, temporary levy protection) targeted at the highest-rate district.
- Write a binding facilities master plan, named keeper and closure buildings, decision dates, and a 5-year maintenance schedule, into the consolidation agreement and referendum language, including voter pre-authorization to close buildings on defined enrollment or deferred-maintenance triggers.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Refinance the three bond series into a single unified instrument with debt service allocated by facility usage, retire closed-building debt early from sale proceeds and year-1 savings, and pursue state consolidation-assistance debt-service support for years 1-3.
- Stand up an independent facilities commission with town representation and an annual capital reserve (1.5-2% of plant value), and publish an annual per-jurisdiction equalization impact statement to keep redistribution transparent and re-equalized as values drift.
- Map and defend the enrollment floor that preserves the state funding tier, pairing retention and program investments funded from the per-pupil gain with the contingency reserve that absorbs formula or enrollment shocks.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- A real 12% per-pupil funding step-change available now. It can fund retention and quality programs rather than only plug deficits, if captured before formula revisions erode it.
- Consolidation creates leverage to right-size a seven-building footprint for a shrinking enrollment and to refinance fragmented debt at a stronger unified credit rating.
- Surplus buildings can be repositioned as hybrid or distance-education pilot sites, turning closures into forward-facing strategy rather than pure loss.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- Break-even arrives after the smallest district's insolvency cliff. Savings alone cannot rescue it. Bridge financing is mandatory, not optional.
- A larger inherited debt load against a declining, lower-value tax base can trigger a credit downgrade and a state fiscal-distress flag if debt service exceeds 10% of revenue.
- The 12% gain, the $1.2M savings, and the deferred-maintenance figure are all softer than stated. Savings can be legally barred by minimum-staffing rules. The funding gain shrinks with enrollment and faces REAP and clawback risk. Maintenance carries non-discretionary ADA and environmental costs.

**Trade-offs**:
- Consolidate now to capture the time-limited funding gain and solve insolvency, or delay for property-value stabilization and a lower debt burden at the cost of the smallest district's survival.
- Full immediate tax equalization (clean and fair on paper) versus a multi-year phase-in (buys the high-rate district's approval but extends uncertainty and grievance).
- Unify all bond debt for refinancing efficiency and better credit, or ring-fence or use usage-based allocation that protects taxpayer fairness but sacrifices some of that efficiency.
