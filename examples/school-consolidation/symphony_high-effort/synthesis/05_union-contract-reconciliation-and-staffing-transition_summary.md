# Summary: Union Contract Reconciliation and Staffing Transition

## Executive Summary

Contract reconciliation presents a dual risk and opportunity. It is simultaneously the largest hidden cost in the consolidation and its single highest-leverage opportunity. Which way it nets depends almost entirely on sequencing and funding choices. On the cost side, the brainstorming consistently identifies a warning: the "highest-common-denominator ratchet" (defaulting to the most favorable term on each contract dimension) is real, underestimated, and likely to consume between $300K and $800K of the projected $1.2M administrative savings. Pension and benefit obligations compound the problem in ways crude wage models miss. The savings figure that justifies the whole consolidation remains speculative until an independent labor economist models all three contracts side-by-side, dimension by dimension, before the number goes public.

On the opportunity side, the same negotiation that threatens the budget is the most powerful tool available for solving the staffing crisis and winning the vote. A unified district of 1,800 students can build career ladders, specialist roles, mentorship pathways, and supports (collaborative planning time, rural stipends, housing assistance) that no single small district could afford. Framed as "the most attractive rural contract in the state" rather than a cost-cutting compromise, reconciliation becomes a recruitment magnet and political proof that consolidation benefits teachers. The catch, named repeatedly, is fragile: this frame only holds if a meaningful share of savings (roughly 40-50%) visibly funds teacher-facing gains, and if the contract genuinely improves on all three legacy versions rather than splitting their differences.

The path between threat and opportunity runs through sequencing. The most consistent prescription in the entire cluster is a phased engagement timeline: binding seniority protection first (the single most-cited teacher anxiety), then salary-floor guarantees, then real (not token) decision authority on the transition committee. The public campaign should launch only after unions reach neutrality. Skipping any step intensifies opposition.

Two structural risks recur. First, the transition vacuum: three superintendents in nominal authority guarantee paralysis and talent flight. A single empowered transition leader and a binding freeze on the vulnerable district's commitments must be in place well before the vote. Second, absorption: without a district-blind seniority list and federated union governance, the largest local quietly absorbs the smaller two, and the resentment outlasts the merger. Across every question, the deciding variable is whether teachers experience consolidation as something built with them or done to them.

## Key Themes

### The savings number is hostage to the contract
The $1.2M administrative savings is the financial justification for consolidation, yet reconciliation cost can erode 25-67% of it before any program improvement is funded. Every analytical persona demands the same first move: an independent, transparent, dimension-by-dimension cost model published before the vote. A capped phase-in (grandfather clauses or fixed catch-up schedules) is essential to avoid both an open-ended ratchet and a fiscally fatal blanket hold-harmless.

### Built with them, not done to them
The single thread connecting recruitment, veteran retention, union leadership, and public support is procedural inclusion. Teachers stay, reps say yes, and votes flip when people have genuine authority over design (committee seats with real power, co-authored contracts, early information access). They flee or resist when decisions feel imposed. Voice consistently outranks money as the retention and persuasion lever.

### Seniority is the gateway, not a detail
Across the engagement-sequence, union-merger, and workforce-transition questions, seniority protection is named as the gateway issue: the deepest teacher fear, the first commitment that must be made binding and in writing, and the technical crux (a district-blind unified list by years of service) where mergers succeed or fail. Solve it first, publicly, and a meaningful share of opposition converts to neutrality.

### Reframe, but only if you can fund it
The cluster's organizing inversion is "contract as attraction, not cost." It is genuinely powerful and broadly endorsed, but the personas are equally clear that it is dangerous if superficial. A halfway housing program or savings that quietly vanish into reserves while workload rises will be detected. This will poison trust faster than honest cost-cutting would.

### Borrowed playbooks de-risk the novel parts
Two analogies do real work: union-merger precedent (federated structure, rotating leadership, district-blind seniority lists, anti-absorption governance) and rural manufacturing workforce transitions (portable seniority, voluntary buyouts, retraining stipends, regional workforce boards). Both let the district adopt proven mechanisms for its hardest problems rather than inventing ad hoc policy under political pressure.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Commission an independent labor economist or actuary to model all three contracts side-by-side by dimension (salary schedule, steps, prep time, extra-duty, health tiers, leave, pension), producing a realistic ratchet cost and a revised net-savings figure before the $1.2M claim is used publicly.
- Appoint or designate a single transition leader with explicit, board-resolution-backed authority over hiring, capital, and union matters, reporting to a consolidation oversight board rather than to the three sitting superintendents.
- Execute a binding inter-district MOU freezing multi-year personnel and capital commitments (e.g., above $50K) across all three districts, requiring unified approval, to prevent the most vulnerable district from encumbering the new entity.
- Convene the three union reps jointly for early information access (financial models, staffing projections) and offer to co-author the cost analysis.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Issue a binding, in-writing seniority-portability guarantee (years in any district count fully) as the first formal commitment, ahead of any compensation negotiation.
- Adopt a board resolution guaranteeing no current teacher's salary decreases plus a modest multi-year escalator (~2%/yr), funded from savings and the 12% per-pupil increase.
- Stand up a transition committee with genuine decision authority for union reps (overridable only by board supermajority) and a federated union governance design (chapters, rotating leadership, guaranteed seats).
- Pool the 14 vacancies into unified postings with a single interview panel, and publish a designed career ladder (instructional coach, curriculum specialist, mentor roles) tied to the new org chart.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Negotiate the unified contract as "the most attractive rural contract in the state," funding two or three concentrated provisions fully (rural stipend, collaborative planning time, mentorship pathway, housing assistance) rather than promising all of them thinly.
- Build a teacher-prep pipeline through university and post-bacc partnerships and state forgivable-loan programs, communicated honestly as a 5-10 year structural fix.
- Establish a county teacher workforce board and portable-benefit/pension-harmonization architecture so seniority and benefits follow teachers across consolidated buildings.
- Use the union-approved unified contract as a public proof point in the voter campaign, and consider a transition contract or sunset clause that defers the fully merged contract 18-24 months.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- Reconciliation reframed as a recruitment magnet: a genuinely better rural contract that pulls teachers from suburban districts tired of large class sizes.
- Real career ladders and specialist roles that only a 1,800-student district can support, addressing the 14-vacancy gap at low marginal cost.
- A union-approved unified contract as political evidence that consolidation benefits teachers, shifting the public-vote narrative.
- The Baby Boomer retirement wave reframed as planned succession, opening internal-promotion pathways.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- The ratchet silently consuming 25-67% of the $1.2M savings, discovered post-vote, destroying credibility.
- A three-superintendent leadership vacuum driving talent flight precisely when experienced staff are most needed.
- The largest union local absorbing rather than integrating the smaller two, breeding resentment that outlasts the merger.
- Unaddressed grief and quiet veteran ambivalence eroding morale invisibly while metrics look fine.
- The "contract as attraction" frame collapsing into bait-and-switch if savings don't visibly reach teachers.

**Trade-offs**:
- Cost control vs. fairness: a blanket hold-harmless is politically reassuring but fiscally fatal. An aggressive ratchet cap controls cost but risks the resentment that sinks the vote.
- Speed vs. legitimacy: phased engagement, co-design, and per-local ratification build buy-in but lengthen the timeline and risk deadlock.
- A district-blind unified seniority list is the fairest and most defensible mechanism. It means some small-district veterans may rank below larger-district peers—fairness in principle that still produces individual losers.
- Spending savings on teacher-visible gains wins the vote and recruitment but reduces the fiscal cushion the consolidation was partly meant to create.
