# Brainstorming Synthesis: Facilities, Transportation, and Operating Economics

---

## Synthesized Insights by Question

### Question 20: Facility scoring matrix and irreversibility

**Full question**: What weighted criteria — condition, capacity utilization, geographic centrality, transportation cost implication, community-anchor function, 30-year financial-obligation horizon — should structure a systematic facility decision framework across the seven buildings, and what monitoring signal would indicate that a closed building should have stayed open before the decision becomes practically irreversible?

* **Keep the scorecard small and utilization-led.** Complexity manufactures pseudo-precision and hides political choices as technical ones. A lean framework beats the full six-criterion matrix. Utilization is the dominant driver, with condition and a fixed-cost-per-pupil or transportation-cost proxy close behind. A 47-factor matrix produces false precision because the signal already lives in the occupancy and cost data. Geographic centrality earns weight mainly as a transportation-cost proxy. A workable default weights utilization, fixed cost per pupil over 10 years, and closure ripple cost at roughly 50/30/20. You can document this from existing asset data in 6-8 weeks.

* **The irreversibility signal is physical decay plus deferred-maintenance accumulation, not enrollment alone.** Instrument this before closing. A "closed" building whose deferred work exceeds its annual operating cost has crossed into practical irreversibility because reopening costs more than remediation. Pair mandatory annual condition assessments with enrollment triggers (automatic closure review if utilization falls below 60% for two consecutive years) and a written 24-month reopen window. Reopening a closed building costs 3-4x the savings achieved, so monitoring must track active decay, not just enrollment statistics.

* **Treat closure as reversible unless proven otherwise.** Build in a reopen or hot-standby option that also earns revenue. Replace the binary keep/close with options: keep-and-upgrade, repurpose-as-asset, mothball-for-optionality. Each building gets a time-limited assignment that can evolve. A declared 2-year "hot standby" (interim classroom space, adult-education hub, or community lease) generates revenue while preserving reversibility and lowers the political temperature. Score buildings for system resilience—which configurations absorb future enrollment shocks—not just current occupancy.

* **A purely numeric matrix systematically condemns the most isolated schools.** Rural satellite buildings lose on every dimension, so a flat matrix all but guarantees closure of the most geographically isolated schools. Either weight community-anchor function heavily (a "rural equity coefficient") to keep at least one satellite open, or acknowledge the bias openly. Closure can trigger a reinforcing cycle: families relocate, enrollment falls in remaining schools, the next closure conversation starts. The real question is which closure the system can absorb without cascading failure.

### Question 21: Total cost of ownership and bond-acceleration risk

**Full question**: For each plausible facility configuration, what is the 10-year total cost of ownership (utilities, maintenance, custodial staff, capital improvements at surviving buildings, plus mothballing or disposition of the rest), and which closure decisions trigger bond-obligation acceleration, community litigation, or other contingent liabilities that the financial model has not quantified?

* **Model TCO configuration-by-configuration, not at the district level; the "obvious" savings evaporate once second-order costs are added.** Aggregate district numbers mislead because facilities do not scale linearly. Each surviving/closing combination needs its own utilities, custodial, capital-backfill, and disposition lines. Closing 2 of 7 buildings saves roughly 20-25% of utilities and custodial, not 29%, because consolidated schools run at lower utilization. Show communities the cost of their own preferred configuration so the conversation shifts from "the district wants to close our school" to "which path can we afford?" Two medium buildings in different quadrants may outperform one centralized megaschool once transportation is included.

* **Mothballing is the underpriced cost; a vacant building runs 30-40% of operating cost and a deferred-maintenance emergency turns it into a six-figure crisis.** A vacant building still carries skeleton utilities, roof maintenance, property tax, and insurance. That runs about $100-150K/year, or $40-50K if mothballed to a defensible minimum. The half-measure "maintain it lightly" middle state is the most expensive option because deferred maintenance becomes a sudden $800K crisis when adaptive-reuse demand appears. Commit each building to a clear open-or-closed status. Where no signed partner exists, weigh sale or demolition explicitly against the cost of indefinite optionality.

* **Bond acceleration is a binary, district-specific risk that can swamp the savings; get bond-counsel certainty in writing before the vote, and model the two scenarios separately.** Many rural bonds carry change-of-control, disposition, or "material change in operational status" clauses. A closure could trigger $2-5M of unbudgeted prepayment. Build two 10-year TCO models, "acceleration triggered" and "no acceleration," because the case may work in one and fail in the other. Engage bond counsel now (a few thousand dollars) to map trigger points. Commission Phase I environmental assessments (remediation runs $50-200K with long-tail seller liability). Reserve 15-20% of claimed savings for contingencies. Negotiate with bondholders before the vote, not after.

* **Flip the bond constraint into a portfolio refinancing opportunity under the unified district's stronger credit.** Consolidation can refinance all three districts' debt at once. Acceleration of older bonds may be offset by a portfolio refi at lower rates, netting 3-5% annual debt-service savings. Quantify the hidden $500K-$2M contingent risk, then structure the consolidation bond to eliminate it. Entering consolidation "with zero contingent liabilities" sends a powerful signal to voters about financial management.

### Question 22: Repurposing as community-anchor preservation and balance-sheet lever

**Full question**: Rather than framing closure as loss, what repurposing models — community centers, early childhood hubs, adult learning facilities, community health outposts, workforce-training centers, senior services — have kept a school building alive as a community asset after K-12 use ends, what are the realistic market value and rental-income potentials per building in this rural context, and how should proceeds be structured (endowment, capital reserve, one-time debt paydown) to maximize long-term fiscal stability?

* **Repurposing revenue materializes only with a signed, independently funded partner.** Rural models like pre-K hubs, workforce joint ventures, and leased community centers at $15-30K/year barely cover 20% of holding costs. Each requires consistent weekday traffic and independent funding. If the building has a genuine second life, a nonprofit or operator will step in with a commitment. If no one steps in, there's no hidden opportunity. Require a market-feasibility study and a partner letter of intent before investing. Buildings that fail this test belong in honest mothballing or sale, not speculative "community asset" framing.

* **Disposition and rental income in rural markets are modest. Put any proceeds into an endowment or capital reserve, not one-time operations or debt smoothing.** A rural school typically sells for $500K-$1M. Disposition costs consume 15-25%, and net rental income averages $100K/year per building after costs (real money, but limited). Create a dedicated facility-maintenance reserve or modest endowment ($200-400K generating $8-16K/year indefinitely). This builds a cycle: good maintenance draws occupancy, occupancy feeds the reserve. One-time capital repairs or operations smoothing breaks that cycle and the district drifts back to deferred maintenance within a few years.

* **Reframe closure as a funded "transition," matched to each town's actual demographic need, and use community-stewardship models to move the building off the district's books.** Say "transitioning School X into a Community Learning Hub," not "closing School X." Match each building to its town: a senior/health hub where the population is aging, pre-K and aftercare where families are young, an incubator where the town is shrinking. Community land trusts and $1/year nonprofit leases clear the building from the balance sheet and preserve its anchor role. A building with pre-K, county health services, and evening classes can net $40-80K/year. Survey towns for unmet needs (early-childhood waitlists, rural health deserts, workforce gaps). Pilot one hub in year one with state and workforce grants. Value it for political stability, not quarterly cash flow.

* **Repurposing does not cure closure grief, and fragile rural nonprofit capacity can hand the district a stranded asset. Gate community-ownership on a readiness assessment.** Communities grieve lost identity, not the absence of a senior center. Repurposing defers the underlying loss rather than solving it. If the goal is fiscal relief, sell or demolish. If it is community continuity, say so plainly. Rural nonprofits are fragile. A tenant lost in year three or four leaves the district holding an empty building. Require a 12-18 month community-readiness assessment before any closure paired with community ownership. Structure shared-facility agreements with a 5-year operating reserve rather than single-tenant leases.

### Question 23: Transportation cost delta and student-experience burden

**Full question**: Consolidating across a larger geographic footprint will increase transportation costs and bus-ride durations — what is the estimated annual cost increase per route-mile under each plausible facility configuration, at what configuration does transportation consume a material portion of the projected administrative savings, and what does a 45-to-60-minute bus ride each way mean for homework completion, extracurricular participation, and fatigue-related learning outcomes that fall unevenly across parts of the county?

* **Transportation is where the headline savings get clawed back; name the offset honestly and cap it with a re-evaluation trigger.** Estimates cluster around a 15-25% increase in transportation cost (often $150-200K in year one), driven by per-student-per-mile distance rather than per-route-mile alone. A move from a 5-mile to a 12-mile radius can raise per-student distance 40%. State the net plainly: "saves $1.2M in admin, costs $200-300K more in transport, net $900K," even though the lower number is harder to defend. Require that transportation increases above ~25% of claimed admin savings trigger a re-evaluation. Budget an additional 5-10% on driver wages as a consolidation friction cost.

* **Long rides impose uneven, unbudgeted academic and equity costs that fall hardest on remote, low-income, and elementary students; protect the most vulnerable cohort explicitly.** A 45-60 minute ride costs remote students homework time, extracurricular access, and college competitiveness. It widens rural achievement gaps. Set ride-time policy now: keep K-5 rides under 20-35 minutes by keeping one small elementary per town and consolidating only middle and high schools. Reject any configuration that pushes more than 15% of students over 50 minutes one-way (or pair it with academic-support funding). The financial model should name the explicit choice: satellite academic hub, funded after-school programs, or accepting lower achievement in affected areas.

* **Treat transportation as a live design signal and a possible learning asset rather than a fixed line item, but do not over-engineer around geography.** Cost-per-route can serve as an early warning signal: a spike above model flags an inefficient configuration before it gets locked in. A distributed multi-hub network may cost less per mile than one megaschool. Staggered, sleep-friendly start times and a 30-minute dismissal window enable route clustering that saves 8-12% with no facility closure. Long rides can be partially converted to supervised homework or literacy time. The counter-discipline: 45-minute rides are a cost of rural geography, not bad planning. The real trade-off is simple. Either lengthen rides to keep small schools open, or consolidate and accept longer rides. Model where it bites (meal timing, extracurriculars) rather than over-engineering around it.

### Question 24: Hidden ongoing operating cost structure

**Full question**: Beyond transportation, what are the second-order operating costs of fewer, larger schools — staffing-ratio shifts at consolidated grade levels that may require backfilling teaching positions, utilities and custodial loads at concentrated facilities, larger facilities-management overhead — and how do these net against the headline administrative savings on a year-one through year-five basis?

* **Teaching-position backfill is the largest hidden cost; the headline $1.2M is closer to ~$670-900K once consolidated grade levels force new hires.** Direct administrative savings run roughly $820K (three superintendents, HR directors, transportation directors), but a consolidated school needs 1.5-2 teachers per grade plus counselor, special-education, and assistant-principal roles absent from the original headcount. Larger student bodies push for new course offerings (AP, special ed, math) that require more teachers, not fewer. Most models wrongly treat teaching costs as fixed when they shift with enrollment and program structure. Budget $80-120K for "new staffing to enable consolidation quality" in years 1-3, then phase out as scale economies kick in.

* **Utilities, custodial, and facilities-management overhead scale non-linearly. Efficiency gains are 10-20%, not 30-40%, and FM overhead often rises.** Concentrating students in older, larger buildings worsens per-square-foot energy intensity (higher surface-area-to-volume ratio) and adds 24-hour security costs. Expect 15-20% custodial and 10-15% utility reductions, not 30-40%. Utilities may even rise in year one when one large HVAC system costs more than three small ones. Facilities-management staff time grows 10-15% with more scheduling, compliance, and capital-project complexity. The projected 1.0-FTE manager may need to be 1.5. Track utilities by square footage, not building count, and budget $40-60K for a needed FM role.

* **Phase the model year-one through year-five so stakeholders see when savings actually arrive. Year three is when capital-replacement and attrition costs surface.** A single five-year average hides the messy middle. Year 1 might show ~$600K, Year 3 dip to ~$600K under backfill, Year 5 rebound to ~$850K as costs stabilize. The year-one honeymoon masks year-three drift as morale normalizes and attrition rises (model ~20% admin attrition and a facilities reserve growing from 2% to 5%). A rolling capital-replacement budget surfaces year-three roof/HVAC failures excluded as "outside consolidation scope." Year-one transition costs (IT, SIS migration, training, legal) run $250-400K and are routinely under-budgeted.

* **Don't manufacture new overhead to manage the merger. The phantom cost is the bureaucracy of consolidation itself.** Creating consolidation managers, facilities coordinators, and transition directors burns the very savings consolidation is meant to produce. Assign transition tasks to existing staff with a deadline. Many "hidden" costs are negotiable rather than fixed, so use consolidation to reset staffing models. Audit current staffing first. Some backfill simply replaces staff who would retire anyway, and a unified system redeploys retirements more efficiently than three separate ones, occasionally saving money.

### Question 25: Facility closure decision authority

**Full question**: The politically most explosive single decision — which school buildings to close or repurpose — will land on the new unified board shortly after consolidation, before trust between the three communities has been established; what governance mechanism (independent facilities committee, binding feasibility criteria, community input process with structured weighting) could be designed now and written into the consolidation agreement to depoliticize that decision enough to survive the early years?

* **Decide closure criteria now and bind them into the consolidation agreement; the worst moment to choose is month two post-merger, before shared trust exists.** This is the cluster's strongest consensus. An independent facilities committee or task force (drawn from all three communities plus neutral experts) should develop a binding facilities plan in the 4-6 months before the vote, so the unified board executes a pre-negotiated agreement rather than deciding on the fly. Building closures into the consolidation proposal makes the referendum "Do you support consolidation as proposed?" rather than "consolidate now, figure out closures later." This lets voters see what they are voting for instead of fearing surprises later.

* **Pair binding, published criteria with reversibility windows and reframe the vote from "buildings" to "scenarios."** Use transparent, published rules instead of consensus-building. Criteria like "average daily attendance below 250 in year 2 triggers a closure recommendation, with a 6-month reversibility window" give communities a metric and signal temporary reallocation rather than permanent abandonment. An independent committee should recommend 2-3 closure scenarios (each with impacts and mitigations), and let the board choose between them rather than individual buildings. This increases legitimacy. A neutral third-party consultant authoring a public decision matrix before consolidation becomes the arbiter when debates heat up.

* **Guard the process against capture and conflict of interest; representation and supermajority rules are load-bearing, not cosmetic.** "Structured weighting" is theater if one town controls the committee or board. Require at least one representative per original district, a two-thirds or supermajority vote, and independent external audits every three years. The newly merged board has inherent conflict because members protect their own district's schools. An external consultant should make the recommendation while a full-board vote ensures accountability. A 5-year community-benefit plan attached to any closure signals managed transition rather than abandonment.

* **Slow the announcement deliberately: separate the technical decision from the political reveal so mitigation is ready when closure is announced.** Finish the committee's analysis, then wait before announcing. Use the gap to design bus-route changes, pre-K hubs, repurposing plans, and union buy-in. This makes the announcement feel like a culmination rather than a surprise. A staged-closure alternative keeps all buildings open for two years, then requires a 90-day input period, repurposing feasibility study, and supermajority community vote before any closure. It trades speed for rebuilt trust with communities that feel blindsided.

### Question 26: The school-as-anchor parallel

**Full question**: Urban planners have long recognized that anchor institutions — hospitals, universities, post offices — don't just provide services; they organize surrounding community identity and economic activity. When anchors close, surrounding neighborhoods often destabilize in ways that extend far beyond the lost service itself. How does this "anchor institution" framework reframe the facility closure decisions: not as a facilities efficiency problem but as a community economic development problem — and what mitigation strategies (adaptive reuse, community ownership models, alternative anchors) have urban planners deployed when anchors leave that could apply here?

* **The reframe is load-bearing: facility closure is a community economic-development decision, and the secondary economic loss can exceed the administrative savings.** A 400-student school injects roughly $5-6M locally through direct payroll and multiplier effects. Saving $1.2M in administration while destroying $2M in secondary economic activity weakens the overall case. The model should quantify that community impact, not just the district budget. Closure triggers residential relocation and neighborhood decline (lost retail, property tax base, and population within five years), since the school is often the only institution spanning all ages in a community's daily life. In rural areas already declining from past closures, the closure signals disinvestment unless something replaces it.

* **Secure a funded successor anchor *before* closing, written into the same agreement that authorizes closure; an anchor lost without a replacement accelerates decline.** Identify and fund the replacement use (community center, health clinic, adult learning, county workforce office) with commitments in writing. Announce the successor at the moment of closure ("School A closes; the building becomes a rural health clinic") so loss converts to a needed gain. Earmark $100-200K per closed building to recruit partners that retain some secondary-spending activity. Communities with a funded alternative adjust better than those whose alternative funding disappears. The commitment belongs in the consolidation agreement itself, paired with county and state economic-development partnerships.

* **The anchor frame is a serious lens with hard limits; rural reuse mostly fails, urban research doesn't transfer cleanly, and the frame must not become cover for pretending to preserve anchors.** Urban adaptive reuse is expensive and frequently fails in rural contexts. Urban anchor research studies dense neighborhoods with alternative institutions and jobs that rural counties lack. Commission case studies of completed rural consolidations (Ohio, Iowa, Maine) tracking property values and migration before assuming reuse or ownership models will work. The frame explains community loss but does not by itself decide closures. The honest mitigation is to acknowledge the loss, decide whether consolidation benefits justify it, invest the savings in the community in other ways, and avoid using the frame as cover for wishful thinking about adaptive reuse.

* **Some communities may be better served by distributed alternatives than by defending a single anchor. Absent a realistic economic anchor, honesty favors mothballing or sale.** Distributed, multi-institution anchors (a mixed-use building where a library, workforce program, and health department each commit stable funding) are more resilient than single-anchor dependence. The consolidated district could position itself as a county-wide tele-tutoring or workforce-training hub. Tie facility disposition to what the county actually needs (a nursing program for a nursing shortage, an ag-extension hub for a labor shortage). Where no realistic anchor emerges, plan mothballing or sale rather than hopeful reuse scenarios.
