# Strengths, Vision, and Coalition-Building - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Peak Moments of Cross-District Collaboration and Staff Loyalty

* **Have we considered whether past collaborations were exceptions, not evidence of capacity?** The districts may have cooperated on a one-time grant-funded program or a single joint sporting event precisely because it was bounded, optional, and low-stakes — nothing like the permanent, mandatory, authority-redistributing reality of consolidation. Before treating those moments as proof of "latent capacity," organizers should audit what actually made them work: Was it a charismatic individual who has since retired? An external funder who subsidized coordination costs? If the answer is yes, the collaboration reveals nothing durable. Mitigation: interview the people who ran those partnerships and ask explicitly what they would not do again.

* **Staff loyalty to small-district culture may be loyalty to the very things consolidation threatens.** Teachers who stayed despite lower pay often cite autonomy, knowing every student by name, and direct access to the principal. A merged 1,800-student district with standardized curriculum, centralized HR, and a superintendent they will never meet may feel like the urban bureaucracy they chose to leave. Treating their persistence as endorsement of consolidation mistakes the reason they stayed. Mitigation: structure the unified district's school-level governance explicitly to preserve site autonomy on pedagogy and staffing culture; make that a contractual commitment, not a campaign promise.

* **One potential challenge is that "peak collaboration" stories get weaponized selectively.** Supporters will highlight the joint AP Biology cohort; opponents will highlight the three-year dispute over shared-bus routing that collapsed before it started. The county board should commission a complete inventory of cross-district cooperation attempts — successes and failures — and publish it before the vote. Selective memory on either side will erode trust once the real negotiations begin.

* **The latent strengths of small-district culture are real, but they are also fragile and hard to transplant at scale.** The multi-grade classroom teacher who knows every family in town is a product of necessity and community embeddedness, not a transferable organizational method. Consolidation planners who promise to "preserve what's great about small districts" without specifying the exact mechanisms are setting an expectation they cannot honor. Mitigation: name three to five specific structural features — advisory systems, family liaison roles, school-level budget discretion — that will be built into the unified district's design documents from day one.

## The Model Others Travel to Study

* **Have we considered that the 2031 success-story framing assumes the hardest problems solve themselves?** The $1.2M administrative savings become a student-facing investment only if the board actively appropriates them that way and resists the gravitational pull toward filling operational gaps, absorbing transportation cost overruns, and addressing the $4.2M deferred maintenance backlog. Without a formal earmark — a board-adopted policy that locks savings into a Student Opportunity Fund before they enter the general fund — the narrative that "savings go to classrooms" is aspirational, not structural. Mitigation: draft that earmark policy as part of the consolidation agreement, not after.

* **The skeptics who "were surprised" in 2031 may not exist in large numbers.** Research on rural school consolidation consistently shows that the most vocal opponents do not become converts — they move, disengage, or wait for the next election cycle to reverse course. A more honest version of the 2031 scenario asks: what percentage of "our school is our town" families actually stayed, and are they satisfied, or merely resigned? Planners who build the whole model on converting opponents will miss the quieter risk of gradual community disengagement after the vote passes. Mitigation: track civic participation indicators (school board turnout, volunteer hours, booster club membership) in each legacy community annually as early-warning metrics.

* **One potential challenge is that the "prototype for other counties" goal is a distraction from the actual goal of serving 1,800 students well.** Optimizing for replicability and press visibility can subtly warp design decisions — choosing a governance model that looks clean on a presentation slide rather than one that fits this county's specific political geography. Consolidations that become case studies often do so because they had unusual advantages (strong county-level leadership, a major anchor employer, state grant support) that do not generalize. Mitigation: build for this community first; let replicability emerge from genuine success rather than design it in as an objective.

* **The 2031 vision sidesteps the decade before 2031.** Enrollment will continue declining; the smallest district faces insolvency within two years; transition costs will likely exceed the Year 1 savings. The success story assumes the district survives the consolidation transition without a budget crisis that forces early facility closures or mid-year layoffs that poison community trust permanently. Mitigation: the feasibility study must model a realistic worst-case transition scenario — not just steady-state savings — and show the board and public exactly how much fiscal runway they have before savings materialize.

## Coalition Builders Already Present and a Small Win Before the Vote

* **Have we considered that the "bridge builders" identified by the county board may not be trusted by their own communities precisely because they are willing to bridge?** In high-identity towns, the parent who is known to have friends in the neighboring district can be dismissed as a traitor or a "regionalist" rather than elevated as a leader. The most effective coalition validators are often not the obvious cross-community connectors but respected internal skeptics who conclude, publicly and reluctantly, that consolidation is necessary. Mitigation: identify two or three respected opponents in each community and create genuine structured opportunities for them to engage with the financial evidence — not town halls designed to persuade them, but small-group sessions where they can ask hard questions and get real answers.

* **The "small win before the vote" strategy carries real execution risk.** If the county board launches a joint program — a shared summer enrichment offering, a combined robotics team — and it is perceived as underfunded, poorly organized, or administratively chaotic, it becomes proof that consolidation will be incompetent, not proof that it will succeed. A failed preview is worse than no preview. Mitigation: the pre-vote pilot should be scoped to something the county can execute flawlessly: a single joint event with logistics managed by an experienced outside coordinator, rather than a months-long program that depends on three separate district offices cooperating smoothly.

* **One potential challenge is that coalition building focuses on persuadable moderates while ignoring the structural veto held by voters in the most resistant district.** All three districts must approve for consolidation to proceed. If the smallest district — the one facing insolvency — is also the most identity-protective, its voters may vote against their own fiscal interest because the coalition strategy never spoke to their specific fear of disappearing. A coalition that wins two districts and loses one has wasted significant effort. Mitigation: map each district's voter population separately; build a distinct message and messenger strategy for each rather than a single unified campaign.

* **The six-month timeline for building coalition credibility may be too optimistic given the underlying trust deficit.** These communities have presumably watched their fiscal situation deteriorate for a decade under the current structure. The county board commissioning a feasibility study is not, by itself, a trust-building act — it signals that outside experts will make decisions about communities' schools. Mitigation: before the board selects coalition builders, host one unstructured listening session in each community with no agenda and no presentations — just board members hearing grievances. Coalition credibility requires demonstrated listening before demonstrated persuasion.
