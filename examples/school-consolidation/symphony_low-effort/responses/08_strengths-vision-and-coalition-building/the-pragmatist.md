# Strengths, Vision, and Coalition-Building - The Pragmatist

---

## Cross-District Collaboration Peaks and Loyal Staff

* **Document what already works before redesigning anything.** A practical approach is to survey teachers and administrators who have remained in these districts for 5+ years and ask a single focused question: "What's the one collaboration with another district that actually stuck?" The answers will surface specific programs — a shared CTE pathway, a joint JV sports co-op, a combined summer school — that already have working logistics behind them. Those programs are your consolidation pilot sites. Build the new district around what demonstrably functions, not around an idealized blueprint.

* **Loyal staff are an underused diagnostic tool.** Teachers who stayed in low-pay rural positions despite options elsewhere are self-selected for place attachment. Their reasoning — proximity to family, commitment to specific students, belief in small-school culture — is exactly the community glue consolidation needs to preserve. A practical first step: conduct 30-minute structured interviews with a dozen of these staff across all three districts to map which elements of small-district culture they'd protect (e.g., multi-grade relationships, staff autonomy, community visibility) versus what they'd willingly trade for better resources. This interview data directly informs the consolidated district's design choices.

* **Identify the cross-district collaboration that produced measurable student outcomes.** Not all past collaborations are equal. The ones worth scaling are those with traceable results — enrollment numbers, test scores, student feedback, teacher retention in the program. If districts shared a science lab or jointly hired a specialist, pull the participation data. A practical coalition argument is not "we once worked together" but "shared Program X served 87 students and cost each district 40% less than running it separately — a consolidated district makes that permanent and extends it."

* **Protect the staff density advantage of small districts.** One genuine small-district strength is that teachers know every student's name, family, and situation. A consolidated district of 1,800 students is still small by national standards. To make this feasible and preserve that advantage, the new governance structure should keep schools as community units — principals with real local authority, no K-12 mega-campus — while centralizing only administration (HR, transportation, payroll). That's the core trade: lose the superintendent redundancy, keep the relationship density.

## The 2031 Success Story Delegation

* **Work backward from the 2031 headline to the 2026 decisions that made it possible.** If the prototype story is "administrative savings redirected to student programs," then the board needs to make one binding commitment now: establish a dedicated fund where the $1.2M annual savings are earmarked for specific programs — AP courses, a counseling hire, a CTE partnership with a community college — and publish the allocations before the vote. Voters don't trust "savings will benefit students" in the abstract; they trust a line-item budget with names attached.

* **The "what surprised former skeptics" answer is usually governance, not programs.** In consolidation case studies, community opposition softens most when skeptics discover their local school still feels local — the principal knows the kids, the school mascot survived, the fall festival still happens. A practical model: unified district administration, retained building identities. This means the 2031 delegation sees three schools with distinct community characters operating under one financial backbone. That story is achievable and genuinely replicable. Committing to it now — in writing, as a board resolution — is the thing that unlocks former opponents.

* **The $1.2M redirection is only credible if the transition costs are honestly accounted first.** Before any 2031 vision lands, the board needs a plain-language one-page showing: Year 1-2 transition costs (severance, IT integration, legal fees), Year 3 break-even point, Year 4+ net positive cash flow. Delegations from other counties will study this district precisely because it was transparent about the messy middle. Building that honest financial narrative now makes the 2031 story more credible, not less.

* **Exportable prototype requires replicable governance documentation.** A practical approach for counties that want to be studied: start documenting process decisions in real time — meeting minutes with rationale, the union negotiation framework, the facility decision criteria. Other rural counties don't want to copy your programs; they want your decision templates. Keeping that record from day one is low-cost and high-value.

## Coalition Builders Already Present and a Small Win Before the Vote

* **Map the existing cross-district connectors by function, not by title.** The practical way to find your coalition builders is to ask one question to school board members and principals in each district: "Who do you call when you need something done in one of the other two districts?" That person — coach, retired administrator, church leader who also volunteers in the school — is your coalition anchor. They already have social capital that took 20 years to build. The county board can't manufacture that in 18 months; they can invest in people who already have it.

* **The single best pre-vote small win is a shared visible program, not a report.** A practical six-month action: launch a joint elective — an AP class via video link, a regional skills competition, a shared robotics team — that requires students from at least two districts to participate together. Cost is minimal (a teacher stipend, video conferencing equipment). The payoff is concrete: parents drive their kids to a unified event, students make friends across town lines, and the local newspaper has a photo. That's harder to vote against than a feasibility study.

* **Business owners are an underused coalition anchor.** Local employers who struggle to find qualified workers have a direct economic stake in what the consolidated district could offer — dual-enrollment with the community college, a healthcare pathway, a trades program. A practical coalition-building move: the county board convenes a 90-minute session with 10-15 local employers from all three towns to ask what skills gaps they face and what programs they'd publicly endorse. Getting three business owners to sign a joint letter — one from each town — is a faster credibility signal than any consultant presentation.

* **Pre-vote transparency on the hard questions reduces opposition more than optimism does.** A practical approach for the six-month window: hold one public session in each town specifically to answer "what happens to our school building?" and "what happens to our teachers?" with specific, honest answers. Not "we'll consider all options" but "here are the three facility scenarios and what each means for your town, with the trade-offs stated plainly." Voters who feel they've been given a straight answer are more persuadable than voters who feel they're being sold a vision. The small win before the vote may simply be demonstrating that the board can be trusted to tell the truth.
