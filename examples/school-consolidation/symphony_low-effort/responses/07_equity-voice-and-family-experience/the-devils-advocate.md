# Equity, Voice, and Family Experience - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Pre-Existing Inequities and What Consolidation Is Obligated to Fix

* **Averaging erases the debt owed to the weakest district.** Have we considered that framing all three districts as "equivalent starting points" in a consolidation plan is itself a political choice that benefits the wealthier districts? If District C has had crumbling science labs, a 40% teacher-turnover rate, and no AP courses for a decade, then consolidation that "levels" programs by splitting the difference perpetuates that disadvantage under a new banner. Mitigation: the feasibility study should document pre-existing gaps per district as a baseline obligation, not merely a historical footnote, and the consolidation agreement should include binding remediation commitments with timelines.

* **Infrastructure inequity will surface in facility decisions.** One potential challenge is that "which buildings to close" discussions will disproportionately target the weakest-maintained facilities — which are often the ones in the lowest-tax-base towns. Planners may rationalize closure on cost grounds while inadvertently punishing the community that could least afford deferred maintenance in the first place. We could address this by requiring the facility analysis to separate "costly because neglected" from "costly because structurally nonviable," and applying an equity lens before any closure recommendation is finalized.

* **Teacher contract reconciliation will replicate old hierarchies.** When three union contracts merge, seniority rules and salary lanes typically favor teachers from the better-resourced district. The result: teachers from the under-resourced district lose scheduling priority, professional development access, and preferred assignments. A merged contract that doesn't explicitly address this will lock in existing inequity. Mitigation: require joint union-management equity review of all seniority provisions before ratification, with the goal of not disadvantaging staff who were already working in harder conditions.

* **Program remediation costs money that savings projections don't include.** The $1.2M administrative savings figure is frequently invoked, but have we stress-tested whether it gets consumed by the cost of bringing District C's program quality up to a defensible standard? Consolidation arithmetic that counts savings without counting remediation obligations is politically convenient but analytically dishonest. The feasibility study should model equity-remediation costs as a line item, not an afterthought.

## Families with Structural Barriers to Participation

* **The public hearing format pre-selects who is heard.** Have we considered that a Tuesday evening town hall at 7pm at the district office is not a neutral venue — it is accessible to employed-but-not-evening-shift parents with reliable transportation and no young children at home? The official communication strategy almost certainly assumes a literate, English-proficient, schedule-flexible audience. If the three districts include migrant families, hourly service workers, or families without reliable internet, those assumptions will silently exclude a large share of the people most affected by school quality. Mitigation: audit the communication plan against actual demographic data before launch; add Spanish-language materials, in-person childcare at hearings, and afternoon or weekend options.

* **Reading-level assumptions are rarely checked.** One potential challenge is that public meeting notices and feasibility study summaries are typically written at a tenth-grade reading level or higher, while a meaningful portion of rural families across socioeconomic spectrums read closer to a sixth-grade level. "We posted it on the website" is not informed consent. We could address this by commissioning a plain-language summary of any public document that runs longer than two pages, with reading-level testing before release.

* **Families with children on IEPs face unique communication barriers.** Parents navigating special education often have prior negative experiences with the district's formal processes — they may distrust official channels and have learned that passive communication from the district usually precedes service reductions. The transition framing needs a distinct outreach strategy for IEP families, not the same generic mailer. Mitigation: route special education communications through the special education directors and existing parent liaison relationships, not through the general public-meeting process.

* **Non-voters and renters are systematically excluded from the outcome.** The formal consolidation mechanism is a district vote, which means families who rent, students' parents who haven't registered, and recent arrivals don't participate — yet their children attend these schools. Have we considered that the vote could pass over the opposition of the families with the greatest stake in program quality, simply because they face higher registration and participation barriers? A mitigation is to supplement the vote with a structured listening process — surveys at school pick-up lines, conversations with PTO groups — specifically designed to capture voices the ballot won't.

## Children in Unstable Circumstances and the Student Transition Experience

* **Transition stress peaks at the moments no one plans for.** Have we considered that the emotionally hardest consolidation moments are not the formal launch date but the invisible ones: the week a child discovers their best friend will attend a different "home" school, the day the familiar counselor's office no longer exists, the moment a kid with an IEP meets the third new case manager in eighteen months? Planning that focuses on logistics and ignores these micro-transitions will produce the outcome consolidation planners fear most — angry families, disengaged students, and post-vote backlash. Mitigation: map the student emotional journey, not just the operational one, and identify the three to five highest-stress transition points with a specific support plan for each.

* **Foster youth and housing-insecure students have no margin for disruption.** One potential challenge is that children already experiencing instability — foster placements, families in motels or shared housing — are disproportionately harmed by any system disruption because they lack the family stability to absorb it. Consolidation changes transportation routes, counselor assignments, and school identities all at once. For these students, "change is normal" is not a reassurance; it is an additional burden. We could address this by designating a specific transition coordinator with caseload responsibility for vulnerable student populations, funded as a line item in the consolidation budget rather than assumed to be absorbed by existing staff.

* **IEP continuity is a legal requirement, not a nice-to-have.** Have we considered that if consolidation disrupts IEP service delivery — even temporarily, even due to administrative chaos — the district is in federal legal violation? Schools have been sued over far less. The transition plan needs an explicit IEP portability protocol, a named individual responsible for each student's file transfer, and a 90-day check-in after transition to verify services are being delivered as written. Ignoring this is not just an equity failure; it is a liability exposure.

* **Naming the risks doesn't mean consolidation is wrong — it means the plan needs to absorb them.** The risks above are real, but the status quo also harms vulnerable students: shrinking programs, high teacher turnover, and financially insolvent districts are not child-friendly either. The question is not "consolidation or no consolidation" but "consolidation with or without adequate safeguards." A consolidation plan that explicitly funds transition support for high-needs students is more defensible than the current fragmented system; one that ignores these risks is not.

## Voter Approval as Proxy Versus the Voices a Vote Cannot Capture

* **The feasibility study's question list is never neutral.** Have we considered who actually drafted the questions the feasibility study is supposed to answer? If it was the county board, the superintendent consortium, or the consulting firm — all of whom have professional stakes in a legible, budget-focused answer — then the study will efficiently answer the wrong questions. Students, non-property-owning parents, and hourly workers were not in the room when the scope was set. Mitigation: convene a citizen study-design committee that includes at least two parents from each district, one or two high school students, and representatives from the communities' lower-income populations before the consultant finalizes the scope of work.

* **A vote does not create accountability; it creates a one-time permission.** One potential challenge is that the consolidation vote, if passed, will be treated as community endorsement forever — "the community chose this" — even though the specific facility closures, program decisions, and staffing choices made afterward were not on the ballot. This is a governance design flaw, not an accident. We could address this by establishing a community oversight board with real authority — not advisory — that includes parent representatives and meets quarterly for the first three years post-consolidation, with the power to flag concerns to the state education authority.

* **Students are the primary stakeholders excluded by design.** The adults vote. The children attend. This inversion of accountability is worth naming explicitly: the group most directly affected by consolidation quality has no formal voice in the process. A student advisory council with documented input rights — not just a feel-good presentation slot at one board meeting — would create a structural check on decisions that affect daily student experience. Mitigation: require the new unified board to seat one high school student representative with speaking rights and establish a student input process for any facility or program change in the first five years.

* **Co-designing the study creates buy-in that a polished consultant report cannot.** If representative community members — including skeptics — helped shape the feasibility study's questions, they are more likely to accept its findings even when those findings are uncomfortable. A study designed by insiders and presented to the public is a document to be contested; a study designed with community input is harder to dismiss as a predetermined conclusion. The cost of co-design is four to six additional months; the cost of a failed vote is years of stasis.

## Sequencing the Feasibility Study Release Against the Political Calendar

* **The first framing sets the floor for the entire debate.** Have we considered that whichever community hears the findings first will set the informal narrative for the other two? If the study is released first in the district most hostile to consolidation, the public framing could calcify around "merger threat" before the district most open to it has a chance to establish "shared opportunity" language. The sequencing decision is not logistical; it is strategic. Mitigation: release a brief, plain-language executive summary simultaneously in all three communities before any individual town hall, so the first public framing is shared rather than fragmented.

* **Who presents matters as much as what is presented.** One potential challenge is that if the feasibility study is presented by the county board or a hired consultant, the messenger will be read as the message: "the people who commissioned this are telling us what they already decided." Conversely, if the study is presented by a local trusted figure — a longtime principal, a respected retired teacher, a community foundation leader — the credibility transfer is dramatically different. We could address this by identifying two or three respected community voices in each district and briefing them on the findings before the public release so they can speak to it authentically.

* **A simultaneous release risks losing control of interpretation.** The argument for sequencing is also real: if all three communities receive the findings at once through a press release, the interpretation will be driven by whoever speaks first publicly — which may be the most vocal opponent. A sequenced approach with facilitated community conversations at least ensures the first public discussion is moderated. Mitigation: pair any simultaneous release with pre-scheduled moderated community conversations in each district within the same two-week window, with a shared facilitator who maintains consistent framing across all three.

* **The political calendar interacts with the study release in ways planners often ignore.** If the feasibility study drops during a budget crisis or local election cycle, the findings will be filtered through that immediate anxiety. Have we considered working backward from the consolidation vote timeline to identify which months are politically low-noise, and scheduling the release accordingly? A finding released in October, two weeks before a local election, will be weaponized differently than the same finding released in February. The timing choice is a risk management decision, not just a scheduling convenience.
