# Equity, Voice, and Family Experience - The Pragmatist

---

## Pre-Existing Inequities and Consolidation Obligations

* **Baseline audit before averaging.** A practical approach is to commission a side-by-side program inventory across all three districts before the feasibility study concludes — listing AP courses, elective breadth, facility condition ratings, and teacher turnover rates by district. This creates an undeniable factual baseline. The weakest-resourced district almost certainly has the thinnest program offerings. If you don't document the gap now, you lose the leverage to write equity floors into the consolidation agreement later.

* **Equity floors written into the merger agreement, not aspirational language.** To make this actionable, the feasibility study should recommend specific minimum program guarantees for the unified district — for instance, at least two AP courses per content area, instruments and uniforms funded per pupil, not per school. These commitments need to be binding at the board level, not left to future budget cycles where the historically advantaged community's advocates will dominate.

* **Phased investment, not averaging down.** The risk of treating consolidation as an averaging exercise is real: the strongest programs get cut to equalize, and students in the weakest district gain nothing. A more feasible framing is a three-year step-up plan for the underfunded district's programs, funded from the $1.2M administrative savings. This gives voters in the underfunded district a concrete reason to vote yes, and it gives fiscal conservatives a clear payback story.

* **Assign a transition equity monitor.** A practical mechanism is to appoint a small advisory committee — two to three parents from the historically underfunded district — with formal standing to flag equity regressions to the new board during the first three years. This isn't a veto body; it's an early-warning system. Low cost, high credibility signal, and it builds trust with the community most likely to be skeptical.

## Families with Structural Barriers to Participation

* **Tiered outreach, not a single town hall format.** A practical minimum is running at least three formats: an evening town hall, a recorded video with a simple feedback form (available at the library and school office), and a daytime parent meeting at each school building. This addresses shift workers and those without childcare without requiring a budget overhaul. The video format also doubles as the plain-language summary most residents actually want.

* **Plain-language materials as a deliverable, not an afterthought.** The feasibility study deliverable should include a two-page plain-language summary written at an eighth-grade reading level and professionally translated into Spanish if more than five percent of the district population is Spanish-speaking. Both versions should be mailed home with report cards, not just posted on a website. This is achievable for under $5,000 and removes the most common barrier immediately.

* **Partner with trusted intermediaries for hard-to-reach families.** Rather than asking non-English-speaking or housing-insecure families to navigate official school channels, a more feasible approach is working through already-trusted organizations — the food pantry, the church that runs the after-school program, the mobile health van. These groups have existing relationships and can pass along information and collect feedback with far less friction than a formal public meeting.

* **Track participation gaps as a process metric.** Before the vote, the county board should publish a simple participation report showing who attended hearings by school building and geography. If one community is systematically underrepresented, that's an actionable finding: schedule another session, add a home visit option, or partner with a local group. Making the gap visible creates accountability that a generic "we held public hearings" sign-off doesn't.

## Children in Unstable Circumstances and the Student Transition Experience

* **Map the high-risk student population before transition planning begins.** A practical first step is pulling three data sets: current IEP caseloads by building, foster care placements on record, and students who have experienced two or more school transitions in the past three years. This takes a week and gives you a concrete population to design around rather than generic "vulnerable students" language. The number is probably manageable — likely a few hundred students — which makes targeted support feasible.

* **Assign continuity staff, not just continuity policies.** Policies that say "IEP services will transfer" don't prevent the relationship disruption that destabilizes kids. A more feasible mechanism is creating a small transition support team — one or two school counselors whose primary job during the first consolidation year is following cohorts of high-risk students across the building change. The $1.2M in administrative savings more than covers this cost for two to three years.

* **Identify the three highest-risk transition moments and staff them specifically.** Rather than designing a broad support program, identify the specific pinch points: the first day in a new building, the first IEP meeting under new staff, and the first report card under new grading systems. Each of these has a known date and a finite set of affected students. Prepare a specific protocol for each moment rather than a general wellness policy.

* **Build a family early-warning system for the first semester.** To make this feasible quickly, create a brief monthly check-in card — paper, not digital — that goes home with students during the first consolidation semester. The question is simple: "Is anything about the transition creating a problem for your child? Yes / No / I'd like to talk with someone." This gives counselors a proactive caseload rather than waiting for crises to surface.

* **Don't dissolve existing staff relationships before the new ones are built.** A practical transition rule: no IEP team reassignments take effect until the new provider has had at least two in-person meetings with the student and the family. This is a concrete, enforceable standard that prevents the most common harm — a student losing a trusted adult before a replacement relationship exists.

## Voter Approval as Proxy and Voices a Vote Cannot Capture

* **Establish a student advisory panel as a concrete deliverable of the feasibility study.** Rather than abstract language about "student voice," a practical approach is forming a twelve- to fifteen-member student panel drawn from all three districts, meeting monthly for the six months before the vote. The panel's job is to review the student-experience sections of the feasibility study and submit a written response that is formally published alongside the study. Low cost, high legitimacy, and it directly addresses the gap the ballot box creates.

* **Run a structured community co-design session, not an open comment period.** Open comment periods consistently over-represent the most organized and vocal constituency groups. A more representative process is one or two structured community workshops using small-group formats where a trained facilitator ensures quieter participants contribute. Record specific input from renters, non-English speakers, and students, and show in the final report how that input changed the study's recommendations.

* **Non-property-owning residents can be reached through the lease channel.** Many renters in rural districts have children in the schools but can't vote on property-related referenda. A practical proxy for their voice is a brief survey distributed through landlords, churches, and the housing authority during the feasibility period. The results don't change the legal vote structure, but they do give the board documented grounds to say the plan reflects a broader constituency than the ballot.

* **Build accountability mechanisms beyond the vote into the merger agreement itself.** A practical approach is writing a five-year review clause into the consolidation agreement, requiring the new board to publish an equity report card — program parity, transportation times, student outcome gaps by feeder community — and hold a public comment session on the results. This creates structural accountability that outlasts the vote and gives skeptical communities a reason to engage rather than just oppose.

## Sequencing the Feasibility Study Release

* **Release the executive summary to all three communities simultaneously, same week.** The single highest-risk sequencing error is letting one community see the findings before the others. If the district with the most to gain hears the financial case first, they'll frame the narrative before the other two communities have context. A practical protocol is a coordinated dual-release: all three school board meetings in the same week, same slide deck, same presenter if possible.

* **Lead with the financial floor, not the opportunity ceiling.** The framing that will land best in the most skeptical community is not "here's how much better things will be" but "here's what becomes unavoidable if we don't consolidate." The two-year fund balance figure for the smallest district is the most clarifying data point in the feasibility study. Release that number first, clearly, with the year-by-year runway. This converts the conversation from "merger" to "what are the real options."

* **Use a neutral third-party presenter for the first public release.** The county board has a credibility problem in any community that suspects the consolidation decision is already made. A practical approach is having the external feasibility consultants present the initial findings, not the board members. This separates "here is what the analysis found" from "here is what we want you to approve" — a distinction that matters enormously to skeptical audiences.

* **Plan community-specific follow-up sessions within two weeks of the initial release.** The coordinated release sets the frame; the follow-up sessions address community-specific concerns. Each district should have a dedicated session two weeks later where residents can ask questions specific to their school, their building, and their community's situation. This acknowledges that the three communities have different stakes and prevents the loudest community from dominating a combined forum.
