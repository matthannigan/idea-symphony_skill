# Facility Decisions and Community Impact - The Pragmatist

---

## Facility Cost Modeling and the Repurpose-Vs-Dispose Decision Framework

* **Build the cost-per-student matrix first, then layer in liabilities.** A practical approach: create a seven-building spreadsheet with three columns — current cost-per-student-served, deferred maintenance as a percentage of replacement value, and remaining debt obligations. Buildings with maintenance-to-value ratios above 25% and utilization below 60% are strong closure candidates regardless of community sentiment. Run the one-, two-, and three-closure scenarios against a ten-year net present value model, including transition costs. This gives the board a defensible, numbers-first framework before any community conversation begins.

* **Set repurposing break-even conditions before announcing any scenarios.** A repurposed building that sits half-empty is worse than a sale: it carries ongoing utility, insurance, and staffing costs without generating the tax base a private buyer would. A practical break-even threshold: repurposing should cover at least 70% of ongoing carrying costs within three years through lease revenue, grants, or shared-service agreements. If a community center, early childhood program, or remote-work hub can't demonstrate a credible revenue model reaching that threshold, the district should not carry the asset. Present this threshold publicly upfront — it removes the appearance of favoritism when individual buildings are evaluated.

* **Treat the $4.2M deferred maintenance as the deciding variable.** Districts often agonize over which buildings have community support, but the deferred maintenance backlog is the clearest forcing function. Buildings that require immediate capital infusion simply cannot be carried without a dedicated funding source. A minimum-viable approach: commission rapid facility assessments (not the full feasibility study) on each building within 90 days, rank by maintenance urgency, and close or mothball the highest-liability buildings in Year 1 of consolidation before the structural deficit compounds further.

* **Model the "partial repurpose" option explicitly.** Rather than a binary keep-or-close decision, consider whether a building could be divided: retain two classrooms for early childhood programming, lease the gymnasium to a fitness cooperative, and sell or demolish the aging wing with the highest maintenance liability. This reduces carrying costs while preserving some community use. The break-even math changes substantially when you're not trying to fill an entire building.

## Measuring Community Economic Dependence and Durable Closure Opposition

* **Conduct a quick economic-function audit of each building before the feasibility study finalizes.** Spend two weeks surveying each community: How many people does the school directly employ who live in that specific town? What community events use the building annually, and what would they cost to host elsewhere? Is there a childcare or Head Start program in the building? This data — collected cheaply via a one-page survey and a few school board interviews — produces a rough "community dependence index" for each of the seven buildings. It costs less than a consultant's rounding error and gives the board defensible data rather than impressions.

* **Sequence closures to avoid the "first domino" problem.** A practical approach: close the building with the weakest community dependence score first, and ensure that closure delivers visible benefit — a new program at the receiving school, a community center announcement — before the second closure is announced. If all closures happen simultaneously or in rapid succession, every community feels it lost before consolidation has proven itself. Spreading closures over Years 2 through 4, with Year 1 focused only on administrative consolidation, gives the new district time to demonstrate value before the hardest conversations.

* **Use the economic dependence data to differentiate the coalition strategy.** Communities with high school dependence — where the school is the largest employer and the gymnasium is the only public gathering space — need a different pitch than communities where the school is primarily symbolic. To make this feasible, identify the one or two towns where closing the school building would be genuinely destabilizing, and design around those buildings first. For towns where the economic footprint is modest, a closure with good process (archiving, naming ceremonies, genuine input on reuse) may be achievable without fracturing the vote.

* **Flag the "hardened no" risk explicitly in the feasibility study.** The feasibility study should model the vote-failure scenario as a financial outcome: what does the district look like in Year 5 if consolidation fails and the smallest district reaches insolvency? That number — concrete, specific, undeniable — is more useful for building coalition than abstract appeals to community benefit. Give fiscal conservatives a number they can defend.

## Dignified Transitions and the Simultaneous Gain/Loss for Students

* **Define "dignified closure" as a checklist, not a sentiment.** A practical dignified-closure process includes: a community input session on reuse (with a real decision, not theater), a formal archival project documenting the school's history (photos, yearbooks, athletic records digitized and donated to the county library), a naming opportunity at the receiving school (a wing, a scholarship, an athletic award), and a transition liaison assigned to each family from the closed building for the first academic year. This is implementable in six months per building and costs roughly $15,000–$30,000 — negligible against the savings.

* **Conduct a student-experience audit for each receiving scenario.** Before finalizing which buildings close, map what students in those buildings actually gain and lose. Gains: expanded course offerings, updated lab equipment, more extracurricular options. Losses: bus ride time increase, class size increase, loss of relationships with specific teachers. Present both columns honestly. A practical approach is a structured focus group with 8–12 students per affected building — it takes a day, generates qualitative data the board can cite, and signals to parents that student experience was actually considered rather than assumed to be net-positive.

* **Treat the teacher-relationship loss as a staffing design problem, not just a grief problem.** Students who lose their school often lose relationships with specific adults who know them. A practical mitigation: during union contract reconciliation, give teachers from closed buildings priority placement at the receiving school their students will attend, rather than assigning them by seniority to wherever vacancies exist. This preserves continuity for students while also addressing teacher anxiety about job security. It requires coordination with union negotiators from the start, but it's achievable if flagged as a priority early.

* **Build a Year 1 transition support budget.** The first year after a closure is the highest-risk period for student disengagement and family withdrawal from the district. A practical approach: budget $50,000–$75,000 per cohort of displaced students for a dedicated transition counselor, a parent liaison, and a transportation subsidy for after-school activities. Measure attendance, participation rates, and academic performance against baseline. This creates accountability, generates data for future consolidations, and demonstrates that the district takes the loss seriously.

## Transportation Burden on the Daily Life of Students

* **Run the actual route-time math before any public scenario is discussed.** Transportation analyses often report average ride times, which mask the worst cases. A practical approach: use current enrollment addresses and the seven-building scenarios to calculate the 90th-percentile ride time — the longest ride that 10% of students would experience — for each scenario. A scenario that produces a 90th-percentile ride of 65 minutes is operationally and politically different from one that produces 45 minutes, and that distinction should be in the feasibility study.

* **Design after-school activity access into the transportation plan from day one.** The most concrete harm of extended bus rides is not the ride itself but the downstream effect: students who can't stay after school stop participating in activities, which reduces engagement and makes the "you're gaining opportunities" pitch ring hollow. A practical mitigation: budget for a second late bus run for activity participants in the consolidated district. Rural consolidated districts in Minnesota and Iowa have done this for under $200,000 annually by routing activity buses along existing late-day mail routes. Cost it out; don't assume it's prohibitive.

* **Disaggregate ride times by geography and flag the outliers.** If the consolidated district's geography means that students from a specific township will always face extreme ride times regardless of which building they attend, that is design information — it may indicate that a satellite facility or a K-4 program in that township is cheaper than the political and logistical cost of routing those families through the consolidated school. A practical analysis: identify any geographic cluster where average ride time exceeds 50 minutes in all building scenarios, and model a satellite option for that cluster specifically.

* **Present the transportation cost-benefit tradeoff explicitly.** Extended ride times are not free: they have real costs in driver hours, fuel, and vehicle wear that partially offset the savings from building consolidation. A practical approach is a side-by-side comparison of transportation cost increases against facility cost savings for each closure scenario. If Scenario B saves $400,000 in facility costs but adds $180,000 in transportation costs, the net savings is $220,000 — not $400,000. The feasibility study should make this arithmetic visible rather than treating transportation costs as a separate line.

## School as Civic Anchor

* **Translate the anchor-institution research into a financial reserve requirement.** The non-linear collapse finding from anchor-institution research is not just a sociological observation — it has a financial analog. If closing Building X triggers a 15% property value decline in Millfield Township over five years, the district's tax base from that township shrinks, partially offsetting the building's operating savings. A practical approach: ask the feasibility study consultants to include a property-value sensitivity analysis for each closure scenario. Even a rough estimate — "closing this building could reduce assessed value in that township by $3–8M over ten years" — changes the break-even calculus.

* **Identify which buildings are true anchors and treat them differently.** Not all seven buildings carry equal anchor weight. A practical classification: buildings where the school is the only public institution in the community (no library, no town hall, no post office) are genuine anchors; buildings in towns with multiple civic institutions are not. For true anchors, the civic-infrastructure framing is operationally correct — the district should plan to retain some public function in the building even if educational use ends, because the alternative is subsidizing eventual community collapse through reduced tax revenue.

* **Use the anchor argument to make the case for state and federal co-investment.** If school buildings are civic infrastructure rather than cost centers, they are eligible for funding streams that purely educational facilities are not: rural development grants, community development block grants, USDA rural business development funds. A practical approach is to identify two or three of the seven buildings as anchor-institution candidates before the feasibility study finalizes, and have a grant researcher spend two weeks identifying available funding for each. This is cheaper than carrying the buildings indefinitely and turns a political liability into a coalition-building asset.

## Managing Facility Closure Politics Before They Become the Story

* **Establish a facility decision timeline and publish it before any scenarios leak.** The worst-case outcome is a leaked spreadsheet with a closure list before the board has a public process. A practical prevention: within the first 60 days of consolidation planning, publish a one-page process document that explains: (1) when facility scenarios will be developed, (2) who will develop them, (3) how community input will be collected, and (4) when final decisions will be made. This doesn't constrain the outcome, but it makes a leak less damaging because the community knows a legitimate process exists.

* **Sequence the announcement: options first, then scenarios, then recommendations.** Rather than announcing a list of buildings to close, announce a structured process. First public meeting: "Here are the seven buildings, here are the criteria we will use." Second meeting: "Here are four scenarios we are evaluating, including one that closes no buildings." Third meeting: "Here is our recommendation and the reasoning." This pacing gives communities time to respond constructively rather than reactively, and it makes the board's decision appear earned rather than predetermined.

* **Assign a single public spokesperson for facility communications.** Facility closure politics collapse when board members give inconsistent signals or when consultants brief individual community groups off-record. A practical approach: designate one person — ideally the interim superintendent rather than a consultant — as the sole public spokesperson for facility scenarios. All community inquiries are routed through that person. This doesn't prevent leaks, but it reduces the number of informal channels through which distorted information travels.

* **Pre-brief the mayors and town supervisors of affected communities before any public announcement.** A 30-minute private briefing for the elected official of each affected town, 48 hours before a public announcement, costs almost nothing and buys significant goodwill. It signals respect for local governance, gives local leaders time to prepare a response rather than appear blindsided, and often surfaces local concerns that the district can address before they become public grievances.

## The Slow Fade After the Flag Comes Down

* **Commission a rural decline economic model as part of the feasibility study.** The predictable post-closure sequence — diner closes, listings age, young families stop arriving — is measurable. A practical approach: identify two or three rural communities in the state that lost their last school building in the past decade and gather economic data: assessed property values, business license counts, population change, and school enrollment change in the receiving district. Use these as a calibration dataset for projecting what Millfield Township looks like in Year 5. This costs a graduate student two months of work and produces a concrete projection the board can act on.

* **Design the repurposing announcement to land before or simultaneously with the closure announcement.** The "slow fade" begins when a community loses its anchor and receives nothing in return. A practical approach: before announcing any building closure, have a signed MOU with a partner organization for the building's future use — early childhood program, community health clinic, shared-service hub. The announcement is then "we are closing the K-12 school AND opening a community learning center in the same building," not "we are closing the school." This is not spin; it requires genuine prework to identify viable partners, but that work is achievable in three to six months.

* **Build a community economic vitality fund as a consolidation benefit.** A practical approach: direct a portion of the Year 1 administrative savings — say, $150,000 of the projected $1.2M — into a rural vitality grant fund administered jointly by the new district and the county. Communities that "gave up" their school building are eligible to apply. This is concrete, budgetable, and gives fiscal conservatives a story about savings being reinvested rather than absorbed into administrative overhead. It doesn't arrest decline, but it interrupts the narrative that consolidation is pure extraction.

* **Monitor leading indicators, not just enrollment, in the five years following closure.** The feasibility study should include a Year 5 monitoring plan that tracks not only student enrollment but: assessed property values in affected townships, business license counts, and after-school participation rates at the consolidated school. This creates accountability and generates early-warning data if the slow-fade sequence is beginning. It also demonstrates to skeptical communities that the board takes long-term community impact seriously, not just the Year 1 savings number.

## Beyond the School Building

* **Start with one pilot building, not a system-wide redesign.** The multi-generational learning hub concept is compelling but operationally complex: it requires partnerships with health systems, broadband providers, employers, and local government, each of which has its own contracting and liability requirements. A practical minimum-viable version: identify the one building most likely to close and least politically contested, and develop a detailed partnership plan for that building only. Secure one health clinic tenant, one remote-work operator, and one community programming agreement before the concept goes public. A working pilot is more persuasive than a conceptual framework.

* **Identify the funding sources before the concept is announced.** "Multi-generational learning hub" is an aspirational reframe, but buildings cost money to retrofit and operate. A practical approach: spend four weeks before any public announcement mapping available funding — USDA rural development, HHS rural health grants, economic development administration funds, state rural school assistance programs. If the retrofit and first-year operating costs can be funded 60–70% by external sources, the concept becomes financeable; if the district would carry the full cost, it's a liability, not an innovation.

* **Use the concept to change the voter coalition math.** The "school as civic institution" reframe has a specific political utility: it gives the consolidation vote a constituency beyond parents of school-age children. A practical approach: if one building will become a health clinic and community center, the patients of that clinic and the residents who use the community center become consolidation supporters who don't currently have a stake in the vote. Expanding the coalition beyond school families — toward retirees, small business owners, health-system employees — is operationally achievable if the programming partnerships are real and credible by the time of the vote.

* **Resolve the governance question before anything else.** Multi-use civic buildings sit at the intersection of school governance, municipal authority, and potentially nonprofit or health-system management. A practical first step: identify which entity owns the building after consolidation, which entity operates the non-educational programs, and how liability is allocated. A shared-use agreement template exists in most states' rural development toolkits and can be adapted in 60–90 days. Without a governance resolution, the concept stalls at the partnership stage regardless of community enthusiasm.

## The Hospital Merger Playbook

* **Apply the hospital merger's "service-line analysis" to schools.** Hospital consolidation consultants focus on which services each facility is uniquely positioned to deliver, rather than treating all facilities as equivalent. A practical translation: map which of the seven school buildings has a specialized asset — a large gymnasium, a vocational shop, a performing arts facility — that the consolidated district would lose entirely if the building closes. These assets are analogous to hospital service lines: closing them may save operating costs but creates a service gap that's expensive to rebuild. They should be treated as factors in the building retention decision, not as afterthoughts.

* **Take the "town identity as magical thinking" test seriously, but define a measurable threshold.** Hospital merger experience shows that "community identity" arguments can be either a genuine signal about economic viability or a reflexive political response. A practical test: if a community's enrollment in the current district has declined more than 30% in the past decade and its assessed property values are also declining, the identity argument is likely a grief response to an inevitable transition — design for dignity, not preservation. If enrollment is stable or growing and property values are holding, the identity argument may reflect genuine economic vitality worth designing around.

* **Borrow the hospital merger's "critical access" designation logic.** The federal critical access hospital program identifies rural facilities that cannot be allowed to close because the geographic isolation creates genuine care-access problems. A parallel logic applies to school buildings: if a facility serves students who would face more than 60-minute bus rides to the next nearest building, it qualifies as a geographic access necessity regardless of enrollment or cost. Identify any of the seven buildings that meet this threshold early, protect them from the standard cost-per-student analysis, and communicate that protection publicly — it demonstrates that the district is not applying a purely financial lens.

* **Use the hospital merger literature to anticipate the second-order failures.** Hospital consolidations frequently produce unexpected downstream costs: increased emergency transport distances, primary care deserts in the closed facility's catchment area, and reduced workforce retention as employees leave communities with fewer services. A practical approach: ask the feasibility study team to explicitly catalogue second-order risks for each closure scenario — not just immediate operating savings but what services, employers, and community functions depend on the school building remaining operational. This is standard due diligence in hospital mergers and is largely absent from school consolidation feasibility studies.
