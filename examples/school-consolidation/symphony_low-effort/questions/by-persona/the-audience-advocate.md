# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## Children and Families Facing Disruption

1. **Student transition experience**: For the roughly 1,800 students who would live through a consolidation, what does the emotional journey actually look like — and at which specific transition points (school reassignment notifications, first day at a new building, loss of familiar teachers) are children most likely to feel abandoned rather than served?

2. **Families with barriers to participation**: The consolidation vote requires approval across all three districts, but which families are least likely to attend public hearings or town halls — those with evening work shifts, no childcare, limited English, or disabilities — and how would a process designed around accessible participation differ from the standard approach?

3. **Children in unstable circumstances**: How would consolidation affect students who are already navigating instability — foster care placements, families experiencing housing insecurity, or kids with IEPs requiring consistent staff relationships — and what safeguards would prevent consolidation from amplifying existing vulnerabilities for these children?

4. **Transportation burden on students**: The REQUEST notes transportation logistics as a key consideration, but from a student's daily-life perspective, what does a materially longer bus ride mean for participation in after-school activities, family dinner time, sleep, and the invisible social costs of arriving exhausted — and how are these burdens distributed unevenly across the geography?

---

## Community Identity and the Meaning of "Our School"

5. **What "our school is our town" actually means**: The REQUEST names "our school IS our town" resistance as a stakeholder dynamic, but what specifically do community members mean by this — shared identity, a physical gathering place, local employment, a sense of continuity across generations — and how do those distinct meanings require different responses in the consolidation design?

6. **Whose identity is centered in resistance**: When advocates argue for preserving district identity, which community members' sense of belonging are they speaking for, and are there residents — newer arrivals, renters, families from historically marginalized groups — whose relationship to "the school" is different and whose voice is absent from that framing?

7. **Designing dignified transitions**: If a building must close, what would it mean to close it in a way that honors the community's history rather than erases it — and what concrete design choices (naming, repurposing, community input on reuse) would distinguish a dignified transition from an institutional imposition?

---

## Equity Across Three Unequal Districts

8. **Tax base disparities and family experience**: The REQUEST references property tax equalization across districts with different tax bases — but from the perspective of families in the district with the weakest tax base, what has underfunding already cost their children in program quality, facility condition, and teacher retention, and how should those pre-existing inequities shape what consolidation is obligated to fix?

9. **Union contract reconciliation and staff experience**: Three separate union contracts must be reconciled, but what do teachers and support staff — particularly those in the smallest, most financially precarious district — actually fear about consolidation, and how would a process designed to surface those fears early differ from one that treats contract reconciliation as a back-room administrative task?

10. **Students who experience both benefit and loss**: For students in underserved buildings who gain access to better programs through consolidation, what is lost simultaneously — proximity to school, relationships with familiar adults, participation in a smaller community — and how should the design weigh those simultaneous gains and losses rather than treating consolidation as an unambiguous upgrade?

---

## Voice, Agency, and Democratic Process

11. **Who shapes the feasibility study framing**: The county board has commissioned a feasibility study, but whose questions and concerns are driving that study's scope — and what would it look like to co-design the study's questions with representative community members, including students, rather than presenting findings to them after the fact?

12. **Voter approval as a proxy for community consent**: The consolidation requires voter approval in all three districts, but a vote aggregates community sentiment without capturing the concerns of those most affected — students, non-property-owning families, people who don't vote. What processes beyond the ballot would create genuine accountability to the communities being reorganized?

13. **Communicating across literacy and language differences**: What assumptions does the official consolidation communication strategy make about how families receive and process information — and what would genuinely accessible communication look like for households where reading-level documents, English-only materials, or formal meeting formats create real barriers to informed participation?
