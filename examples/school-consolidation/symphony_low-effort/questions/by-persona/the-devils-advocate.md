# Brainstorming Questions from The Devil's Advocate: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## The Savings Mirage: Are the Financial Assumptions Sound?

1. **Promised savings vs. transition costs**: The $1.2M in annual administrative savings assumes clean elimination of duplicate roles, but what are the one-time costs of severance, contract buyouts, legal fees, and systems integration — and how many years will it take before the consolidated district actually breaks even on the upfront investment?

2. **Property tax equalization exposure**: If the three districts currently have different tax bases and mill rates, consolidation requires harmonizing them — what if equalization raises taxes in the wealthier district enough to trigger voter rejection, even among residents who support consolidation in principle?

3. **Deferred maintenance as a liability transfer**: The $4.2M in deferred building maintenance doesn't disappear upon consolidation — it gets inherited by the new entity. Have the projections accounted for this liability, and could facility decisions forced by consolidation trigger bond obligations or environmental remediation costs that dwarf the projected savings?

---

## The Enrollment Decline Trap: Does Consolidation Solve the Right Problem?

4. **Treating the symptom, not the cause**: Consolidation addresses the fiscal strain of declining enrollment, but what evidence exists that it slows or reverses that decline — and could closing schools in smaller towns actually accelerate outmigration by removing one of the last anchors keeping young families in the county?

5. **Per-pupil funding floor and future decline**: The 12% per-pupil funding increase assumes enrollment stabilizes, but projections show another 15% decline by 2035 — at what enrollment level does the new per-pupil allocation fail to offset the fixed costs of running a larger unified district, and has anyone modeled that threshold explicitly?

---

## The Voter Approval Gauntlet: What Could Sink the Vote?

6. **The asymmetric veto problem**: Consolidation requires approval in all three districts, meaning the smallest and most resistant community can block the entire effort — what is the plan if two districts vote yes and one votes no, and is there a fallback that achieves partial savings without full consolidation?

7. **Coalition fragility under facility closure decisions**: Any promise made to preserve a particular school building to win votes in that town becomes a constraint on the consolidated district's ability to achieve actual savings — how does the planning process avoid making contradictory commitments to different communities that undermine both the vote and the post-consolidation governance?

---

## Union Contract Reconciliation: The Hidden Schedule Risk

8. **The highest-contract-wins dynamic**: When three union contracts are reconciled, there is strong pressure to harmonize upward to the most favorable terms — how much of the projected $1.2M in administrative savings gets eroded if teacher compensation, benefits, and seniority provisions from the most generous existing contract become the floor for the unified agreement?

9. **Seniority and staffing displacement**: Merging seniority lists from three districts creates winners and losers among existing staff — what is the risk that the reconciliation process triggers grievances, early retirements, or voluntary departures among the most experienced teachers at precisely the moment the new district needs stability?

---

## Governance Design: Who Controls What After Day One?

10. **Board composition and town representation**: A unified board elected at-large from a combined district risks leaving the smallest community with no board representation — and a board structured to guarantee geographic representation may produce gridlock on the facility closure decisions that drove consolidation in the first place. How is this tension resolved in a governance design that is both equitable and functional?
