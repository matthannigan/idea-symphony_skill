# Brainstorming Questions from The Politician: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## Coalition Architecture and Voter Math

1. **Who are your three swing-district anchors**: Each district requires a majority vote for consolidation to pass — which local figures (school board members, selectpersons, union chapter leaders, prominent business owners) carry enough trust in each community to serve as visible champions, and what would each of them need to see in the proposal before lending their name to it?

2. **Sequencing the endorsement cascade**: If fiscal conservatives in the smallest, most-insolvent district are most likely to vote yes first, how do you use an early win there to generate credibility and momentum in the two more identity-resistant districts — and what specific message does a "yes" in District 3 send to voters in Districts 1 and 2?

3. **Converting the teachers' unions from opponent to neutral or ally**: Three separate union contracts must be reconciled, and union leadership can mobilize member opposition or silence it — what protections around seniority, salary floors, and job security would need to be written into the consolidation agreement to shift each union's public posture from opposition to at least non-interference?

---

## Opposition Mapping and Neutralization

4. **Anatomy of the "our school is our town" veto**: The town-identity bloc is named as a key resistance point, but it is not monolithic — which towns have the strongest identity attachment to their school building as a civic anchor, who are the informal leaders of that sentiment, and what is the difference between principled cultural resistance (which needs to be honored structurally) versus activated anxiety about facility closure (which can be addressed with concrete commitments)?

5. **Managing the facility closure politics before they become the story**: The decision about which of the seven buildings to close or repurpose will generate the most intense local opposition — at what point in the process should facility scenarios be made public, who should own that announcement, and how do you prevent a leaked closure list from collapsing the coalition before the vote?

6. **Fiscal conservative activation risk**: If the $4.2M deferred maintenance backlog and structural deficits become the dominant narrative, voters may conflate "consolidation" with "austerity" — how do you ensure the financial case for consolidation reads as a path to stability rather than a managed decline, and who are the credible messengers for that frame in each community?

---

## Governance Design as a Trust Mechanism

7. **Board composition as a coalition-stabilizing instrument**: The new unified board's structure — seat allocation by former district geography, at-large versus district seats, staggered terms — will signal to each community whether consolidation means absorption or genuine merger. What board design gives smaller communities enough formal representation to feel protected without creating a veto structure that makes governance unworkable?

8. **Binding commitments vs. aspirational promises**: Voter approval will likely require some communities to accept facility uncertainty they find uncomfortable — what decisions can realistically be locked into the consolidation agreement or the new board's founding charter (e.g., a 5-year moratorium on closing the smallest district's school), and which commitments, if made, would be politically necessary but institutionally unenforceable?

---

## Process Design and Timing

9. **Sequencing the feasibility study release against the political calendar**: The county board has commissioned a feasibility study, and its release will set the terms of public debate — who should present the findings, in what venue, and in what order across the three communities to maximize the chance that the first public framing is "shared opportunity" rather than "forced merger"?
