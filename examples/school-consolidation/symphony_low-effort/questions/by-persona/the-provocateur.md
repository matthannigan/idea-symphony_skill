# Brainstorming Questions from The Provocateur: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## What If the Crisis Is the Cure

1. **Deliberate insolvency as leverage**: What if the smallest district's looming insolvency — two years of fund balance remaining — is actually the most powerful consolidation argument available, and the county is squandering it by framing consolidation as a technocratic process rather than a rescue? What happens to the political calculus if the community watches one district actually collapse first, and how does that reframe the vote in the surviving two?

2. **Making it deliberately worse before the vote**: Imagine the county *froze* all deferred maintenance spending, published a vivid 10-year facility decay report, and let every parent see a photo of their child's school at year seven. What assumptions about "protecting community identity" would that crack open — and what does the willingness to preserve comfortable denial over honest collapse reveal about whose interests the current structure actually serves?

---

## Inverting Who Owns the Problem

3. **The towns are the product, not the schools**: Here's something absurd, but stay with it — what if the school districts aren't failing because of enrollment decline, but enrollment is declining *because* the districts have been quietly failing for a decade, and the county has been treating the symptom (fiscal stress) while the cause (educational quality erosion) drives families away? If that's even 30% true, what would a consolidation plan look like that led with academic improvement rather than administrative efficiency?

4. **Voters as the obstacle vs. voters as the designers**: The current process ends in a voter approval requirement that everyone treats as a hurdle to clear. What if the process instead began with voters — what if all three communities co-designed the consolidated district structure before any feasibility study concluded? The absurd version: hold the vote *first*, approve the principle of consolidation, then figure out the details. The real insight: what does treating voters as approvers rather than authors reveal about why consolidation campaigns routinely fail?

---

## Collisions with Unrelated Domains

5. **The hospital merger playbook**: Rural hospital consolidations have killed towns and saved them — sometimes the same town. What does the consolidation literature from rural healthcare mergers reveal about the moment when a "town identity" argument becomes magical thinking versus a legitimate preservation signal worth designing around? What assumptions in the school consolidation framing would a hospital administrator immediately identify as naive?

---

## Pushing Community Identity to Its Logical Extreme

6. **If the school truly IS the town, what happens to the town when the school closes anyway**: The "our school is our town" advocates are almost certainly right — rural community identity does concentrate in the school building. So push that premise to its conclusion: if the smallest district goes insolvent in two years and its school closes *without* consolidation, what is the community identity argument worth at that point? What would it mean to design a consolidation that explicitly preserves the school building as a community asset — a civic center, a branch campus, a shared facility — while dissolving the administrative district, and does that dissolve the identity objection or only displace it?

7. **Winning the vote in the district that loses the most**: Consolidation math almost certainly means one of the three towns loses its high school. Imagine the county decided to hold the vote in that town *first*, as a signal of respect rather than a calculation of political sequencing. What does that reveal about every assumption baked into the current coalition-building strategy — and what would it cost to actually try it?
