# Brainstorming Questions from The Storyteller: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## The Last Day the Lights Were On

1. **When "our school" disappears**: Imagine Maria, a grandmother in the smallest district, who graduated from that building, watched her children graduate there, and now volunteers in the library every Tuesday — what happens to her sense of belonging, and to the dozens of informal community rituals anchored to the school building, the morning a closure announcement is made? How should the consolidation process honor and grieve these losses rather than treating them as mere sentiment to overcome?

2. **The student who falls through the cracks**: Walk through the day of a 10th grader who lives fifteen miles from where the nearest consolidated school would be located — her bus ride doubles, she misses tryouts, her after-school job becomes impossible to reach, and her parents can't afford a second car. What specific transportation, scheduling, and support-structure decisions during consolidation planning would prevent her story from becoming the norm, and how do you surface her experience before the vote rather than discovering the failure afterward?

---

## A Town Without Its School

3. **The slow fade after the flag comes down**: In communities that have lost their last school building, researchers document a predictable sequence — the diner closes, the realtor's listings age longer, young families stop moving in. Picture Millfield Township five years after its K-12 building is mothballed by the new unified district. What economic and social ripple effects should the feasibility study model beyond the direct operating-cost math, and what consolidation design choices — repurposing the building, hosting community programming there, naming the new district's performing arts wing after the town — might interrupt that spiral?

4. **The town that kept its identity anyway**: Somewhere, a small community went through a consolidation and found that its sense of place not only survived but was renewed — maybe because the building became a community hub, or because the new district created a named "campus" that kept local sports rivalries alive. What would that story look like for each of the three districts here, and what specific governance and facility decisions would need to be made to allow that story to become possible?

---

## The Teachers Who Stayed and the Teachers Who Left

5. **Three contracts, three career arcs**: Picture an experienced teacher nearing retirement who has built her identity around being "a Millfield teacher" for twenty years — her union contract, her department head title, her relationship with the principal are all wrapped up in that identity. Now picture a young teacher in year three who desperately wants professional development opportunities and a department large enough to offer real mentorship. How does the contract reconciliation process speak to both of them in a way that earns trust rather than breeding resentment, and what storytelling does district leadership need to do in public meetings to make both teachers feel seen?

---

## What the Feasibility Study Can't Capture

6. **The moment the vote swings**: Consolidation campaigns in rural communities often turn not on spreadsheets but on a single public meeting where a trusted voice — a longtime coach, a local pastor, a respected retired principal — either endorses or condemns the plan. Who are those voices in each of the three districts, what would it take for them to become genuine champions of a well-designed consolidation rather than opponents or reluctant neutrals, and what does the engagement process need to look like to reach them before the opposition does?

7. **The children who aren't in the room**: The students most affected by fiscal collapse — those who would face dwindling course offerings, crumbling facilities, and revolving-door teachers in a financially insolvent district — have no vote and often no voice at public hearings. Imagine a future where one of these students, now an adult, looks back on the choice the community made in 2026. What does she say happened, and what does that reveal about which outcomes the planning process should be optimizing for when stakeholder pressure focuses attention on the loudest adult voices?
