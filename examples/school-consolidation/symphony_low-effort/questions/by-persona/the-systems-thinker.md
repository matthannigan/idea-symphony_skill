# Brainstorming Questions from The Systems Thinker: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## Enrollment Decline as a Self-Amplifying Spiral

1. **The enrollment-quality doom loop**: As enrollment falls and budgets tighten, program cuts follow — fewer AP courses, fewer extracurriculars, fewer specialized teachers — which makes the district less attractive to families with options, accelerating the outmigration that caused the cuts in the first place. At what point does consolidation interrupt this compounding dynamic versus simply delaying it, and what would a consolidated district need to do structurally to reverse the spiral rather than just slow it?

2. **When the fix becomes the problem**: If consolidation is adopted primarily as a fiscal rescue for the most distressed district, it may relieve that district's immediate pressure while creating a governance body whose first act is associated with closure and job loss — poisoning the political well for the harder decisions that follow. How can the consolidation be designed so the short-term financial relief does not consume the political capital needed to make the long-term structural reforms that actually produce sustainability?

3. **Projecting the second wave of decline**: The REQUEST notes a projected additional 15% enrollment decline by 2035. A consolidated district optimized for 1,800 students will face the same facility and staffing overcapacity problem at 1,530. What enrollment floor should the consolidated district be designed around, and what governance triggers or decision rules would allow it to adapt to further decline without requiring another political crisis?

---

## Facility Decisions and Community Identity Feedback

4. **Which school closure creates the most durable opposition**: Closing a school building does not just reduce costs — it permanently alters the symbolic relationship between a community and its district, potentially hardening a "no" vote in that community on every future bond or levy. How should the facility decision sequence be ordered to minimize the number of communities that feel they lost before the consolidation has delivered any visible benefit?

5. **The community identity stabilizer**: "Our school is our town" is often treated as sentiment to overcome, but it also functions as a built-in governor on how fast change can happen. Rather than treating identity attachment as pure resistance, what elements of school-community identity could be preserved or transformed within a consolidated structure — and what is the minimum preservation necessary to keep each community's coalition on board?

6. **Deferred maintenance as a hidden time bomb**: The $4.2M in deferred building maintenance is not a sunk cost — it is a liability that will force facility decisions whether or not consolidation happens. How does the deferred maintenance figure change the range of viable facility scenarios, and which buildings' maintenance trajectories would effectively force closure decisions within the first five years regardless of what the consolidation plan specifies today?

---

## Governance Structure and Political Sustainability

7. **Designing a board that doesn't reproduce the old conflicts**: A unified board elected at-large from across the county could systematically underrepresent the smallest community if voting patterns follow population; geographic representation guarantees each community a seat but risks gridlock on the facility decisions that require majority votes. What governance structure creates enough representation legitimacy to survive a contested facility closure vote while still being able to make decisions?

8. **The three-district voter approval requirement as a constraint and a signal**: Requiring voter approval in all three districts means a single community can veto consolidation — which means the plan must be viable for the most skeptical community, not just the average voter. What does the analysis need to demonstrate specifically for the community with the strongest identity resistance and the least fiscal distress, and how does designing for that community's "yes" change the overall consolidation structure?

---

## Union Contract Reconciliation and Staffing Dynamics

9. **Reconciling three contracts without triggering a work-to-rule campaign**: When three teacher contracts are merged, someone will end up with a less favorable outcome on salary schedules, benefits, or working conditions — and the teachers who lose ground have both the incentive and the means to make the transition visibly painful. What sequence and process for contract reconciliation minimizes the number of teachers who feel they lost ground, and what role does the $1.2M in administrative savings play in funding compensation harmonization that keeps the teaching staff neutral or supportive?

10. **Administrative reduction as a one-time gain with permanent political cost**: Eliminating two superintendent positions and consolidating HR and transportation saves $1.2M annually, but those administrators are also among the most visible community leaders in small towns. Their departure is not just a headcount reduction — it removes people who have historically been advocates for school funding in their communities. How can the consolidated district maintain community advocacy capacity after eliminating the administrative roles that previously provided it?

---

## Long-Term Fiscal Sustainability Under Continued Decline

11. **The per-pupil funding increase is structurally fragile**: The 12% increase in per-pupil allocation from the state funding formula is real money today, but it depends on a formula that legislators can modify. A consolidated district that builds its operating budget around this increase is exposed if the state recalibrates the formula in response to statewide fiscal pressure. How should the budget model treat the formula improvement — as reliable baseline revenue or as a margin of safety that buys time for structural cost reduction?

12. **Transition costs as a test of fiscal resolve**: Consolidation will require upfront investment — legal fees, technology integration, facilities assessment, staff transition costs — before any savings materialize. If the smallest district has only a two-year fund balance, the timing of when transition costs hit versus when savings arrive determines whether consolidation can be funded without a bridge borrowing. What is the realistic cash flow profile of the first three years, and does the county have the fiscal mechanism to cover any gap without requiring an emergency levy that reopens the voter approval question?
