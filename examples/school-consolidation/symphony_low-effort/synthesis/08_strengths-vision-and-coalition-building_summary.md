# Summary: Strengths, Vision, and Coalition-Building

## Executive Summary

The central tension in this cluster is not whether the three districts have real strengths to build on — both lenses agree they do — but whether those strengths can survive the transition at all. A feasibility-oriented case argues that loyal staff, documented cross-district programs with measurable outcomes, and existing social connectors are concrete assets the consolidation can be designed around. Survey long-tenured teachers, quantify what past collaborations actually delivered, and build the unified district's governance structure on what demonstrably functions. A risk-oriented counterpoint flags that each of these same assets may be strength only under conditions consolidation will destroy. Staff loyalty to small-district culture may be loyalty to autonomy, direct principal access, and knowing every student by name—precisely the things a 1,800-student unified district threatens unless site-level governance is locked in structurally, not merely promised in campaign materials. This is the inverted-framing pair at the heart of the cluster: the feasibility lens reads loyal staff and past collaboration as proof of consolidation capacity. The risk lens reads the same evidence as proof of what consolidation can lose if the design is careless.

Both lenses converge on three high-confidence conclusions: the $1.2M in administrative savings must be formally earmarked by board policy, before the vote, rather than left as an abstract promise. Transition costs and a worst-case fiscal scenario must be modeled transparently before the vote, not after. The path to winning skeptics runs through retained community identity at the building level (local principal authority, school-specific culture), not through programmatic optimism alone. Where they diverge most sharply is on coalition strategy. A feasibility-oriented approach identifies trusted internal connectors by function ("who do you call when you need something done in another district?") and mobilizes business owners as an underused credibility anchor. A risk-oriented counterpoint warns that obvious cross-community bridges may be dismissed as regionalists in high-identity towns, and that the most persuasive validators are respected internal skeptics who conclude publicly and reluctantly that consolidation is necessary—a different and harder recruitment task.

## Key Themes

### Build on What Is Already Documented, Not What Is Remembered Selectively

Both lenses flag the danger of treating "peak collaboration" stories as proof of durable capacity without examining what actually made them work. A feasibility-oriented approach recommends auditing past cross-district programs for traceable outcomes (enrollment numbers, cost-per-student, teacher retention in the program) and building the consolidation case around the most quantifiable successes. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues the county board should publish a complete inventory of both successes and failures before the vote. Selective memory on either side will erode trust once the real negotiations begin.

### The $1.2M Savings Story Is Only Credible With a Structural Earmark and an Honest Transition Bridge

Both lenses converge on this as the highest-confidence insight in the cluster. Framing savings as going to classrooms is aspirational until a board-adopted policy creates a named Student Opportunity Fund with specific program allocations published before the vote. That policy locks savings in before they enter the general fund. The feasibility lens adds that voters need a plain-language one-page document showing Year 1–2 transition costs, break-even year, and Year 4+ net positive cash flow. The risk lens emphasizes that without this, the worst-case transition scenario (budget crisis, early facility closures, mid-year layoffs) can poison community trust permanently before savings ever materialize.

### Coalition Anchors Are Already Present But Require a Different Recruitment Strategy Than Expected

Both lenses agree the most effective coalition validators are not enthusiastic cross-community cheerleaders but trusted internal figures with earned social capital. They diverge on how to find and activate them. A feasibility-oriented approach recommends asking board members and principals the single question "who do you call when you need something done in another district?" and then investing in those people. A risk-oriented counterpoint emphasizes identifying respected opponents in each community (people dismissing consolidation) and creating structured opportunities for them to engage with the financial evidence in small-group sessions rather than town halls designed to persuade. Both views agree that each district needs a distinct message and messenger. A unified campaign that ignores the structural veto held by the most resistant district can win two votes and lose the one that matters.

### Pre-Vote Transparency on Hard Questions Outperforms Pre-Vote Optimism

Both lenses converge on this point. A risk-oriented view warns that a poorly executed pilot program (underfunded, administratively chaotic, dependent on three separate district offices cooperating smoothly) is worse than no pilot. A feasibility-oriented view agrees the small win should be a single visible joint program, scoped tightly enough to execute flawlessly. Add a distinct recommendation: hold one public session in each town specifically to answer "what happens to our school building?" and "what happens to our teachers?" with specific, honest answers. Voters who receive a straight answer are more persuadable than voters who feel they are being sold a vision.

### Retained Building Identity Is the Actual Mechanism for Converting Skeptics

Both lenses converge here. Research on consolidation (raised by the risk lens) and case study patterns (raised by the feasibility lens) both point to the same finding: community opposition softens most when skeptics discover their local school still feels local. The principal knows the kids, the school mascot survived, the fall festival still happens. This means the 2031 success story depends on a specific governance commitment made in 2026: unified district administration, retained building identities, principals with real local authority. That commitment, codified as a board resolution before the vote, is the single design decision most likely to move the "our school is our town" voter.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)

- Commission a complete inventory of all cross-district cooperation attempts in the past decade (successes and documented failures) and publish it before the vote. Include specific outcome data where available (participation counts, per-student cost differentials, program longevity). [convergent]
- Host one unstructured listening session in each community with no agenda and no presentations — board members hear grievances only. Begin coalition credibility with demonstrated listening before demonstrated persuasion. [unique: risk]
- Ask board members and principals in each district one question: "Who do you call when you need something done in another district?" Map those names; they are your coalition anchors. [unique: feasibility]
- Conduct 30-minute structured interviews with 12–15 long-tenured teachers across all three districts to identify which elements of small-district culture they would protect versus trade for better resources. Use findings directly in unified district governance design. [unique: feasibility]

### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Draft a board-adopted Student Opportunity Fund policy that earmarks the $1.2M annual administrative savings for specific named programs (AP course expansion, counselor hire, CTE community college partnership) before those savings enter the general fund. Publish line-item allocations before the vote. [convergent]
- Publish a plain-language one-page transition document: Year 1–2 costs (severance, IT integration, legal), break-even year, Year 4+ net cash flow, and a realistic worst-case scenario. [convergent]
- Launch one joint elective or program requiring students from at least two districts, scoped tightly enough to execute flawlessly, with logistics managed by an experienced outside coordinator if needed. Ensure local press coverage of the student-facing event. [trade-off: feasibility-oriented case for a months-long shared program; risk-oriented counterpoint that a failed pilot is worse than no pilot — scope accordingly]
- Convene a 90-minute session with 10–15 local employers from all three towns to identify skills gaps and secure at least one joint public endorsement letter signed by one business owner from each town. [unique: feasibility]
- Hold one public session in each town specifically answering "what happens to our school building?" and "what happens to our teachers?" with specific facility scenarios and trade-offs stated plainly, not deferred. [convergent]

### Long-term (1+ years)

- Codify unified-district administration with retained building identities as a board resolution before the vote: named schools, local principal authority, building-level budget discretion for pedagogy and culture. Make it a structural commitment, not a campaign promise. [convergent]
- Build district-specific message and messenger strategies for each community — especially the smallest/most resistant — rather than a single unified campaign. Track voter population composition and identify respected internal skeptics in each community as the highest-value validators. [unique: risk]
- Document governance process decisions in real time from the first planning meeting: meeting minutes with rationale, union negotiation framework, facility decision criteria. This creates replicable templates for other rural counties at minimal cost. [unique: feasibility]
- Track civic participation indicators annually in each legacy community — school board election turnout, volunteer hours, booster club membership — as early-warning signals of disengagement rather than conversion. [unique: risk]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- The $1.2M savings claim is only credible with a formal earmark policy adopted before the vote, not as a campaign promise.
- Transition costs and a realistic worst-case scenario must be modeled and published; the 2031 success story requires surviving the 2026–2028 transition first.
- Retained building identity — local principal authority, school mascot, community events — is the actual mechanism for converting "our school is our town" skeptics; governance design must commit to this structurally.
- Transparency on hard questions (facilities, jobs) moves persuadable voters more than optimistic framing.
- A failed pre-vote pilot is worse than no pilot; scope any joint program to what can be executed flawlessly.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility case for using obvious cross-community connectors as coalition anchors, because they have the broadest existing reach; a risk-oriented counterpoint that those same people may be seen as regionalists or traitors in high-identity towns, making respected internal skeptics — who conclude reluctantly that consolidation is necessary — the higher-value but harder-to-recruit validators.
- A feasibility case for a months-long joint program (shared elective, robotics team) as a sustained pre-vote demonstration. A risk-oriented counterpoint holds that multi-month programs requiring three separate district offices to cooperate carry significant execution risk, and a scoped single event with outside logistics management is safer.
- A feasibility case for building the 2031 prototype story into design from the start (documentation, replicable governance templates). A risk-oriented counterpoint suggests that optimizing for replicability can subtly warp design toward what looks clean on a presentation slide rather than what fits this county's specific political geography.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- The structural veto: all three districts must approve. A coalition strategy must be mapped district by district, with a specific plan for the most resistant community, not a single unified campaign. If the smallest district is also the most identity-protective, its voters may vote against their own fiscal interest. [unique: risk]
- Business owners as an underused coalition anchor: local employers with direct economic stake in workforce development programs can provide a credibility signal (a joint employer letter) faster than any consultant presentation. [unique: feasibility]
- Quantified past collaboration ("shared Program X served 87 students at 40% lower cost per district") as a concrete coalition argument — not "we once worked together" but documented proof. [unique: feasibility]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- Emotional/phenomenological dimension: Neither lens examined what consolidation feels like for students during the transition itself, especially students whose school building closes or whose team identity disappears mid-enrollment. Student voice in the design process (student advisory panels, structured student input on facility and program decisions) is absent from both accounts and may be the most authentic trust-building mechanism available to the board.
- Equity/distributional dimension: Neither lens examined whether the strengths-and-vision process equitably elevates the smallest district's community in coalition building, or whether the most organizationally capable community (likely the largest district) defaults to setting the agenda. The coalition-building strategy should explicitly map whose voices are structurally underrepresented in the planning process, not just in the vote.
