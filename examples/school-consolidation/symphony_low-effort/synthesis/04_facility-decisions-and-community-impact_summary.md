# Summary: Facility Decisions and Community Impact

## Executive Summary

Closing buildings saves the district money that appears on the consolidated ledger, but the surrounding community absorbs costs that do not. The core tension this cluster surfaces is not simply "close or keep" but who captures the savings and who bears the spillover. A feasibility-oriented view holds that buildings with maintenance-to-replacement-value ratios above 25% and utilization below 60% are defensible closure candidates that the board can act on with a numbers-first framework. A risk-oriented view counters that enrollment utilization is the wrong primary metric because a building running at 40% student capacity may still be running at 95% civic-function capacity, and that anchor-institution research documents non-linear (not proportional) economic collapse when that anchor closes. Applying standard cost-per-student analysis uniformly across all seven buildings systematically misprices the ones whose community role has diversified farthest beyond education.

Both lenses converge on two high-confidence points: deferred maintenance liability does not disappear at closure (it converts to demolition costs, remediation liability, or carrying costs on an unsellable asset), and repurposing scenarios require a signed operator commitment before they can count as savings. Where they diverge is on timing and sequencing. A feasibility-oriented view argues for rapid facility assessments within 90 days, closing highest-liability buildings in Year 1 before structural deficits compound. A risk-oriented counterpoint is that sequencing closures ahead of verified savings milestones means the communities that accepted closure first gave up their buildings for a promise that hasn't materialized, and that a poorly executed first closure raises the district's cost of capital for a decade by hardening future bond and levy opposition.

Both lenses also converge, from opposite directions, on communication strategy. A risk-oriented view treats a leaked closure list as near-certain rather than a contingency (the number of people with access makes it so). A feasibility-oriented view prescribes a specific structural antidote: publish a one-page process document within 60 days, sequence public engagement as options to scenarios to recommendation, and designate a single spokesperson. They agree on the problem's severity and the messenger's importance, reaching similar prescriptions through different threat models.

## Key Themes

### Civic Anchor vs. Cost Center: An Inverted Framing

Both lenses treat school buildings as civic anchors, but from opposite directions. A risk-oriented view flags that once the civic anchor argument enters public debate, every community will invoke it symmetrically to block its own closure, making it a de facto veto unless the district pre-defines an evidentiary threshold distinguishing genuine anchor function from sentimental attachment. A feasibility-oriented view argues the anchor framing is operationally useful because it unlocks non-educational funding streams (USDA rural development, community development block grants, rural health grants) that purely educational facilities cannot access, turning a political liability into a coalition asset. Both agree the framing is accurate, but they disagree on whether to lead with it publicly.

### Deferred Maintenance as the Honest Forcing Function

Both lenses converge here: the $4.2M in deferred maintenance is the clearest decision variable the district has, and it should drive the building-rationalization sequence rather than community sentiment. A feasibility-oriented view specifies a practical threshold (buildings whose maintenance liability exceeds 25% of replacement value) and recommends rapid assessments within 90 days to rank by urgency. A risk-oriented view adds that mothballing rather than closing a building does not remove maintenance costs. Instead, it converts them into ongoing insurance, security, and eventual demolition costs. The district should price this carrying cost into every scenario, including the do-nothing baseline that is often presented as costless.

### The Transportation Burden Is Not Distributed Evenly

Both lenses flag that the students who will bear the longest bus rides are almost certainly those whose communities lost their buildings, meaning facility closure and transportation disadvantage stack on the same households. A feasibility-oriented view calls for 90th-percentile ride-time modeling rather than average ride times, and prices the late-bus cost explicitly (comparable rural districts have done it for under $200K annually). A risk-oriented view adds that earlier wake times driven by longer routes have measurable academic consequences (lower performance, higher absenteeism) that will underperform the consolidated district's educational projections, eroding the academic case for consolidation that supporters made to win the vote.

### Dignified Closure as Checklist, Not Aspiration

Both lenses converge that "honoring community history" is meaningless unless operationalized before the vote. A feasibility-oriented view translates it into a concrete, budgetable checklist: community input session with a real decision, formal archival project, naming opportunity at the receiving school, and a transition liaison per family (estimated at $15,000–$30,000 per building). A risk-oriented view agrees on specificity but flags the limits of sequencing: naming a performing arts wing after a closed school is a consolation prize offered after the decision is made, not genuine co-design. Genuine dignity means allowing communities to shape whether and how their building closes, not just what it is called afterward.

### The Slow Fade Is Partially Predictable and Partially Already Underway

A risk-oriented view raises the possibility that for at least one of the three communities, the economic and social spiral the question describes may not require a school closure to begin. It may already be in progress, driven by the same demographic forces shrinking enrollment. A feasibility-oriented view responds with a practical modeling approach: identify two or three communities in the state that lost their last school building in the past decade, gather property values, business license counts, and population data, and use them as a calibration dataset. Both lenses treat the slow-fade sequence as real and measurable, but they differ on how much of it consolidation is causing versus accelerating.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)

- Commission rapid facility assessments on all seven buildings — ranked by deferred maintenance as a percentage of replacement value — before the full feasibility study finalizes. Flag any building above 25% for Year 1 closure or mothball consideration. [convergent]
- Publish a one-page facility decision process document within 60 days: when scenarios will be developed, who develops them, how community input will be collected, and when final decisions will be made. Designate a single public spokesperson — ideally the interim superintendent — before any scenario is discussed publicly. [convergent]
- Require the feasibility study to model the do-nothing baseline (all seven buildings open under continued enrollment decline) alongside consolidation scenarios, so both sides of the cost ledger face the same evidentiary standard. [unique: risk]
- Map employment by home zip code for all seven buildings, identifying which communities suffer net job loss at which income levels if their building closes. This population votes, and the data should inform sequencing before it surfaces in opposition. [unique: risk]

### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Before announcing any building closure, secure a signed MOU with a partner organization for the building's future use. The public announcement should pair the closure and the new use: "closing the K-12 school and opening a community learning center" rather than announcing them sequentially. [convergent]
- Identify any geographic cluster where average ride time exceeds 50 minutes in all building scenarios and model a satellite K-4 option for that cluster. Cost the late-bus run for activity participants explicitly; rural districts in Minnesota and Iowa have done it for under $200K annually by routing along existing late-day mail routes. [trade-off: feasibility-oriented view holds this is affordable and essential; risk-oriented counterpoint is that the consolidated district's financial stress will defer these costs, so they should be locked in as a contractual commitment before the vote, not a budgetary aspiration]
- During union contract reconciliation, give teachers from closed buildings priority placement at the receiving school their students will attend. Coordinate with union negotiators from the outset; this is achievable if flagged as a non-negotiable priority early and loses its value if treated as an afterthought. [convergent]
- Direct $150,000 of Year 1 administrative savings into a rural vitality grant fund, administered jointly by the new district and the county, for which communities that gave up their buildings are eligible to apply. [unique: feasibility]

### Long-term (1+ years)

- Pilot the multi-generational civic hub concept in one building (the one most likely to close and least politically contested) before announcing a system-wide concept. Secure one health tenant, one remote-work operator, and one community programming agreement before the concept goes public. A working pilot is more persuasive than a framework. [trade-off: feasibility-oriented view holds this is achievable in 3–6 months; risk-oriented counterpoint is that multi-use civic buildings require interagency agreements, shared operating budgets, and conflict-resolution mechanisms that add administrative complexity before the district has realized any savings. Resolve governance and liability allocation first.]
- Build a Year 5 monitoring plan that tracks assessed property values in affected townships, business license counts, and after-school participation rates alongside enrollment — not just enrollment. Publish results annually. [convergent]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- Deferred maintenance liability does not disappear at closure; it converts to demolition cost, remediation liability, or carrying cost on an unsellable asset. Every scenario must include a realistic asset-disposal path.
- Repurposing break-even analysis built on hypothetical tenants is not analysis. Require a signed letter of intent from an actual operator before modeling repurposing as a financial benefit.
- A poorly executed first closure hardens opposition to future bonds and levies for a decade. The facility decision sequence should be modeled for its effect on future borrowing capacity, not just direct operating savings.
- "Dignified closure" must be defined as a specific checklist before the vote, not an aspiration stated after the decision is made.
- Communication strategy should treat a premature leak as near-certain; a process document published within 60 days is the primary mitigation.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility-oriented case for rapid Year 1 closures of highest-maintenance buildings before deficits compound further. A risk-oriented counterpoint holds that sequencing closures ahead of verified (not projected) savings milestones asks the first communities to sacrifice on a promise rather than a result. Savings projections in school consolidations are systematically overestimated, as the hospital merger literature documents explicitly.
- A feasibility-oriented case for leading publicly with the civic anchor framing to unlock alternative funding streams and expand the voter coalition beyond school families. A risk-oriented counterpoint holds that surfacing the civic anchor argument before defining its evidentiary threshold gives every community a symmetrical veto argument, making it impossible to close any building.
- A feasibility-oriented case that the transportation late-bus cost is manageable and should be committed in the plan. A risk-oriented counterpoint holds that a financially stressed consolidated district will defer late-bus commitments first, and that students who lose both their building and their extracurricular access experience compound harm that won't appear in the Year 1 budget.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- The do-nothing baseline is not costless. Keeping all seven buildings open under continued enrollment decline carries per-building fixed costs that don't scale with enrollment. Feasibility studies that present only consolidation scenarios treat inaction as neutral when it is not. [unique: risk]
- The "town identity" argument should be tested against measurable thresholds. If enrollment has declined more than 30% in the past decade and assessed property values are also declining, the identity argument likely reflects grief over an inevitable transition, and the design should emphasize dignity rather than preservation. If enrollment is stable and values are holding, the argument may reflect genuine economic vitality worth designing around. [unique: feasibility]
- The hospital merger playbook includes not just cautionary cases but the common middle outcome: a merger that produced modest savings, disrupted community trust, and left successor institutions weaker than expected. This middle outcome is underrepresented in the literature. The feasibility study should trace two or three comparable consolidations at Year 5, not just at merger completion. [unique: risk]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- *Emotional/phenomenological*: Neither lens adequately addresses the experience of students, particularly younger children, transitioning out of a school where they have deep relational roots. The teacher-relationship loss is flagged, but the cumulative disorientation of losing a building, familiar adults, and a smaller community simultaneously during formative years is a distinct harm that deserves a dedicated student-experience framework, not just a transition liaison budget line.
- *Equity/distributional*: Both lenses note that transportation burden falls unevenly, but neither fully reckons with the distributional gap in who benefits from consolidation. Students whose families have schedule flexibility and transportation access will capture the expanded AP courses, better labs, and enrichment programs. Students from lower-income households in closed-school communities often receive those programs on paper but not in practice. The design should explicitly ask which students benefit in practice and what structural mitigation (transportation stipends, on-site tutoring at satellite facilities) changes that distribution.
- *Political-economy/institutional*: The feasibility study is commissioned by the county board, but the downstream fiscal costs of community decline (reduced tax base, increased social services demand, infrastructure depreciation) are absorbed by county, state, and federal budgets, not the school district. Neither lens models who captures the savings and who bears the spillover across jurisdictions. A cross-jurisdictional fiscal analysis would change the closure calculus for the buildings whose community collapse risk is highest.
