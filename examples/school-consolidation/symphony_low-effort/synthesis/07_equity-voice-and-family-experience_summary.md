# Summary: Equity, Voice, and Family Experience

## Executive Summary

The central unresolved tension in this cluster is not whether to include marginalized voices but how deeply. A feasibility-oriented view argues that tiered outreach formats, plain-language materials, and structured community workshops are achievable for under $5,000–$10,000. A risk-oriented counterpoint insists that participation mechanisms designed within the existing political process still pre-select who gets heard. A vote can pass while the families with the greatest stake (renters, non-registered parents, students) were never genuinely in the room. Both lenses agree the official process as currently designed will systematically exclude a meaningful share of affected families. They disagree on whether practical fixes are sufficient or whether structural mechanisms (a community oversight board with real authority, a student representative with speaking rights, an equity review clause in the merger agreement itself) are necessary.

On pre-existing inequities, both lenses converge strongly. The feasibility study must document per-district program gaps (AP course access, facility condition ratings, teacher turnover) as a binding baseline obligation. Without that documentation, the consolidation agreement will be written as if all three districts start from the same place, which benefits the wealthier districts and locks in existing disadvantage under a new name. A feasibility-oriented case frames the $1.2M in projected administrative savings as funding for a three-year step-up plan for the underfunded district's programs. A risk-oriented counterpoint asks whether those same savings have already been claimed against deficits and whether remediation costs were ever modeled as a line item.

On student transitions, both lenses agree that generic "vulnerable students" language is insufficient and that IEP continuity is a legal requirement. A risk-oriented view names the emotional micro-transitions (the week a child learns their familiar counselor is gone, the third new case manager in eighteen months) as moments consolidation planners typically fail to plan for. Any disruption to IEP service delivery exposes the new district to federal legal liability. A feasibility-oriented view translates this into actionable protocols: map the high-risk student population using three existing data sets, identify the highest-risk transition moments by their actual calendar dates, staff them specifically, and set a rule that no IEP reassignment takes effect until the new provider has had at least two in-person meetings with the student and family.

## Key Themes

### Equity Floors Must Be Written Into the Agreement, Not Left to Future Budgets

Both lenses agree that consolidation framed as "averaging" programs across three districts will not fix (and may perpetuate) the program deficits that children in the weakest-resourced district have already absorbed. The feasibility-oriented view recommends specific minimum program guarantees written as binding board-level commitments (at least two AP courses per content area, per-pupil rather than per-school instrument funding). The risk-oriented view adds that union contract reconciliation poses a parallel risk: seniority provisions written without an equity lens will favor teachers from the better-resourced district, replicating old hierarchies in staffing and professional development access. Both agree that if equity is not written into the merger agreement with binding timelines, it will lose to future budget cycles where historically advantaged advocates will dominate.

### Participation Mechanisms Must Reach the Families the Ballot Cannot

Both lenses flag the town hall format as structurally excluding evening-shift workers, families without childcare, non-English speakers, and renters who cannot vote on property referenda. The feasibility-oriented view identifies a practical minimum: three outreach formats (evening town hall, recorded video with feedback form available at libraries, daytime building-level meeting), plain-language materials at an eighth-grade reading level mailed home with report cards, and trusted intermediaries (food pantries, churches, the mobile health van) as distribution channels. The risk-oriented view adds that IEP families require a distinct outreach strategy routed through special education directors and existing parent liaisons. Their prior experience with official district communications has often preceded service reductions.

### IEP Continuity Is a Legal Constraint and the Clearest Transition Design Standard

Both lenses treat IEP continuity as the most concrete and legally binding design constraint in the student transition plan. A risk-oriented view names federal liability exposure explicitly: even temporary disruption to IEP service delivery puts the district in legal violation. Schools have been sued for less. A feasibility-oriented view converts this into an enforceable rule: no IEP reassignment takes effect until the new provider has completed at least two in-person meetings with the student and family. Both agree that a dedicated transition support team (one or two counselors whose primary role in the first consolidation year is following cohorts of high-risk students) is affordable within the $1.2M administrative savings and far more protective than a general wellness policy.

### The Vote Is a Permission Slip, Not an Accountability Structure

Both lenses identify a governance design flaw: the consolidation vote, if passed, will be treated as permanent community endorsement even though the specific facility closures, program decisions, and staffing choices made afterward were not on the ballot. They diverge on the remedy. A feasibility-oriented view proposes writing a five-year equity report card clause into the merger agreement (requiring the new board to publish program parity data and hold a public comment session) as a durable accountability mechanism. A risk-oriented view calls for a community oversight board with real authority (not just advisory), meeting quarterly for the first three years, with standing to escalate concerns to the state education authority. Both also converge on the need for a student advisory panel (twelve to fifteen members from all three districts) whose written response to the feasibility study's student-experience sections is formally published alongside the study.

### Sequencing the Feasibility Study Release Is a Strategic, Not Logistical, Decision

Both lenses agree that releasing findings to one community before the others is the single highest-risk sequencing error: whichever community sees the data first will set the narrative before the others have context. Both call for a simultaneous coordinated release across all three districts in the same week. They diverge on framing. A feasibility-oriented view argues for leading with the financial floor ("here is what becomes unavoidable if we don't consolidate," using the two-year fund balance runway as the clarifying data point) rather than an opportunity ceiling. A risk-oriented view agrees on simultaneity but adds that trusted local voices (a longtime principal, a respected retired teacher) should be briefed on the findings before the public release so they can speak to them authentically. The political calendar should be checked for low-noise windows, since findings released during a budget crisis or local election cycle will be processed through that immediate anxiety.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)

- Commission a side-by-side program inventory across all three districts (AP course access, elective breadth, facility condition ratings by building, teacher turnover rates by district) and designate this as the baseline obligation document for the consolidation agreement. [convergent]
- Audit the existing communication plan against demographic data for all three districts; add Spanish-language materials, childcare at evening hearings, and at least one daytime session per building before the first public meeting is scheduled. [convergent]
- Pull three existing data sets to map the high-risk student transition population: current IEP caseloads by building, foster care placements on record, and students who have experienced two or more school transfers in the past three years. This takes approximately one week and gives planners a concrete population to design around. [unique: feasibility]
- Convene a citizen study-design committee — at least two parents per district, one or two high school students, and representatives from lower-income populations — before the consulting firm finalizes the scope of work, so the feasibility study answers the communities' questions rather than the board's. [convergent]

### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Write binding program-floor commitments into the merger agreement with specific timelines: minimum AP course counts per content area, per-pupil (not per-school) instrument and elective funding, and a phased three-year step-up plan for the underfunded district funded explicitly from the $1.2M administrative savings. [convergent]
- Establish a twelve-to-fifteen-member student advisory panel drawn from all three districts, meeting monthly for six months before the vote; publish the panel's written response to the feasibility study's student-experience sections alongside the full study. [convergent]
- Require a joint union-management equity review of all seniority provisions in the merged contract before ratification, with the explicit goal of not disadvantaging teachers who were already working in harder conditions. [unique: risk]
- Set an IEP reassignment rule as a named standard in the transition plan: no IEP team reassignment takes effect until the new provider has completed at least two in-person meetings with the student and family. Pair this with a named individual responsible for each student's file transfer and a 90-day post-transition service verification check-in. [convergent]
- Appoint a transition support team of one to two counselors whose primary role in the first consolidation year is following cohorts of high-risk students. Fund this as a line item in the consolidation budget. [convergent]

### Long-term (1+ years)

- Write a five-year equity review clause into the merger agreement requiring the new board to publish an annual equity report card (program parity by feeder community, transportation times, student outcome gaps) and hold a public comment session on the results each year. A risk-oriented view adds that this body should have real authority to escalate findings to the state education authority. [trade-off: feasibility-oriented view favors the reporting mechanism alone; risk-oriented view requires escalation authority to prevent the report from becoming a formality]
- Seat one high school student representative with speaking rights on the new unified board and establish a formal student input process for any facility or program change in the first five years. [unique: risk]
- Conduct a renter and non-property-owner survey through landlords, churches, and the housing authority during the feasibility period; publish the results so the board has documented grounds to show the plan reflects a broader constituency than the ballot. [unique: feasibility]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- The feasibility study must document per-district program gaps as a binding baseline. Consolidation cannot be designed as if all three districts start from the same place.
- Public hearings as currently formatted will systematically exclude evening-shift workers, families without childcare, non-English speakers, and IEP families with distrust of official channels. Tiered outreach formats are not optional.
- IEP continuity is a federal legal requirement. Any service disruption, even temporary, exposes the consolidated district to liability and must be treated as the hardest constraint in transition planning.
- A simultaneous coordinated feasibility study release across all three communities in the same week is the minimum viable sequencing. Sequential release hands narrative control to whichever community hears first.
- A student advisory panel with published written input is achievable at low cost and directly addresses the ballot's structural exclusion of students

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility-oriented case for tiered outreach formats, plain-language materials, and trusted-intermediary partnerships as sufficient to satisfy accountability to hard-to-reach families. A risk-oriented counterpoint holds that these mechanisms still operate within the same political process that pre-selects who gets heard, and that a vote can pass while families with the greatest stake were never genuinely in the room.
- A feasibility-oriented case for leading the feasibility study release with the financial floor ("here is what becomes unavoidable") as the most clarifying framing for skeptical audiences. A risk-oriented view holds that the first public framing must be "shared opportunity" rather than financial crisis, or the narrative will harden around fear rather than agency.
- A feasibility-oriented case for a five-year equity report card clause as a durable accountability structure post-consolidation. A risk-oriented counterpoint holds that a reporting mechanism without escalation authority becomes a formality that administrators can satisfy without changing anything.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- Union seniority reconciliation replicating existing hierarchies in teacher assignments and professional development access — even under a unified contract — is a structural equity risk that a feasibility lens alone won't surface [risk-oriented]
- Savings projections must be stress-tested against equity remediation costs as a modeled line item, not an assumption that savings flow directly to deficit reduction [risk-oriented]
- Tracking participation gaps as a published process metric before the vote — showing who attended hearings by building and geography — creates visible accountability that "we held public hearings" sign-offs do not [feasibility-oriented]
- Briefing trusted local voices before the public release so they can speak authentically to the findings, and checking the political calendar for low-noise release windows, are risk management decisions with no feasibility cost [risk-oriented]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- The emotional and phenomenological dimension of consolidation for students and parents (the experience of losing a school that anchored daily identity, not just the logistics of IEP transfers) was named by the risk-oriented lens as "micro-transitions" but not developed as a design problem. Neither lens asked: what would it mean to take seriously that some children's sense of safety is located in a specific building, a specific hallway, a specific face? That question would generate different safeguards than the operational checklists both lenses produced.
- Federal special education law (IDEA) was mentioned once as a liability exposure but neither lens examined it as a design constraint that runs the entire transition: the requirement to maintain a free appropriate public education without interruption sets the floor for what "adequate safeguards" actually means legally, and neither lens worked from that floor upward.
- The lifecycle dimension of continued enrollment decline was absent: both lenses addressed consolidation as a transition event, but the county's enrollment is projected to fall another 15% by 2035. Neither lens asked what equity and voice structures look like in a district that consolidates now and continues shrinking. At what point do the equity floors written today become unaffordable, and what governance mechanism allows communities to renegotiate rather than absorb the next round of cuts silently?
