# Summary: Enrollment Decline and Long-Term Sustainability

## Executive Summary

Consolidation may arrest the fiscal crisis without interrupting the enrollment-quality doom loop that is driving it. A feasibility-oriented view holds that the $1.2M in administrative savings can reverse the program-cuts-to-outmigration spiral, but only if those savings are explicitly committed to curriculum restoration in Year 2, not absorbed into deficit reduction. A risk-oriented counterpoint flags that this commitment requires a binding, measurable mechanism. Without that, the consolidated district inherits three districts' worth of deferred problems and produces the same declining educational product at higher administrative cost. Both lenses agree on the diagnosis: the fiscal crisis and the quality crisis are intertwined. A plan that addresses only one will fail on the other.

On the insolvency timeline, both perspectives converge with unusual force. The smallest district's two-year fund balance is not a financial footnote. It is the strongest consolidation argument available, and it is currently being squandered through vague "fiscal stress" framing. Both lenses call for translating this into a specific, public insolvency date alongside a concrete description of what state receivership actually means: loss of local governance, state-appointed administration, and merger terms dictated by state budget priorities rather than community values. Where the two lenses diverge is on the "deliberate insolvency as leverage" strategy. A feasibility-oriented view recommends using the two-year window as urgency framing without waiting for actual collapse, compressing the governance timeline accordingly. A risk-oriented view warns that receivership is not a controlled event and that District C's collapse could dissolve political pressure in Districts A and B rather than intensify it.

The most important structural gap both lenses identify is the failure to design for the second enrollment wave. A consolidated district built for 1,800 students will hit the same facility overcapacity and staffing problems at 1,530 by 2035. Both perspectives independently recommended designing around 1,400 students, treating current headcount as temporary abundance, and embedding automatic review triggers in the consolidation charter rather than waiting for the next political crisis.

## Key Themes

### The Doom Loop Requires a Binding Reinvestment Commitment

Both lenses agree that administrative savings only interrupt the enrollment-quality spiral if they are contractually redirected into curriculum restoration. A feasibility-oriented view recommends identifying a high-visibility "consolidation dividend"—a dual-enrollment program, competitive robotics team, or performing arts offering—and pricing it as an explicit Year 2 budget line item. A risk-oriented view adds that without a binding percentage commitment (proposed: 60% of administrative savings into programming within 24 months), the consolidated district will face the same quality-erosion dynamic within a decade, just from a larger fiscal baseline. Both perspectives note that surveying exit data is a prerequisite. If outmigration is primarily economic (families leaving the county), program investment cannot reverse it. If it is school-choice driven, it can.

### Insolvency as Concrete Fact, Not Abstract Warning

This theme is convergent across both lenses with unusual specificity. The smallest district's two-year fund balance should be translated into a public, dated insolvency projection—not "within two years" but a specific quarter and year under documented assumptions. State receivership should be characterized with equal precision: a plain-language one-pager from the state education department on what powers shift, what local control is eliminated, and what historical precedents show about academic outcomes under state administration. Both lenses also call for quantifying the difference in transition costs and governance options between voluntary consolidation now and state-forced merger in 2028. A feasibility-oriented view emphasizes that early disclosure gives voters time to process the stakes. A risk-oriented view warns that the argument must proactively close the "District C is the small district's problem, not ours" interpretation before it becomes the dominant narrative in Districts A and B.

### Design for Continued Decline, Not Current Enrollment

Both lenses independently converged on designing the consolidated district for 1,400 students rather than 1,800, and embedding automatic charter triggers for facility and staffing reviews at pre-specified numerical thresholds. A feasibility-oriented view recommends separating the 10-year operating plan (updated annually) from the 30-year facility plan (buildings classified by priority tier from day one), and adding an enrollment floor analysis to the feasibility study scope before the vote. A risk-oriented view flags that the 30-year planning horizon is politically toxic if foregrounded in the public campaign. The honest analysis and the voter-facing materials can present different time windows without deception. But the charter triggers must be binding and numerical, not aspirational.

### The Quality Hypothesis Must Be Tested Empirically

A unique risk-oriented contribution: if educational quality erosion is even partially causing outmigration (rather than merely accompanying it), a consolidation plan that leads with administrative efficiency and ignores curriculum fails its own stated goal. The feasibility study should include a comparative analysis of the three districts' academic outcome metrics against demographically similar rural districts that have not lost population. A feasibility-oriented view adds that this comparison should be produced by a neutral third party—state university or regional education service agency—because parents who have watched a decade of program cuts will not trust district-produced quality narratives. Both agree the comparison is an empirical question the county can answer before the vote, and that leaving it unanswered is an avoidable gap.

### Pilot Before District-Wide Reimagining

Both lenses treated the "decline as invitation" framing cautiously as a public message. It reads as rationalization to current families. Both acknowledged real operational opportunity in smaller enrollment. A feasibility-oriented view recommends identifying underutilized buildings amenable to hybrid school/community-hub use and surveying top county employers about apprenticeship program participation before assuming the model is viable. A risk-oriented view calls for a genuine 18-month pilot in one grade at one school, with honest evaluation before scaling. Both agree that the facility rationalization required to fund innovative programming must happen simultaneously. Keeping all seven buildings open for community sentiment reasons consumes the resources that smaller enrollment could otherwise redirect.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)

- Commission a three-scenario insolvency model (current trends, modest recovery, continued decline) producing specific insolvency quarters for each district by name. Translate the smallest district's two-year fund balance into a public, dated projection and disclose it at the start of the feasibility process, not as a late campaign reveal. [convergent]
- Add an enrollment floor analysis to the feasibility study scope: model consolidated district viability at 1,200, 1,400, and 1,600 students, with per-pupil cost, staffing implications, and facility requirements at each level. [convergent]
- Survey exit data from the past three years to identify where departing families went (home school, private, neighboring district, out of county) and disaggregate enrollment trends by grade cohort to distinguish demographic outmigration from school-choice-driven exit. [convergent]

### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Draft three specific numerical tripwires for the consolidation charter — per-pupil operating cost threshold, bond rating trigger, enrollment floor checkpoint (proposed: 1,400) — at which the board is required to commission a formal facility and staffing review within 90 days and produce a public report with a board vote. [convergent]
- Identify the two or three program gaps most cited by departing families, cost their restoration, and show in the consolidation plan that Year 2 administrative savings fund those restorations as explicit budget line items. Announce at least one high-visibility "consolidation dividend" (e.g., dual-enrollment partnership, AP course expansion) that launches in Year 1. [trade-off: a feasibility-oriented case that this reverses the doom loop; a risk-oriented counterpoint that without a binding percentage commitment (proposed 60% of administrative savings), savings will be absorbed into deficit reduction, reproducing the same dynamic]
- Obtain a plain-language one-pager from the state education department on receivership mechanics and document the cost and governance differences between voluntary consolidation now and state-forced merger in 2028. Model the millage rate increase required to sustain the current three-district structure and make that number explicit in public meetings. [convergent]
- Commission comparative academic outcome analysis from a neutral third party (state university or regional education service agency) comparing the three districts against demographically similar rural districts; use the results to calibrate whether the consolidation narrative leads with efficiency or curriculum improvement. [unique: feasibility]

### Long-term (1+ years)

- Build a rolling five-year operating plan updated annually alongside a 30-year facility plan that classifies all seven buildings by priority tier (anchor, adaptive reuse candidate, closure candidate) based on documented enrollment scenarios. Keep these as separate governance documents with separate decision processes. [convergent]
- Design and launch a genuine 18-month pilot of a self-directed or project-based learning cohort (one grade, one school, honest evaluation criteria) and survey the county's top 20 employers about structured apprenticeship program participation before scaling any work-integrated learning model. [trade-off: a feasibility-oriented case for identifying existing teachers already using project-based methods and building the pilot around them; a risk-oriented counterpoint that teacher professional development capacity in a smaller district may be insufficient to execute the model without significant external investment]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- The insolvency framing must be specific and dated — vague fiscal stress warnings allow voters to believe the situation is recoverable; a concrete insolvency quarter and year changes the political calculus fundamentally.
- The consolidated district must be designed for 1,400 students (below the 2035 floor estimate), with governance charter triggers requiring mandatory review at pre-specified thresholds — waiting for another political crisis is the default and must be explicitly designed against.
- Administrative savings interrupt the enrollment-quality spiral only if they are committed to curriculum restoration; a plan that absorbs savings into deficit reduction or deferred maintenance replicates the same dynamic at consolidated scale.
- Exit data and grade-cohort survival rates are empirically available and must be analyzed before the vote; building a consolidation argument on the wrong causal model wastes the intervention.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility-oriented case for using the smallest district's two-year fund balance as an urgency frame that compresses the governance timeline toward an 18-month vote deadline. The window is real and depreciating. A risk-oriented counterpoint views the "watch one district collapse first" variant as a high-risk strategy: state receivership is not a controlled event, and District C's collapse may dissolve political pressure in Districts A and B rather than intensify it, while also generating harm to students and staff.
- A feasibility-oriented case for leading the campaign with academic quality improvement as the primary voter argument. Families respond to specific program offerings, not abstract efficiency. A risk-oriented counterpoint: promising academic improvement sets an accountability trap if the consolidated district delivers marginal gains, and the remaining community (revealed-preference: relatively insensitive to academic quality) may be more persuaded by cost, stability, and "keeping schools open" than by course catalogs.
- A feasibility-oriented case that the 30-year planning horizon should be embedded in the governance charter from the start. A risk-oriented view: foregrounding a 30-year plan acknowledging further facility closures will cause voters to vote no. Separating the feasibility analysis from the public campaign materials is sequencing rather than deception.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- The insolvency argument must proactively close the "District C's problem, not ours" interpretation before it becomes dominant in Districts A and B — including a legal analysis of what happens to District C's students, assets, and debts in a receivership scenario. [risk-oriented]
- Union dynamics are not neutral in the insolvency scenario: if District C's teachers face a receivership-driven layoff, unions in the other two districts may harden contract demands rather than cooperate on reconciliation — engage union leadership privately and early. [risk-oriented]
- The "consolidation dividend" program offering should be selected based on what is actually feasible at 1,800 students and can be announced credibly for Year 1, not on what sounds most compelling in campaign materials. [feasibility-oriented]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- **Equity and distributional**: Neither lens addressed which students are leaving versus which are staying. If the families who have remained are disproportionately lower-income or have fewer school-choice options, the community bearing the cost of continued decline (program erosion, deferred maintenance) is not the same community that exercised exit. A consolidation plan treating remaining enrollment as a uniform constituency may misallocate its equity commitments and misjudge voter motivation.
- **Relational and community identity**: The doom-loop and insolvency framings both treat enrollment as a number to be stabilized. Neither addressed what happens to the social fabric of communities that lose their school as an anchor institution, including effects on property values, local business, and intergenerational community attachment. The "our school is our town" resistance flagged in the request is treated as a political obstacle rather than as evidence about what schools actually do for these communities that consolidation must explicitly preserve or replace.
- **Lifecycle and temporal sequencing**: Both lenses focused on the consolidation vote and the first decade. Neither addressed the teacher pipeline problem over a 30-year horizon — whether a smaller, consolidated district in a rural county can sustain the recruiting pipeline needed to staff the innovative programs both lenses recommend, or whether the staffing vacancies (14 last year) will persist and worsen regardless of governance structure.
