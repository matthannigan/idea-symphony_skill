# Summary: Governance Design and Voter Approval

## Executive Summary

The core tension in this cluster is not resolved by either lens, and naming it directly matters. The governance structures that are politically necessary to win all three district votes (geographic seat guarantees, facility moratoriums, enforceable representation floors) are structurally in tension with the board's ability to make the closure and consolidation decisions that justify the merger in the first place. A feasibility-oriented view holds that a 7-seat hybrid board (three geographic seats plus four at-large) with the geographic guarantee written into the founding charter is the right design. It's manageable, road-tested in analogous rural consolidations, and legible to skeptical voters as a real protection. A risk-oriented counterpoint is that any governance structure designed primarily to survive the approval vote is optimized for the wrong outcome. A board that cannot execute a closure decision under political pressure isn't a success—it's a delayed failure. The two lenses converge on the destination (enforceable structural protections, not aspirational promises) but diverge sharply on how much institutional constraint is prudent.

Both perspectives agree that the binding-vs.-aspirational distinction is the consolidation's most dangerous design gap. Politically necessary commitments ("we will never close your school") are often the least enforceable ones. Voters who believe them become the most dangerous post-consolidation opponents when circumstances change. A feasibility-oriented approach proposes a two-category framework in all campaign materials: "Consolidation Agreement Commitments" (chartered, enforceable) versus "Board Priorities" (stated intentions subject to conditions). Adding a deferred-maintenance escrow funded from administrative savings makes the facility moratorium credible. A risk-oriented view flags that even charter commitments can be amended by a future board majority. The only genuinely enforceable protections are those with operational teeth: endowment funds tied to building status, state-supervised trigger provisions, or community veto rights with a defined legal remedy.

Both lenses also converge on a warning about the endorsement cascade: framing one district's "yes" as momentum leverage for the others risks being read by identity-resistant communities as proof that their concerns are obstacles to be managed, not values to be accommodated. The sequencing strategy should be logistically pre-staged but not publicly instrumentalized.

## Key Themes

### Board Composition as a Merger Signal, Not Just an Administrative Decision
Both lenses treat board structure as the most visible early signal of whether consolidation means absorption or genuine merger. Both warn that formal seat allocation alone is insufficient. A feasibility-oriented view proposes a 7-seat hybrid with geographic seats written into the founding charter and a cap preventing any former-district area from holding more than two at-large seats. A risk-oriented counterpoint flags that communities will judge merger versus absorption not by the board map but by early decisions: which superintendent is hired, what the district is named, where the central office lands. A perfect board composition followed by hiring the largest district's superintendent reads as absorption regardless of seat count.

### Enforceability Is the Structural Weakness of the Approval Campaign
Both lenses converge on this as the most critical design failure point. Campaign commitments that win votes — facility guarantees, representation protections — are systematically the hardest to enforce. A feasibility-oriented view offers a practical framework: write everything that voters need protected into the consolidation agreement filed with the state; delegate operational but politically sensitive decisions to the new board with a defined community input process. A risk-oriented counterpoint insists that even charter provisions survive only if communities have resources to litigate violations, and that genuinely enforceable protections require operational mechanisms (escrow accounts, threshold-based triggers, state supervision) rather than political promises with no legal remedy.

### The Asymmetric Veto Requires Designing for the Hardest Case
Both lenses flag the all-three-districts approval requirement as the governing constraint that shapes the entire strategy. A feasibility-oriented view recommends commissioning a targeted survey in the most identity-resistant district before the feasibility study is finalized. Resistance is not monolithic. Knowing whether it centers on school closure, governance voice, tax equity, or cultural identity determines which design features are non-negotiable. A risk-oriented counterpoint warns that designing explicitly to win over the hardest case causes other communities to notice they are being deprioritized and become the new hardest cases. The solution is to design around shared principles with transparent process commitments, not community-specific sweeteners that create a bidding war for objections.

### Champion Credibility Is Domain-Specific
Both lenses treat local champions as essential but warn that credibility is not transferable across issue frames. A feasibility-oriented view identifies union chapter presidents as the highest-leverage early targets. They are trusted across the broadest voter cross-section, and their quiet support (or silence) is worth more than formal endorsements from elected officials. A risk-oriented counterpoint adds that the most prominent figures in each community may activate counter-organizing by signaling that the establishment is forcing the decision. A "second tier" strategy recruiting parents, coaches, and teachers who are trusted in specific social networks but not visible enough to galvanize opposition may be more effective. Both agree that champions need something concrete to show their constituents, not just something to believe.

### Binding Commitments Need Operational Teeth
Both lenses converge on the 5-year facility moratorium as politically necessary. They diverge on what makes it credible. A feasibility-oriented view recommends pairing the moratorium with a deferred-maintenance escrow funded from the $1.2M in projected administrative savings. This converts it from a political promise into a funded commitment. A risk-oriented counterpoint proposes conditional rather than absolute moratoriums. The school remains open as long as enrollment in the attendance zone exceeds a defined threshold, giving communities meaningful protection while building in a data-driven off-ramp that prevents the board from maintaining an underutilized building through a future budget crisis.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Commission a targeted community survey in the most identity-resistant district, specifically diagnosing whether resistance centers on school closure, governance voice, tax rates, or cultural identity — this determines which design features are structurally non-negotiable before the feasibility study is finalized. `[convergent]`
- Designate a single spokesperson with authority to speak to facility futures; document every public commitment made to any community, and require all board members and campaign leadership to sign off on a single written facilities statement before the public campaign launches. `[convergent]`
- Research the sequential approval option under state consolidation statute and AG opinion history; if permitted, define a fallback structure (e.g., a 90-day two-district negotiation window if one district declines) and publish it before the first vote so ambiguity does not suppress turnout. `[unique: feasibility]`

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Publish the proposed 7-seat board structure — three geographic seats plus four at-large, with the geographic guarantee and a cap preventing any former-district area from holding more than two at-large seats — at least six months before the referendum, written into the founding charter (not just board policy) so it survives future political shifts. `[convergent]`
- Develop a two-category framework for all campaign materials: "Consolidation Agreement Commitments" (chartered, filed with the state, requiring supermajority plus public notice to amend) versus "Board Priorities" (stated intentions subject to conditions); apply this labeling consistently so voters who read it understand what is and is not locked down. `[convergent]`
- Write the facility moratorium into the consolidation agreement as a conditional commitment: the school remains open as long as enrollment in the attendance zone exceeds a defined threshold, with a specific expiration date and a defined closure process (board vote, community input period, state notification). Include a deferred-maintenance escrow line in the first consolidated budget, funded by a portion of the $1.2M administrative savings. `[trade-off: feasibility-oriented view supports an absolute 5-year moratorium as the more legible political commitment; risk-oriented counterpoint prefers the enrollment-threshold trigger to prevent maintenance costs from overwhelming the savings consolidation was meant to generate]`
- Stagger terms for all seven board seats with geographic seats offset (Seat A expires year 3, Seat B year 5, Seat C year 7) so no single election cycle can flip all geographic representation simultaneously. `[convergent]`

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Write a mandatory five-year governance effectiveness review into the founding charter — with a public report and structured community input process — signaling that the governance structure is not permanent and can adapt as the district matures. `[convergent]`
- Require district representatives to hold annual public town halls in their geographic area with published questions and responses, creating an accountability mechanism that outlasts the initial honeymoon period and prevents board members from shifting loyalty entirely to the unified institution. `[unique: risk]`

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- Geographic seat guarantees must be written into the founding charter filed with the state, not just board policy — charter provisions require supermajority plus public notice to amend, which is the difference between a promise and an enforceable structural protection.
- A public commitment registry — documenting every facility or governance promise made by any official representative, reviewed by all three communities' planning liaisons — is essential before contradictory informal commitments made in town halls harden into incompatible expectations.
- Champion credibility is issue-specific: union chapter presidents carry the broadest cross-community credibility on staffing and job security questions; prominent business owners may damage consolidation credibility with parents if their endorsements frame schools through an economic lens.
- Pre-staging logistical response infrastructure (town halls, FAQ updates, campaign materials) before any district vote is necessary to capture sequential momentum — improvised follow-through loses the window.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility-oriented case for an absolute 5-year facility moratorium written into the consolidation agreement as the most politically legible commitment skeptical voters can evaluate; a risk-oriented counterpoint that an absolute moratorium forces the board to maintain underutilized buildings (if enrollment drops faster than projected) and consumes the savings consolidation was meant to generate. A conditional trigger (enrollment threshold plus defined off-ramp) provides meaningful protection without removing the board's ability to respond to actual conditions.
- A feasibility-oriented case for using District 3's "yes" vote as a targeted credibility accelerator, with pre-drafted district-specific messages ready to deploy within 72 hours; a risk-oriented counterpoint that publicly instrumentalizing one district's distress as a persuasion tool for others signals to identity-resistant communities that their concerns are obstacles to manage rather than values to accommodate. The cascade strategy should be logistically pre-staged but never publicly framed as a momentum signal.
- A feasibility-oriented case for designing governance structures explicitly around the most skeptical community's specific objections; a risk-oriented counterpoint that community-specific design sweeteners create a bidding war for objections and cause other communities to notice they are being deprioritized — shared-principle framing with transparent process commitments is more durable than targeted accommodation.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- The civic participation infrastructure that looks like an asset may be organized opposition. Communities with strong "defend our school" civic networks have built political infrastructure for "no." Treating existing civic engagement capacity as a consolidation resource without first assessing whether its leaders are consolidation-neutral or consolidation-hostile is a significant campaign planning error. `[unique: risk]`
- Novel governance features — supermajority thresholds for specific decision categories, multi-stakeholder models — may require state waivers or legislative changes that add 12-24 months to the consolidation timeline; communities in fiscal distress may not have that runway. The most innovative features that can be implemented within existing state school district law should be identified before committing to designs that assume waivers will be granted. `[unique: risk]`
- A deferred-maintenance escrow funded from administrative savings converts the facility moratorium from a political promise into a funded commitment — and gives fiscal conservatives a concrete demonstration that savings are being deployed responsibly rather than absorbed into general operations. `[unique: feasibility]`

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- **Equity/access/distributional:** Neither lens examined what the governance structure means for students from lower-income households or those with disabilities. Specifically, whether geographic board seats and advisory councils are accessible to the parents those students have, or whether civic participation infrastructure disproportionately represents the most organized and least economically precarious households. A consolidated district serving 1,800 students across a rural county is large enough that distributional blind spots in governance design will compound over time.
- **Relational/social:** Neither lens addressed the social fabric effects of the governance transition itself: relationships between teachers across the three former districts, between building principals who now report to the same superintendent, and between parent communities that have had adversarial competitive identities (rival sports programs, curriculum philosophy differences). Governance structures that look functional on paper can generate real dysfunction when the humans inside them have unresolved relational history.
- **Lifecycle/temporal:** Both lenses focused on the governance architecture needed for years 1-5 (stabilization and vote credibility). Neither examined what governance the district needs in years 10-20, when the consolidation cohort of board members has retired, the merger trauma has faded, and the district faces a second round of enrollment decline under demographic conditions no current model projects. A 5-year governance review addresses this partially, but the founding charter's design choices will still shape institutional culture two decades out.
