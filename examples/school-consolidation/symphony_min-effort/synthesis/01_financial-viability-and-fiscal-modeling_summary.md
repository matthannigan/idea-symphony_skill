# Summary: Financial Viability and Fiscal Modeling

## Executive Summary

The central fiscal challenge of this consolidation isn't whether it saves money; it does. The real question is whether the district can survive the gap between when transition costs arrive and when structural savings accumulate. The brainstormer surfaces this timing problem repeatedly: payback periods of 4–7 years, a 12–15 month state funding gap, day-one debt inheritance, and a phase-in period for property tax equalization all compress into a critical window of financial vulnerability. The practical task for planners is to bound each gap with a named number, a named funding source, and a named timeline. Leaving them undefined isn't neutral—it's an invitation for opponents to fill the vacuum with worst-case figures.

The strongest recurring argument is comparative: consolidation's trajectory isn't ideal, but status quo collapse is faster. The smallest district faces insolvency in 2028 under current conditions; a consolidated district, even under stress, has a longer runway. Putting both trajectories on the same chart with the status quo cliff visible is the most persuasive fiscal communication available to consolidation advocates.

The brainstormer also consistently identifies grant and incentive funding as underused levers. State consolidation incentive programs, deferred maintenance grants, bridge loans, and negotiated funding guarantees recur across multiple questions as mechanisms that reduce net taxpayer exposure. Pursuing these proactively, before the vote, converts abstract savings projections into concrete secured commitments — a qualitatively different political message.

## Key Themes

### Bounded Uncertainty as Credibility Tool
Across every cost and risk dimension, the brainstormer emphasizes naming the number: the transition cost range ($1.8M–$5M), the cash flow bridge needed ($300K–$500K over 14 months), the payback window (4–7 years base case). Bounded problems with identified solutions command more trust from voters and bond markets than vague transition risks. Presenting three fiscal scenarios (optimistic, base, and stress) is more persuasive than a single engineered figure.

### Proactive Grant Pursuit Reduces Net Exposure
State consolidation incentive programs, USDA Rural Development grants, deferred maintenance infrastructure grants, and bridge loan programs appear in four of the five questions. Each recommendation urges applying before the vote, with conditional approval pending voter authorization. Securing partial grant commitments before the campaign transforms the fiscal narrative from "here is what consolidation will cost" to "here is what we have already obtained to offset costs."

### Comparative Trajectory Over Absolute Savings
The case for consolidation is strengthened by head-to-head comparison with status quo trajectories, not by absolute savings claims alone. This argument appears consistently: consolidation's declining enrollment path is shallower than status quo's; phase-in over 5–7 years is less disruptive than the shock of district failure; negotiating a year-one funding guarantee by framing state intervention costs as the alternative. The "status quo cliff" framing recurs across the questions.

### Granularity Defeats Opposition Arguments
Abstract fiscal claims invite opposition arithmetic. Household-level tax impact calculations ("a $120,000 home in Riverdale pays $47 more in year one"), per-category transition cost inventories, and ring-fenced debt accounting by community of origin make it harder for opponents to substitute their own numbers. Granularity serves as both political armor and fiscal discipline.

### Sequenced and Phased Execution
Order and timing are risk management tools themselves. Multiple recommendations call for engineering the merger date to minimize the state formula recalculation gap, sequencing consolidation so the most liability-laden district resolves its worst problems before merger day, and phasing property tax equalization over 5–7 years rather than equalizing on day one. The fiscal model functions as a scheduling document, not just a projection.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Commission a line-item transition cost inventory covering severance (2x annual salary per affected employee for union contracts), ERP/SIS unification ($400K–$800K range), legal fees ($150K–$250K), and facility remediation — do not release a single aggregate figure until this itemization is complete. `[recurring]`
- Audit fund balances across all three districts and identify restricted funds (capital project funds, insurance reserves) eligible for operating cash redirection during transition. `[single]`
- Identify and apply for state consolidation incentive funds, USDA Rural Development grants, and deferred maintenance infrastructure grants before the merger vote, with conditional approval language pending voter authorization. `[recurring]`

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Build a 10-year fiscal model with three scenarios (optimistic: payback year 3; base: payback year 5; stress: payback year 9+) and a 24-month post-merger monthly cash flow projection that maps bridge funding against the state formula recalculation gap. `[recurring]`
- Model both consolidation and status quo enrollment trajectories on a single chart with discrete trigger points (state-mandated program cuts, second facility closure threshold, required athletic co-op enrollment levels) so the comparison is visual and the cliff is visible. `[recurring]`
- Identify the exact state funding formula recalculation date and engineer the merger effective date to minimize the gap — a calendar choice alone could be worth $400K–$500K in earlier revenue. `[single]`
- Negotiate with state education officials for a consolidation-year funding guarantee at the consolidated rate, framing it as cheaper for the state than an insolvency proceeding. `[single]`
- Commission a binding pre-consolidation facility condition assessment, incorporated into the consolidation agreement, to establish a legal liability schedule before any district can quietly transfer hidden structural problems. `[single]`

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Structure debt service accounting by community of origin for bonds approved by specific district voters, and phase property tax equalization over 5–7 years with household-level dollar impact statements (not millage rates) published per district. `[recurring]`
- Explore a consolidation bond at the same election as the merger vote to convert the $4.2M deferred maintenance backlog from an unfunded liability into a funded obligation with a repayment schedule. `[single]`

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- State consolidation incentive programs in Iowa, Kansas, and Nebraska range $200K–$600K; deferred maintenance grant narratives are compelling when three districts' backlogs are bundled into a single ask.
- Assessed value growth from wind energy or commercial development in any of the three districts could naturally narrow the property tax rate gap without requiring rate increases in the lowest-tax district.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- The 12–15 month window between merger effective date and state formula recalculation creates a real cash flow gap estimated at $300K–$500K. Without pre-arranged bridge loan approval, this gap becomes a crisis rather than a managed transition.
- Rural employer closure (factory, farm cooperative, regional hospital) could compress a decade of demographic decline into two years. The consolidated district's ability to survive a 25% enrollment drop in three years should be stress-tested explicitly.

**Trade-offs**:
- A January 1 merger effective date may trigger state formula recalculation sooner (saving $400K–$500K in earlier revenue) but compresses implementation time compared to a July 1 date.
- Ring-fencing legacy bond debt by community of origin is politically protective in low-debt districts but adds accounting complexity and may complicate the consolidated district's unified credit profile.

**Conspicuous absences** (stances the brainstormer's output never took):
- The brainstormer never names the teachers' union or existing collective bargaining agreements as a structural cost variable. Salary schedule harmonization across three districts is typically one of the largest long-term fiscal drivers in rural consolidations — the question of whether the consolidated district adopts the highest, lowest, or a blended schedule is a precondition for any credible long-term savings projection. This is an (a)-class absence: administrators and legal fees are named, but the workforce cost structure is not.
- The brainstormer recommends issuing a consolidation bond but never addresses voter approval risk for that bond — a bond measure failing while the merger passes would leave the deferred maintenance liability unfunded and the merger's fiscal premise weakened. The precondition (voter appetite for a bond) is never established.
