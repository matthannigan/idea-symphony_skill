# Brainstorming Responses: Community Identity and Political Coalition Building

---

## Question 15: Identity Preservation Strategies

*Beyond keeping a school building open, what structural or symbolic elements — team names, local advisory councils, feeder school identities, community use agreements — can a consolidated district offer to communities worried about losing their local character?*

* **Retain legacy team names as permanently protected identifiers.** Rather than forcing a merged mascot or brand, enshrine in the consolidation agreement that elementary and middle feeder schools keep their legacy names, mascots, and colors permanently — not subject to future board revision. This turns a one-time political concession into a structural guarantee. Many rural communities attach 100+ years of identity to "the Ridgemont Wildcats"; erasing that generates opposition that outlasts the consolidation vote itself. A named athletic tradition costs nothing to preserve and buys enormous goodwill.

* **Charter a Local Community Advisory Council (LCAC) for each legacy district, with a defined role in school decisions.** Each LCAC meets quarterly, reviews facility use, program offerings, and principal hiring recommendations for its campus, and has formal standing to petition the unified board. Codify this in the board governance bylaws — not just a policy that can be repealed. The difference between a real LCAC and a "we'll listen to you" promise is whether it has a chair on the district calendar, a budget line for facilitation, and a written response requirement when the board overrides a recommendation.

* **Community use agreements with teeth — square footage guarantees, not just good intentions.** Negotiate binding community use agreements before the vote that specify minimum hours per week each legacy school building must remain available for non-school community functions (town meetings, senior programs, 4-H, polling places). Include a clawback clause: if the district fails to honor access commitments for two consecutive quarters, affected towns can petition the state education department. This converts "we promise to keep the gym open" into an enforceable contract, addressing the real fear that consolidation = building closure.

* **Establish a "Heritage Fund" for each community, seeded from administrative savings.** Dedicate a fixed percentage of the first-year administrative savings (say, 15% of the $1.2M, or ~$180K) to a community heritage fund split three ways. Each community controls its share for local school-linked programming: a town history mural in the hallway, a local scholarship, a homecoming parade. This makes the cost savings tangible and local, rather than disappearing into the consolidated district's general fund. It also gives identity advocates something they helped create, not just something they agreed not to fight.

* **Feeder school identity through graduation ceremony and traditions.** Let each legacy elementary and middle campus run its own end-of-year ceremony, issue its own yearbook, and maintain its own student council — even after consolidation. The unified district sets curriculum and staffing, but the cultural surface of each school stays locally shaped. Students who graduate from the consolidated high school still "grew up at Ridgemont" or "came from Valley Elementary." This costs almost nothing and addresses the layered identity question: it's not just the high school people are mourning, it's the pipeline of belonging.

## Question 16: Coalition Asymmetry

*If the fiscal case for consolidation is strongest in the most financially distressed district and weakest in the district with the healthiest fund balance, what benefits or assurances would make consolidation compelling to voters in the district with the least to gain?*

* **Translate the healthy district's "healthy fund balance" into a quantified risk they're not seeing.** The district with the healthiest fund balance likely believes it can outlast the problem. Commission a scenario analysis showing what happens to their per-pupil state funding, property tax burden, and course offerings if the two weaker districts consolidate with each other — or if one goes insolvent — without them. Their fund balance may be intact, but their enrollment is declining too. The asymmetry argument only holds if voters believe the status quo is stable; the job is to show it isn't, specifically and numerically.

* **Negotiate preferential governance representation for the district with the least fiscal urgency.** If the healthy district has 700 students out of 1,800 total, a pure proportional board would give it ~39% of seats. Offer 45% or a formal veto right on facilities decisions (since building closures are their primary concern) in exchange for their yes vote. This is not unfair — it reflects the political reality that consolidation cannot happen without them, and they are giving up more autonomy than the distressed districts. Structure this as a sunset clause: weighted representation phases to proportional after 8 years once trust is established.

* **Cap property tax increases for the healthy district's taxpayers in the consolidation agreement.** One fear in a wealthier-tax-base district is that consolidation means subsidizing neighbors' infrastructure debt. Commission an independent property tax equalization analysis, publish it prominently, and if it shows a net tax increase for healthy-district residents, build a rate cap into the consolidation plan — funded by state transition aid or phased revenue sharing. Voters will not approve a deal they can't calculate the cost of.

* **Offer the healthy district a named, durable program asset.** Identify one curricular or co-curricular program that the healthy district has and values — an agricultural science program, a performing arts tradition, a JROTC — and write it into the consolidation plan as a guaranteed offering for 10 years, housed at their campus. This transforms consolidation from "we lose something" to "our strength becomes a flagship for the whole district." Pair it with naming rights: "the [Town] Center for Agricultural Education."

* **Reframe "least to gain" as "most to protect."** The healthy district's voters need to hear an argument that consolidation protects their advantage, not erodes it. With declining enrollment, the per-pupil state funding penalty on small districts will eventually bite them too. Consolidation locks in a 12% per-pupil increase now, while they still have leverage to negotiate governance terms. If they wait until they're distressed, they'll consolidate with no bargaining power. The political pitch is: "You're strong enough to set the terms today. In five years, you won't be."

## Question 17: Winning the Voter Approval Campaign

*Given that all three districts must approve consolidation simultaneously, what sequencing of public engagement — joint forums, district-specific listening sessions, feasibility study rollout — builds the broadest possible informed consent before the vote?*

* **Lead with listening, not the feasibility study.** Hold district-specific listening sessions (not joint forums) in each community before any study findings are released. Ask: "What would you need to see to consider this?" Record and publish the answers. This inverts the typical consolidation process, which leads with financial data and generates defensiveness. When the feasibility study is released 6-8 weeks later, it can explicitly address the concerns collected in the listening sessions — "voters in District B asked about bus ride times; here's what the routing model shows." This converts the study from a document that advocates for consolidation into a document that responds to actual community questions.

* **Stagger the feasibility study rollout: district-specific briefings before the joint release.** Present the study findings to each district's school board separately, one week apart, before the public joint release. Give each board 72 hours to ask questions and request clarifications. Then hold a joint public forum where all three boards are on stage together. This sequence means board members are informed advocates, not surprised bystanders, when constituents ask hard questions at the joint forum. It also prevents any one board from being blindsided in public and going defensive.

* **Build a "Consolidation Study Committee" with 3-5 trusted residents from each district, appointed before the study begins.** These 9-15 people shadow the consultants throughout the feasibility study, attend work sessions, and are the first to see draft findings. Their role is not to endorse — it's to be informed enough to answer neighbor questions accurately. By the time the vote happens, each community has 3-5 people who can say "I saw the data, here's what it actually says" rather than every community relying on rumor and suspicion. These committee members become the peer-to-peer persuasion network that no campaign consultant can replicate.

* **Run the vote campaign as three simultaneous district-specific campaigns, not one unified campaign.** Create separate vote materials, spokespeople, and messaging for each district that emphasizes what that community gets. District A's flyer talks about program expansion and fiscal stability. District B's flyer talks about keeping their elementary school open. District C's flyer talks about governance representation. Voters vote for their district's interests; give them a locally-grounded reason to say yes. A single "consolidation is good for the county" message will lose in two of the three districts because it answers a question nobody asked.

* **Set a public timeline with named decision gates, and hold to it.** Announce at the outset: listening sessions in months 1-2, feasibility study released month 4, community forums months 5-6, board votes on ballot measure month 7, public vote month 10. Publish this on a shared website. When communities don't know when decisions will be made, they assume decisions are already made. A named timeline with public checkpoints signals legitimacy and gives opponents a structured process to engage rather than fight. Missing a gate erodes trust more than any single bad fact in the study.

## Question 18: Governance Design for Trust

*What board structure, representation formula, and transition governance arrangements for the new unified district would give each legacy community confidence that its interests won't be steamrolled by the combined majority?*

* **Adopt weighted representation with a sunset, not permanent proportional seats.** Give each legacy district two guaranteed board seats regardless of enrollment size, plus one at-large seat elected district-wide, for a 7-member board total. Set a 10-year sunset: at year 10, the board votes (requiring 5 of 7 members) on whether to shift to pure proportional representation. This gives small communities structural protection during the trust-building period without permanently distorting democratic representation. The sunset prevents the guaranteed seats from becoming entrenched, while the supermajority requirement means the shift can't happen without buy-in from legacy community representatives.

* **Establish a Transition Governance Board during the first 18 months, parallel to the new elected board.** Before the first consolidated board election, create a transition body with 2 representatives from each district's existing school board — 6 people — plus the new superintendent. This body has authority only over transition decisions: facility closures, contract reconciliation, and staffing reductions. Full operational governance transfers to the elected consolidated board after 18 months. This prevents the first 18 months from being governed by whoever wins the first election, which may not reflect the coalition that approved consolidation.

* **Require supermajority votes for facility closures.** Write into the new district's governance bylaws that closing or converting any school building requires a 5-of-7 board vote, not a simple majority. Building closure is the action legacy communities fear most, and a simple majority enables the two larger communities to outvote the smallest. A supermajority requirement forces coalition-building before any facility decision, effectively giving each legacy community a partial veto. Pair this with a community notification requirement: 180 days public notice before any closure vote, with two mandatory community hearings in the affected town.

* **Name the transition superintendent as a neutral party, selected jointly.** The first superintendent of the consolidated district should be selected by a committee that includes 2 representatives from each legacy district — equal weight regardless of size — and should be someone with no prior relationship to any of the three districts. This matters enormously: if the superintendent is seen as the candidate of the largest district, the smaller communities will never trust the new administration. Consider a two-year interim superintendent from outside the region while the new board develops a permanent search process together.

* **Publish a "Consolidation Commitments Dashboard" and require annual public reporting.** At the moment of consolidation approval, publish a one-page list of specific commitments: community use hours guaranteed, program offerings preserved, facility decisions deferred for X years, tax rate caps, LCAC meeting frequency. Every year for the first five years, the board reports publicly on each commitment: met, partially met, or missed — with explanation. If a commitment is missed, there's a defined remediation process. This turns abstract governance promises into trackable accountability, and gives skeptical voters a specific instrument to hold the new board to account rather than relying on goodwill.
