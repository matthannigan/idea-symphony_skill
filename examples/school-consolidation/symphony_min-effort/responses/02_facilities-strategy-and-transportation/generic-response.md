# Brainstorming Responses: Facilities Strategy and Transportation

---

## Question 6: Facility Decision Framework

* **Score buildings on a weighted five-factor rubric before any public meeting.** Assign weights: utilization rate (25%), structural condition and deferred maintenance cost (25%), geographic centrality to student population (20%), remediation cost for known issues like asbestos or lead (20%), and community anchoring function (10%). Apply the weights mechanically to all seven buildings and publish the raw scores before political pressure shapes the conversation. The rubric forces explicit tradeoffs — a building that scores high on community anchoring but low on condition and utilization can't hide that tension behind vague talk of "our school." The 10% weight on community role is real but bounded, so advocates for beloved buildings must compete on merits, not just emotion.

* **Separate "which buildings stay open as schools" from "which buildings the district retains."** A building can be closed as an instructional facility while remaining district-owned and repurposed — this distinction dramatically reduces political resistance because closing a school is no longer synonymous with abandoning a community. Run two parallel scoring processes: one for educational functionality (enrollment concentration, grade-band fit, building capacity vs. projected headcount) and one for asset disposition (sell, lease, repurpose, hold). Keeping these tracks separate prevents the loudest closure opponent from conflating "we lose the school" with "we lose everything."

* **Commission an independent building condition assessment with dollar-specific findings.** The $4.2M in deferred maintenance is a combined figure — break it down per building, with 10-year projected cost curves. A building that needs a $900K roof in year 2 and $400K in mechanical work in year 5 looks very different from one with $1.3M spread evenly over 15 years. These numbers give the board a defensible, non-political basis to prioritize closures: buildings with front-loaded, non-deferrable capital requirements are clear candidates for closure regardless of community sentiment.

* **Grant formal standing to a citizens' facilities advisory committee, not just public comment.** Assign three parent/community representatives, one union rep, and two fiscal watchdogs to a standing committee that reviews the rubric scores and can submit a minority report before the board votes. Giving stakeholders a structured role in the process — with actual documents to review, not just a microphone at a public hearing — converts opponents into participants and makes any final decision harder to dismiss as a backroom deal. The committee's input is advisory; the board retains authority.

## Question 7: Closure Sequencing and Politics

* **Close the building with the fewest students and clearest fiscal case in year one, before consolidation is complete.** Identify the single building where the numbers are most indefensible — lowest utilization, highest per-pupil maintenance cost, smallest enrollment — and move students in the year prior to full consolidation. This proves the district can execute a closure without catastrophe, reduces resistance to subsequent closures by establishing a track record, and captures real savings before the political window for action narrows. Frame it as a "right-sizing" step during consolidation planning, not as a closure at all.

* **Phase closures to one per school year, with a two-year buffer between the first and second.** Stagger closures so each affected community has time to see that students land well, programs continue, and the town doesn't collapse before the next closure is announced. The two-year buffer is not wasted time — use it to document the first closure's outcomes (travel time impacts, program participation data, parent satisfaction surveys) and publish the results. Communities watching from the sidelines will trust the process more if they see evidence rather than promises. This trades speed for coalition durability.

* **Announce closure sequencing with a binding trigger mechanism tied to enrollment thresholds.** Instead of the board deciding each year which building to close, publish a predetermined rule: "When enrollment in Building X falls below 80 students for two consecutive years, it enters a closure review process with a 12-month community engagement period." This removes the appearance of arbitrary political targeting. The board sets the rule; the demographics trigger the review. Parents in communities with healthy enrollment feel protected; parents in declining communities have advance warning with time to plan.

* **Offer the community of the first-closed building a visible compensating benefit before the closure vote.** Negotiate a concrete reinvestment in the closing town — a new pre-K program housed in the existing building, a commitment to keep the athletic fields maintained for community use, or priority hiring of laid-off staff at the consolidated district. The compensating benefit must be specific, funded, and announced simultaneously with the closure decision. Generic promises of "we'll work together" do not close the political gap; a signed memorandum with a dollar figure does.

## Question 8: Repurposing vs. Disposal

* **Convert one closed building into a district-operated early childhood center.** Rural districts consistently face gaps in pre-K access, and a consolidated district with 1,800 students likely has unmet demand for ages 3-4. Repurposing a closed K-12 building as a Head Start/pre-K hub serves multiple goals: it keeps the building in use and prevents deterioration, generates potential revenue through state early childhood grants, and lets the district honestly say it is expanding services in the community even as it closes the school. The building's existing cafeteria, gym, and classrooms need minimal reconfiguration for early childhood use.

* **Lease closed buildings to nonprofits or municipal governments at below-market rates in exchange for maintenance obligations.** Structure a 10-year lease where the tenant (library system, county health department, arts organization) pays a nominal annual rent but accepts full responsibility for routine maintenance and utilities. The district sheds the carrying cost of an empty building — estimated at $40–80K/year per building in utilities, insurance, and minimal upkeep — while preserving community use and avoiding the political cost of selling to a private buyer. Include a right-of-first-refusal clause so the district can reacquire the building if enrollments unexpectedly recover.

* **Sell buildings outright in markets where commercial demand exists, and reinvest proceeds in the highest-need remaining facilities.** Not every rural town has a nonprofit or agency ready to absorb a 40,000 sq ft building. Where genuine commercial or residential buyers exist, a clean sale at fair market value is more fiscally rational than subsidizing occupancy that doesn't generate program value. Apply the proceeds to the $4.2M deferred maintenance backlog in the buildings the district retains — this gives fiscal conservatives a concrete return and prevents the "we sold the school" narrative from sticking if residents can see the money flowing back into classrooms.

* **Mothball buildings in a documented standby status rather than committing immediately to sale or repurpose.** Given that enrollment projections carry real uncertainty, a district that sells a building in year two and then needs space in year eight faces an expensive problem. Mothballing — winterizing systems, maintaining the structure, securing the property — costs roughly $15–25K/year per building but preserves optionality. Set a five-year review trigger: if no viable repurpose has materialized and enrollment projections have not changed, then move to disposition. This is the conservative play for buildings where the community is most attached and the district least certain of long-term needs.

## Question 9: Transportation Cost Modeling

* **Build a per-route cost model before, not after, the facility decision.** The mistake most consolidation feasibility studies make is modeling transportation as a fixed percentage increase on the existing budget rather than building route-by-route cost projections under each facility scenario. Commission GIS-based route modeling for each of the realistic building configurations (e.g., keep 4 buildings vs. keep 3 vs. keep 2). The model should output: total annual route miles, number of buses required, driver hours, and fuel cost — under both current diesel pricing and a 20% fuel cost stress scenario. Without this analysis, the $1.2M administrative savings claim cannot be evaluated against transportation cost increases with any credibility.

* **Identify the crossover point at which transportation spending consumes administrative savings.** If the district saves $1.2M in administrative overhead but adds $800K in annual transportation costs (new routes, additional buses, longer driver shifts), the net fiscal benefit of consolidation shrinks to $400K — a figure that may not justify transition costs and political capital. Calculate this crossover explicitly and publish it. If transportation adds more than ~$900K annually, the case for consolidation rests almost entirely on the 12% per-pupil funding increase from the state formula, not on operational savings. Decision-makers need to know which variable is doing the work.

* **Model the capital cost of fleet expansion separately from operating costs.** Longer routes in a larger geographic district may require 2–4 additional buses at $80–120K each, plus a proportional increase in spare parts inventory and maintenance staffing. These are one-time capital costs that show up in year one or two, not operating costs that amortize over time. A consolidation plan that reports only annual operating savings without flagging $300–400K in required fleet investment in year one is misleading. Present a full 10-year financial model that shows the investment curve alongside the savings curve, with a break-even year clearly labeled.

* **Test whether a hub-and-spoke model with community transfer points can contain route costs.** Rather than running district buses door-to-door across a larger geographic area, establish 3–4 community pickup hubs (a church parking lot, a town hall lot, an existing school that remains open) where parents drop students by 7:15 AM. District buses run express routes between hubs and school buildings. This model reduces total route miles by 20–35% in comparable rural consolidations, cuts driver hours, and keeps fleet size stable. The tradeoff: it requires parents in remote areas to drive to a hub, which is a real equity concern that must be addressed (see Question 10).

## Question 10: Rural Access and Equity

* **Quantify the actual time-burden increase before claiming mitigation strategies are sufficient.** Map every student's current door-to-school travel time against projected travel times under each facility configuration. Segment by grade band — a 75-minute bus ride affects a kindergartner differently than a high schooler. Then overlay extracurricular schedules: if after-school sports or rehearsals end at 5:30 PM and a student's home is 55 minutes away, they arrive home at 6:25 PM. Do this math explicitly for the 50 most remote students. "Transportation equity" is an abstraction until you have the 90th-percentile travel time figure in hand. Many consolidation plans promise mitigation before knowing how bad the problem actually is.

* **Negotiate extended-day bus runs for extracurricular activities into the consolidation agreement from day one.** The most common equity failure in rural consolidations is that activity buses for sports, music, and clubs get cut within two years because they're expensive and easy to trim. Prevent this by building a dedicated activity transportation budget — funded at a specific dollar amount, say $85K/year — into the consolidated district's inaugural budget resolution, with a board policy requiring a supermajority vote to reduce it below that floor. Lock in the commitment when political pressure to demonstrate goodwill is highest, before cost pressures accumulate.

* **Establish a structured family engagement fund for remote communities.** Increased travel time suppresses parent participation at school events not because parents don't care but because a 40-minute round trip to attend a 30-minute classroom event is a different calculus than a 10-minute drive. Counter this by creating a small family engagement grant program — $500–1,500 per community per year — that local parent groups can use to fund satellite events closer to home: a teacher visits the community center for conferences, the principal hosts a town hall at the fire station. The fund costs $10–20K/year district-wide and preserves community connection that travel time would otherwise erode.

* **Pilot a late-start or compressed-schedule option for the most remote grade bands.** If the furthest students are predominantly in grades 6-12, consider whether a 9:00 AM start time for middle and high school eliminates one of the most complained-about impacts (early bus pickup times in the dark during winter months) without requiring additional buses. Many consolidated rural districts have reduced early-morning pickup pressure by staggering elementary and secondary start times by 45–60 minutes, using the same bus fleet on overlapping routes. The change costs nothing in capital, reduces family stress for remote households, and is reversible if it creates problems — making it a low-risk first mitigation step.

* **Create a community liaison position funded jointly by the district and county.** Assign one staff member — not an administrator, but a community connector — whose explicit job is to monitor equity impacts in the most remote communities, flag participation drops before they become crises, and serve as a conduit between remote families and district leadership. Fund the position at $45–55K/year, split 60/40 between the district and a county social services or community development fund. This is cheaper than any transportation subsidy and more durable than a promise in a consolidation agreement, because it creates a named human being accountable for monitoring outcomes.
