# Brainstorming Responses: Financial Viability and Fiscal Modeling

---

## Question 1: True Cost Calculus

*Beyond the $1.2M administrative savings estimate, what one-time transition costs — severance, system integration, legal fees, facility remediation — and over what payback period does consolidation become net-positive?*

* **Build a transition cost inventory by category, not a lump sum.** Commission a line-item transition budget covering at minimum: (1) severance and buyout for eliminated administrative positions — estimate 2x annual salary per affected employee for union contracts; (2) ERP/student information system unification — rural consolidations routinely run $400K–$800K for mid-size districts; (3) legal fees for contract reconciliation, bond restructuring, and voter campaign filings — budget $150K–$250K; (4) facility assessment and remediation costs to determine which buildings can be repurposed or sold. Without this itemization, the $1.2M savings figure floats in a vacuum and opponents will fill the vacuum with worst-case numbers.

* **Model payback period as a range, not a single point.** A 10-year fiscal model should run three scenarios — optimistic (transition costs $1.8M, payback year 3), base (transition costs $3.2M, payback year 5), and stress (transition costs $5M, enrollment decline accelerates, payback year 9+). Present all three to voters. Hiding the stress scenario invites credibility collapse when a skeptical board member produces their own math. The payback period is likely 4–7 years even in a base case, and framing it honestly ("consolidation breaks even by year 5, then saves $1.2M annually") is more persuasive than a number that looks engineered.

* **Separate one-time sunk costs from ongoing structural savings.** Some transition costs — facility studies, legal fees, staff transition — are truly one-time. Others, like transportation route redesign and curriculum alignment, have ongoing maintenance costs that offset the $1.2M figure. Track gross savings vs. net savings separately. A consolidated district may absorb one transportation director instead of three but still need additional driver time for longer routes — model this explicitly rather than assuming the headcount savings translate 1:1 to the bottom line.

* **Identify which transition costs are partially grant-funded.** Many state education departments and rural foundations (e.g., USDA Rural Development grants, state consolidation incentive funds) offer one-time consolidation assistance precisely to cover transition costs. Iowa, Kansas, and Nebraska have dedicated consolidation incentive programs in the $200K–$600K range. Filing for these funds before the vote reduces the net taxpayer exposure and is a concrete "we've already secured X" message for the campaign. Budget the transition cost net of likely grants, not gross.

## Question 2: Enrollment Decline Stress Testing

*If the projected 15% further decline by 2035 materializes (or accelerates), what per-pupil funding levels and minimum enrollment thresholds does the consolidated district need to remain solvent, and how does that compare to the status quo trajectory?*

* **Calculate the fixed-cost floor for the consolidated district.** A consolidated rural district serving 1,800 students (declining to ~1,530 by 2035) has a cost structure that is roughly 60–70% fixed — facilities, debt service, minimum staffing ratios required by state law, transportation. Identify that fixed-cost floor in dollars, then divide by projected enrollment at three points (2028, 2032, 2035) to get the minimum per-pupil revenue needed for solvency. If state formula at current rates yields $9,200/pupil and the solvency floor is $8,800/pupil at 1,500 students, the margin is thin enough to warrant a contingency plan — model it now, not when you're in crisis.

* **Compare consolidation trajectory against status quo trajectory explicitly.** The most persuasive fiscal argument for consolidation is not "consolidation is great" but "consolidation's declining path is slower than status quo's declining path." The smallest district has 2 years of fund balance left — that means insolvency in 2028 under status quo, likely triggering a state takeover with no local control. Model both trajectories on the same chart: status quo shows a cliff in 2028, consolidation shows a shallower slope with a runway to 2033 or beyond. The chart is the argument.

* **Identify enrollment trigger points that require policy responses.** Rather than treating declining enrollment as a smooth curve, identify the discrete thresholds that trigger required action: the enrollment level at which the state mandates program cuts, the level at which a second facility closure becomes necessary, the level at which athletic co-ops with neighboring districts become required to field teams. Building these trigger points into the fiscal model turns "what if enrollment drops more?" from an anxiety-inducing question into a decision tree — "if enrollment drops below X by 2030, we execute plan B."

* **Stress test with a catastrophic scenario.** Rural counties sometimes experience employer closure (a factory, a farm cooperative, a regional hospital) that compresses a decade of demographic decline into two years. Model a scenario where a major local employer closes in 2028 and enrollment drops 25% in three years. Ask: does the consolidated district survive that? What does survival require (emergency levy, facility closure, state bridge loan)? If the consolidated district's answer to this scenario is better than "three separate insolvency proceedings," that's a meaningful argument for consolidation even under extreme stress.

## Question 3: Debt and Liability Inheritance

*How should the consolidated district structure the assumption of each legacy district's bonded debt and deferred maintenance obligations — particularly the $4.2M backlog — to avoid inheriting a liability crisis on day one?*

* **Conduct a pre-consolidation facility condition assessment with legal force.** Before any merger agreement is signed, commission an independent facility assessment that creates a binding liability schedule — what deferred maintenance exists, in what buildings, at what estimated remediation cost. This document should be incorporated into the consolidation agreement so that no district can close a building quietly before merger day and transfer a hidden structural problem. The $4.2M figure is an estimate; the actual number could be higher once roofs, HVAC, and ADA compliance are assessed building by building.

* **Sequence the merger to resolve the worst liabilities before day one.** If one of the three districts holds disproportionate debt or deferred maintenance, structure the consolidation timeline so that district either sells assets, receives a state remediation grant, or places its worst buildings into a separate trust before the merger closes. This is analogous to a corporate acquisition in which a seller spins off a toxic subsidiary before the deal closes. It requires negotiation and goodwill, but it prevents the consolidated district from inheriting a liability that poisons its balance sheet on day one.

* **Issue a consolidation bond to address the deferred maintenance backlog directly.** Rather than inheriting the $4.2M as an unfunded liability, have the newly consolidated district issue a single consolidation bond — possibly supported by a state consolidation incentive — that funds remediation on a planned schedule. Voter approval of a consolidation bond at the same election as the merger vote bundles the "fix the buildings" narrative with the merger narrative. This approach converts a balance-sheet liability into a funded obligation with a repayment schedule, which is categorically different from an unfunded backlog.

* **Ring-fence legacy bonded debt by community of origin.** Where existing bonds were approved by voters in a specific community (e.g., a 2018 bond for renovating the Millbrook gymnasium), structure the consolidated district's debt so that Millbrook-area taxpayers continue to service that debt separately. This is legally achievable in most states through debt service fund accounting and is politically important — it prevents the "our taxes will pay for their old debt" argument from derailing the consolidation vote in lower-debt districts.

* **Negotiate with the state for a deferred maintenance grant as a consolidation incentive.** State education agencies often have discretionary funds for rural school infrastructure, and consolidation creates a compelling grant narrative: "three districts' worth of deferred maintenance, one district going forward." A targeted ask to the state — $1.5M in infrastructure grant funding contingent on voter approval of consolidation — reduces the day-one liability problem and gives the merger campaign a concrete win to announce before the vote.

## Question 4: Property Tax Equalization

*Given that the three districts likely have different tax rates and assessed values, what approach to property tax equalization minimizes shock to taxpayers in the lowest-tax district while meeting the consolidated district's revenue needs?*

* **Phase in rate equalization over 5–7 years rather than equalizing on day one.** The most politically dangerous moment in consolidation is when taxpayers in the lowest-rate district see their first bill from the new entity. A 5-year phase-in that raises rates by 20% of the gap per year spreads the shock, gives property owners time to plan, and allows the district to demonstrate fiscal competence before asking for more. This approach requires the consolidated district to bridge the revenue gap in early years through reserves or state transition funding — identify that bridge explicitly in the fiscal model.

* **Differentiate between operating levy rates and bond levy rates.** Taxpayers in different districts may face different rate shocks depending on which levy type drives the gap. Operating levy equalization is more politically sensitive than bond levy equalization, because bonds have defined endpoints and voters understand they're paying for something specific. Separate the equalization conversation: "your bond levy goes up X cents/thousand this year to cover the Millbrook gym bond; your operating levy phases up Y cents/thousand over five years" is a more defensible message than a single blended number.

* **Model the impact at the household level, not just the millage rate.** Millage rates mean nothing to most voters. Translate the equalization impact into annual dollar amounts for a median-value home in each district — "a homeowner with a $120,000 assessed value in Riverdale will pay $47 more per year in year one, $93 more in year two." This transparency pre-empts opponent messaging and lets supporters answer the question at the door. If the lowest-tax district's homeowners are paying an extra $50/year for significantly better educational programming, that's a different conversation than "your taxes are going up."

* **Use assessed value growth as a natural equalizer where possible.** In some rural districts, assessed value growth in one community (due to new development, wind energy infrastructure, or commercial investment) can allow rate reductions even as the levy dollar amount holds steady. If any of the three districts sits in an area with recent taxable investment — e.g., a wind farm added to the tax base — model whether the rate gap naturally narrows over 5 years without requiring rate increases at all in the lowest-tax district. This is an underused argument: "we may not need to raise Riverdale's rate at all if growth continues."

## Question 5: State Funding Timing

*The 12% per-pupil funding increase from the state formula takes effect at what point in the consolidation process, and how does the district bridge cash flow during the transition window before that revenue arrives?*

* **Identify the exact state funding trigger date and work backward to set the merger timeline.** State education funding formulas typically recalculate once per year — usually tied to October enrollment count dates or fiscal year starts. If the consolidation effective date is July 1, 2027, and the state recalculates funding the following October, the new district may operate for 15 months under the old, fragmented funding rates before the 12% increase materializes. Know that date precisely. Then engineer the merger timeline to minimize the gap — if a January 1 effective date triggers the formula recalculation 9 months sooner than a July 1 date, the calendar choice alone could be worth $400K–$500K in earlier revenue.

* **Build a cash flow bridge using state transition loans and existing reserves.** Most states with active rural consolidation programs offer short-term, low-interest bridge loans specifically for the funding gap period. Apply for these before the vote, with conditional approval pending voter authorization. Additionally, audit the current fund balances of all three districts — even districts running structural deficits may have restricted funds (e.g., capital project funds, insurance reserves) that can be temporarily redirected to operating cash under a consolidation transition plan. A combined bridge loan plus reserve drawdown strategy can cover 12–15 months of gap without a tax increase.

* **Negotiate with the state for a consolidation-year funding guarantee.** Rather than accepting the standard formula timing, negotiate directly with the state education department for a one-year funding guarantee at the consolidated rate, retroactive to the merger effective date. Several states have made this accommodation for rural consolidations because the alternative — a district insolvency proceeding — is more expensive for the state. Frame the ask as: "give us the consolidated rate in year one; you recover the cost in years two through five from reduced state intervention spending." This is a political negotiation, not just a regulatory one, and requires the county board to engage state legislative allies.

* **Treat the funding gap as a known, bounded risk, not an open-ended uncertainty.** The cash flow gap has a maximum duration (one funding cycle, likely 12–15 months) and a maximum dollar amount (12% of per-pupil funding times enrollment, spread over that period — roughly $300K–$500K). Naming the number — "we need to bridge up to $400K for 14 months" — is more reassuring than leaving the gap undefined. Voters and bond markets respond better to bounded problems with identified solutions than to vague transition risks. The feasibility study should include a cash flow projection by month for the first 24 months post-merger, with the bridge strategy mapped explicitly against it.
