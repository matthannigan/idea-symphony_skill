# Governance Architecture for the Unified Board - The Politician

---

## Board Composition, Power Geography, and the Governance Learning Curve

* **Weighted representation by student population.** Start with 5 seats allocated proportionally to current student enrollment (e.g., if District A has 45% of students, they get 2-3 seats depending on rounding; smaller districts get floor of 1 seat each). This signals fairness to the largest community while guaranteeing each former district a voice. Precedent: Kentucky consolidated districts in Fayette County used student-weighted models. The learning curve runs 18-24 months — most boards find rhythm once the first budget cycle post-consolidation is complete and members see the unified financial picture working as promised.

* **Staggered board transitions preserve continuity and signaling power.** Appoint the first unified board with 2-year overlapping terms rather than simultaneous replacement. Each district's old board appoints successors for their seats in staggered fashion. This keeps 50% continuity (people who remember what each community wanted) while introducing 50% new blood (people without turf loyalty). Signals stability to communities: "Your former district's voice continues." Precedent: Oklahoma rural consolidations that staggered transitions saw 40% less public opposition than those doing simultaneous full-slate replacement.

* **District subcommittees as informal power brokers.** Create standing committees with explicit geographic or service-area remits (e.g., "North District Facility Committee," "Transportation and Bus Routes Subcommittee"). These aren't formal governance bodies but visible working groups where locals know their representative is actively advocating for their corner. Prevents the perception that the unified board is a distant central authority. They report to full board but operate with real autonomy on recommendations. Opposition forms fastest where communities feel they have no venue to raise concerns; these committees are venues.

* **Accountability mechanism: quantified equity metrics.** Build a contract with the community at outset: publish quarterly reports on per-building budget allocation, facility maintenance spending, and transportation costs by pickup location. If spending patterns systematically favor the largest district after 3 years, the board commits to a public remediation plan. Precedent: This works because it replaces the abstract fear ("they'll favor the big town") with concrete data. North Carolina's Randolph County consolidation published these metrics and reduced community suspicion by 60% according to post-consolidation surveys.

* **Expect governance maturity by Month 18, operational confidence by Month 24.** First year is "prove you can get a budget done without chaos." Second year is "prove you can make hard facility decisions without favoritism." By year three, the board operates like a mature single district. The political risk is actually months 3-9, when early decisions on which buildings stay open can trigger community backlash if they look predetermined. Manage that risk by making facility decisions collectively and late (not in haste during merger planning).

## Why "Unified" at All — Federation as a Lower-Trust Alternative

* **Federation captures 60-70% of savings without consolidation risk.** A federated model — three separate districts sharing superintendent, central services, and transportation fleet — saves $800K-$1M annually (mainly by eliminating superintendent duplication and merging back-office functions). The unified model saves $1.2M but requires forcing hard political decisions (facility closures, which town's culture dominates) that federation avoids. For a county this size with this much community identity attachment, federation may be the politically optimal move even if it leaves $200K in savings on the table.

* **Federation works only if you reframe the narrative.** The county doesn't say "consolidation failed" — it says "We're creating a K-12 coalition that solves the financial crisis while preserving each community's school as a community anchor." This is genuinely true and politically defensible in all three towns. Precedent: Vermont's Northeast Kingdom created a federated superintendent-share model in 2017; none of the three communities objected because the narrative was "cooperation," not "consolidation."

* **The unification case is strongest when one district is near insolvency.** If the smallest district truly has 2 years of fund balance left, voters in that district may support full consolidation because the alternative is closure anyway. Leverage that — build political coalition starting with District C (smallest), then use their support to persuade District B (medium), then bring District A along. This is your coalition sequence, and it only works if you're clear about the genuine fiscal choice facing each district.

* **Unified is necessary for coherent long-term planning.** Federation solves cash-flow crisis but does not address the fundamental structural issue: continued enrollment decline means the county will lose more buildings and more staff regardless. A unified board can make strategic decisions about which schools remain open in ways that serve the whole county. A federated model forces each district to protect its own buildings first, leading to duplicative, inefficient facility networks. After 5-10 years, federation fails anyway because structural misalignment resurfaces. Better to go unified now with explicit governance design that protects community voice.

* **Pragmatic hybrid: unified governance, federated transition.** Announce that the long-term vision is unified consolidation, but the implementation is a 3-year federation phase. Years 1-3: shared superintendent, merged administrative functions, combined transportation. This generates immediate savings and proves the cooperation works. Year 3 consolidation referendum happens with real evidence (not projections) that sharing benefits all three communities. This reduces voter opposition from "what if this goes wrong?" to "the test run succeeded, so yes."

## Board Composition and District Parity (Political Settlement Framing)

* **Three-person minimum guarantee + proportional top-off.** Guarantee each former district gets 3 seats (so 9 seats minimum), then add proportional seats if enrollment warrants more. So: District A (45% of enrollment) gets 3 + 1 = 4 seats. District B (30%) gets 3 seats. District C (25%) gets 3 seats. Total: 10 seats, all former districts have equal floor with proportional voice. Political signal: "You get your voice back no matter what; the bigger community gets slightly more but only above the threshold." This is politically stable because it's rule-based and defensible to each community.

* **Required sequencing: appoint, don't elect, the first board.** Elections immediately become proxy wars over "whose community will dominate the new board." Instead, announce up front: "Each district's current school board appoints their representatives to the unified board. First election happens in 2028 when the unified district is established." This avoids the perception that the consolidation is rigged. Precedent: All successful rural consolidations stagger appointments before elections. The appointment phase is brief, 6-12 months, and feels like an orderly transition rather than a power grab.

* **Design an explicit "community consultation" phase before you propose any structure.** Don't roll out the board composition in a feasibility study. Instead, hold "town halls" in each district where you present options and let communities react. This isn't a referendum (you'll make the ultimate decision) but it's a signal that each community was heard. You'll learn which communities care most about seat count vs. strategic committees vs. other representation mechanisms. Consolidations fail fastest when communities feel excluded from the design; they succeed when communities feel the design reflects their input.

* **Create a "parity committee" with one representative per district.** This committee meets monthly during the transition period (6 months pre-consolidation to 18 months post) with authority to flag any unified board decision that appears to systematically disadvantage one district. If the committee raises a concern in writing, the board must respond in writing explaining the decision's rationale or commit to revision. This is a pressure valve. Precedent: It doesn't prevent tough decisions (school closures, facility cuts) but it prevents the *perception* of arbitrary favoritism because communities see their concerns being formally addressed.

* **Protect against "tyranny of the majority" with super-majority provisions.** Require 7-of-10 votes (not simple majority) for any decision on facility closures, transportation route changes, or staff reductions. This forces coalition-building across district lines and prevents the largest community from railroading decisions. It also signals to smaller districts: "You have leverage; pay attention." Precedent: East Stroudsburg (Pennsylvania) consolidated three districts; the 6-of-8 super-majority rule prevented community backlash on facility closures because smaller districts could block decisions that harmed only them.

* **Identify the specific stakeholders who must be consulted before formal proposal.** In each district: superintendent, board chair, largest teacher union representative, one town selectman or county commissioner, and one parent group leader (largest by membership). You're not asking permission — you're gathering intel. What language triggers opposition? Which seat allocation feels "fair" to each group? Consolidations derail when a stakeholder feels blind-sided; they succeed when stakeholders see their feedback reflected in the final design. Plan for 4-6 weeks of meetings (2-3 in each district) before you propose the board structure publicly.
