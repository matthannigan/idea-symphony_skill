# Governance Architecture for the Unified Board - The Pragmatist

---

## Board Composition, Power Geography, and the Governance Learning Curve

* **Start with 7-9 member board, not 5 or 11.** Five is too small to represent three communities credibly; eleven becomes unwieldy and expensive (honoraria, scheduling, quorum logistics). Seven or nine allows for true geographic representation (2-3 per former district) with a neutral swing seat or rotating leadership. This is proven in comparable rural consolidations (e.g., upstate NY Boces structures). Build in a six-month transition period where the board explicitly focuses on operations and defers major closures; the learning curve for new board coherence typically runs 12-18 months.

* **Use geographic subdistricts with staggered four-year terms, not at-large seats.** At-large elections in rural areas default to whoever has name recognition in the largest community. Geographic subdistricts force each former district to vote for its own representatives, ensuring real local accountability. Stagger terms (rotate 2 seats every other year) so knowledge isn't lost on election cycles. Measurable outcome: track school board meeting attendance and public comment by geographic origin for the first three years—it should stay distributed, not concentrate in one town.

* **Install an explicit decision-log audit for the first two years.** Every facility decision, staffing change, and budget reallocation gets logged with the geographic impact: "How does this affect enrollment/spending/jobs in each former district?" At the 18-month mark, publish a summary showing whether major decisions skewed toward one community. This is cheap accountability that prevents the perception (and reality) of systematic favoritism that kills consolidation consent.

* **Hire a professional board facilitator for the first 12 months, not forever.** The learning curve is real—three separate governance cultures colliding. A facilitator runs the first year's meetings, coaches the board chair and superintendent on consensus-building, and exits once voting patterns stabilize. Cost: ~$20k/year. Benefit: prevents the board from locking in winner-take-all voting blocks that poison the consolidation.

* **Link board member compensation to attendance and to outcomes, not just seat-holding.** If a unified board is to succeed politically, it needs visible engagement from all three communities. Offer modest stipends ($500-1,500/year) conditional on attending 70% of meetings and serving on assigned committees. Link the board chair term limits to a mandatory annual governance survey—if two-thirds of the public thinks the board doesn't represent them, the chair is ineligible to stand for re-election.

## Why "Unified" at All — Federation as a Lower-Trust Alternative

* **Federation makes financial sense only if transportation, central IT, and shared logistics are outsourced.** The stated $1.2M savings from eliminating 3 superintendencies and administrative duplication requires a single unified operations structure. A federated model with three separate boards and three separate HR/finance functions keeps most of those costs in place. The real savings come from scale, not from ceremonial "cooperation." If the goal is to save money without consolidating governance, be honest: federation works only if you hire a single shared-services director to run operations across all three districts while keeping separate boards. That's a compromise, not a federation.

* **Federation requires ironclad legal agreements, which rural districts struggle to maintain.** Even relatively successful shared-services arrangements (e.g., a shared superintendent across two districts) often fail when one district's board votes to pull out because of a single unpopular decision. Rural school boards are locally accountable and culturally resistant to long-term contractual entanglement. If you're building a governance structure that depends on three separate boards keeping their hands off operational decisions, you're building fragility.

* **The state funding formula doesn't recognize federal structures.** Per-pupil funding improves with size; per-district overhead penalties don't go away with federation. If you keep three separate districts with three separate superintendents, you lose the $1.2M in admin savings AND you don't fully capture the state per-pupil funding uplift. Federation is a mirage—it costs more than consolidation and delivers less governance clarity.

* **Federation works only if the purpose is purely operational (back-office sharing), not educational.** If the goal is to improve teacher quality, coordinate curriculum, or manage facility utilization, federation fails because those decisions need unified authority. Three boards will deadlock on curriculum, transportation routes, and closures. If consolidation is really about "efficiency," skip federation and build a single unified structure with embedded local voice (through geographic subdistricts and explicit audit trails). That's more honest and more likely to work.

## Board Composition and District Parity (Political Settlement Framing)

* **Offer each of the three former districts a minimum of 2 seats on a 7-seat board, with the 7th as a rotating at-large seat.** This guarantees each community floor representation and signals that no district was absorbed. The 7th seat rotates annually among the three communities on a pre-set schedule; in year one, Community A holds it; year two, Community B; and so on. This gives each community a guaranteed leadership role every three years without creating a deadlocked 3-2-2 split. Test this design with each district's existing board chair and superintendent before public announcement.

* **Require super-majority (6 of 7) votes for facility closures, boundary changes, and major capital decisions for the first five years.** This reassures communities that a narrow 4-3 vote can't close "their" school and taxes can't suddenly shift to a neighboring town. After five years, default to simple majority; by then the board will have proven itself through transparent decision-making. This is a temporary political settlement, not a permanent governance feature—but it buys the consent the consolidation needs in year one.

* **Pre-negotiate the union contracts as part of the consolidation proposal, not after the vote.** The three separate teachers' unions will be skeptical of unified governance. Before you propose a board structure, work with each union to draft a unified contract that preserves step-and-salary progression, protects tenure rights across the consolidated entity, and addresses transfer/relocation issues. Include union representatives in the board design phase, not as an afterthought. If the unions buy in, community buy-in follows.

* **Consult each town's selectboard (or town council equivalent) before publicly proposing the governance structure, not after.** Rural communities care what town leadership thinks. Schedule a meeting with each town's selectboard, explain the governance design, invite feedback on representation and decision-making, and incorporate what you learn. If a selectboard feels cut out of the process, they'll tell voters the consolidation is a state power-grab, even if the governance structure is fair. Political settlement requires real consultation.

* **Build an explicit sunset clause into the initial governance structure: reassess board composition after three years.** Propose a five-member committee (one from each former district, plus two at-large) to review whether the board structure is working and propose changes by the end of year three. This signals flexibility and gives communities a voice in adaptation. If one community genuinely feels underrepresented after three years of lived experience, the structure can change. That adaptability is the best way to hold consent.
