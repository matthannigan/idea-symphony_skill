# Financial Case, Transition Costs, and Tax Burden - Pragmatist

---

## Break-Even Horizon and Savings Stress Test

* **Itemize transition costs upfront, phase savings realization.** The $1.2M administrative savings claim needs a detailed two-year cost accounting: severance for 3 superintendents (~$300K), union negotiation/legal (~$150K), IT systems integration and dual-run costs (~$250K), staff retraining (~$100K), and facilities consolidation logistics (~$200K). That's roughly $1M in Year 1 costs, meaning true net savings don't appear until Year 2-3 at best. A practical approach: conduct a 90-day operational audit that isolates which administrative functions are genuinely redundant versus those that reflect local statutory obligations or union protections—be realistic about which savings are achievable.

* **Build a tiered savings waterfall tied to actual decisions.** Don't present savings as a fixed number. Instead, model three scenarios: conservative (only HR consolidation realized, ~$400K), moderate (HR + some transportation overlap, ~$700K), and optimistic (full administrative reduction, $1.2M). Attach each scenario to specific decisions that must be made—which superintendent positions dissolve, which transportation director roles merge. This shows decision-makers where savings come from and what trade-offs are required.

* **Model negative sensitivity: what if savings take twice as long?** Create a five-year pro forma that shows breakeven under delayed realization—if administrative consolidation takes two years instead of one, or if half the savings prove unrealistic once union contracts are honored. Frame this not as pessimism but as prudent scenario planning. Fiscal conservatives will support you more if you've already thought about what happens if the optimistic case fails.

## Per-Pupil Formula Dependency and Long-Fuse Risk

* **Separate the 12% bonus from the baseline operating model.** The per-pupil funding boost is real but contingent. Build the financial model with two distinct revenue buckets: baseline operations (assume current formula, apply conservative enrollment decline, say 15% over 10 years), and the consolidation bonus (treat as a 3-5 year window unless the state commits to longer extension). Calculate the consolidated district's break-even point without the bonus. If consolidation only works with the bonus, that's a red flag you need to solve before asking voters to approve.

* **Request a state formula commitment letter, with duration.** A practical next step: ask the state education department in writing whether the 12% bonus: (a) is automatic post-consolidation or requires application, (b) continues indefinitely or has a sunset, and (c) is inflation-adjusted. Get a 5-year commitment in writing if possible. This gives voters concrete assurance rather than relying on assumption.

* **Model the 2035 scenario to stress-test sustainability.** If enrollment is down 15% by 2035 and the district has 1,530 students instead of 1,800, what's the per-pupil cost? At $15,555 per pupil today, a 15% enrollment drop means per-pupil costs rise unless operating expenses fall proportionally—which they won't (transportation, special education, debt service are partially fixed). Work backward from 2035 to identify what unit-economics decisions must be locked in now (which facilities close? what staff reductions are assumed?) to stay solvent in that scenario.

## Deferred Maintenance Debt Trap and Stranded-Cost Accounting

* **Conduct a building-by-building condition audit with three-year refresh timeline.** The $4.2M figure is a snapshot; you need a five-year projection showing which roofs, HVAC systems, and boiler plants will fail next. Get a third-party condition assessment for each of seven buildings. Then make the hard call: some buildings will be closed, so don't sink money into their deferred maintenance. Others will be the "hubs" for the new district—invest in those. The rest are candidates for divestiture or contingency reserves. Pragmatic frame: "We're making facility decisions based on a five-year capital plan, not pretending the backlog doesn't exist."

* **Ring-fence closed-building costs and communicate them explicitly.** If a building is being closed, the stranded maintenance liability (roof, foundation issues) either becomes a community severance cost (accept the liability, spend the money upfront, close with dignity) or a write-off (the county's facilities group absorbs it, or the community keeps the building and its own maintenance burden). Be transparent about which scenario applies to each facility. This prevents the inherited backlog from being quietly re-deferred under new governance—if it's on the books, it's harder to ignore.

* **Structure a three-year "facilities triage" contingency reserve.** Instead of pretending the backlog vanishes, build into the consolidated budget a $300-400K annual contingency line explicitly for deferred-maintenance surprises (a deteriorated roof discovered during a building inspection, asbestos remediation). This is psychologically different from an open-ended obligation—it's a time-bound, capped commitment. After three years, any remaining deferred maintenance becomes a full-budget conversation, not a hidden liability.

## Property Tax Equalization Across Asymmetric Tax Bases

* **Map current mill levies and effective tax rates by district before modeling scenarios.** You need a tax-base reality check: District A might have a 0.8% effective residential rate while District B has 1.1%, depending on commercial property concentration, farmland assessment, and historical levy choices. Consolidation will equalize these, but equalization is political dynamite. A practical approach: build a transparent tax impact table showing: under current system (do nothing), under full equalization (one district-wide mill levy), and under a "blended approach" (temporary transition with a phase-in period of 5-7 years toward equalization). Show residential and commercial property owners the dollar impact per $100K of assessed value.

* **Identify and quantify the tax winners and losers early.** Some communities will pay more tax after consolidation, others less. That's inevitable, but you can minimize opposition by: (a) identifying which communities absorb the largest increase, (b) quantifying that increase transparently, (c) proposing transition mechanisms like temporary hold-harmless periods for the highest-impact communities. Frame this as fairness, not burden-shifting: "We're moving toward equal treatment, and here's how we'll manage the transition so no community bears an unfair shock."

* **Explore earmarked revenue sharing for high-impact communities.** If a community faces a significant tax increase, propose that a portion of consolidation savings are returned to that community via facility improvements, athletic programs, or technology investments. This converts a tax increase into a net-plus investment story. It's practical because it uses real dollars (from administrative savings) rather than abstract promises.

## Hospital-System "Affiliate Before You Absorb" Sequencing

* **Pilot operational unification in low-stakes functions before full merger.** Apply the hospital parallel concretely: before the consolidation vote, propose a 12-month "operational alliance" where the three districts agree to unified purchasing (supplies, transportation fuel, special-education services, IT platforms). This is pre-consolidation and voluntary—communities maintain their identity and governance, but reap some efficiency gains and prove the operational model works. By the time the consolidation vote happens, stakeholders have already experienced cost savings and operational benefits without losing local control of curriculum or building decisions.

* **Preserve local school branding and management under unified back-office.** Rural communities care deeply about "their school"—the building, the sports program, the sense of ownership. Unify transportation, food services, special education, and HR at the district level, but let each school keep its name, principal, and some autonomy on how the budget is deployed. The parallel to hospital affiliates is direct: local branding and identity are preserved while achieving back-office efficiency. This changes the emotional story from "we're losing our school" to "we're joining a network."

* **Create a phased timeline that reaches consolidation vote only after proving the model.** Don't ask for a permanent commitment before demonstrating temporary benefits. Run the 12-month alliance, publish savings and operational improvements, then ask for consolidation approval in Year 2. This is psychologically powerful: voters are supporting something they've already experienced working, not betting on promises.

## Municipal Bond Refinancing and Debt-Assumption Models

* **Audit each district's debt portfolio by maturity, creditor, and covenant.** Each district has bonds with different maturity dates, interest rates, and creditor requirements. Some bonds may have "change of control" covenants that trigger refinancing or accelerated payoff if consolidation occurs. Before the consolidation vote, get a detailed debt analysis: what are the true costs of assuming each district's obligations? Are there refinancing opportunities (can consolidation allow you to refinance at lower rates due to increased credit strength)? Which debt carries the highest interest burden and should be prioritized for early payoff?

* **Design debt assumption in tranches to minimize credit-rating risk.** Consolidation creates a larger, potentially stronger credit entity, but only if managed carefully. A practical approach: (a) assume all debt on day one to avoid the appearance of selective burden-shifting, but (b) refinance in tranches over 2-3 years as the consolidated district proves operational stability. This avoids the perception that one community is "bailing out" another's debt while allowing credit markets to re-rate the new entity based on actual performance.

* **Use facility closure decisions to offset debt in politically sensitive communities.** If a community will bear a disproportionate property-tax increase due to inherited debt, offer a facility improvement or capital project funded by proceeds from selling closed buildings or consolidation savings. This is not subsidy—it's using real consolidated-district savings to mitigate local impact. It converts abstract debt concerns into concrete investment the community benefits from.

## Fiscal Conservative Persuasion Path

* **Publish a Consolidation Fiscal Accountability Agreement with enforceable mechanisms.** Don't rely on trust; build mechanisms. Propose: (a) independent annual audit of consolidation savings vs. projections, with public reporting, (b) a binding three-year "savings target"—if consolidated district realizes only 70% of promised savings, efficiency gains cannot be spent on new programs, must return to taxpayers via property-tax credits, (c) a sunset clause: if the consolidated district runs a deficit in any year, the consolidation agreement can be revisited (lower stakes, but credible commitment to fiscal discipline). Fiscal conservatives respect defined, auditable commitments over vague promises.

* **Establish a Finance Oversight Committee with fiscal-conservative representation.** Before consolidation, propose a standing five-person committee (include a CPA, a local business owner, a parent, and two elected officials) with power to: (a) review annual budgets, (b) certify that promised efficiencies are being realized, (c) recommend property-tax adjustments if savings targets are exceeded. This committee reports quarterly to the board and annually to the public. It's a structural assurance that fiscal discipline is embedded, not aspirational.

* **Start with a mini-consolidation pilot to build credibility.** Instead of asking for a leap of faith, propose a limited consolidation first: merge transportation systems across all three districts for a single school year under a shared director. Measure savings, prove the model, then ask for full consolidation. When fiscal conservatives have evidence of cost control from a tangible pilot, resistance becomes harder to sustain. This is the pragmatist's path: prove it works before asking for permanent commitment.
