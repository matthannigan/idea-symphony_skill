# Voter Approval and the Three-District Coalition - The Audience Advocate

---

## The Holdout Problem, Asymmetric Incentives, and Defining a Real Win

* **Reframe "winning" from passage to actual households feeling better off.** Don't design a consolidation that passes by the slimmest margin — design one where residents in each district concretely experience why their community benefits. Ask residents in each district directly: "What would have to be true for you to feel confident about consolidation?" This isn't a yes/no question. Listen for the specific fears (Will our kids get moved? Will the new superintendent understand our valley?). Then design the consolidation *around* those fears. A 55% yes vote in one district signals coercion, not consensus; aim for 70%+ in all three, which requires each district to feel like the proposal was *designed for them*, not imposed on them.

* **Make each district's financial timeline visible and personal.** The smallest district has 2 years of fund balance remaining — but families don't experience fund balance. They experience: "Will my child finish 8th grade here, or will the school close mid-semester?" Conduct town halls where residents hear a financial officer explain, in plain language, what happens in year 1, year 2, and year 3 under status quo (no consolidation). Then show what happens if you consolidate. Show it in a way that answers: "Will my property taxes go down? Will my school building stay open? Will I know my kids' teachers?" Different communities have different answers to these questions, and the proposal needs to reflect that.

* **Identify the hidden deal-breakers in each community that no amount of messaging can fix.** In some districts, it's identity (the school *is* the town). In others, it's distrust of adjacent communities ("Those kids are different from ours"). In others, it's union anxieties. Don't campaign uniformly on financial efficiency. Campaign on the real fear that each community has. For the identity-protective community, emphasize that consolidation preserves the school building *and* adds resources to make it thrive. For the distrusting community, bring young people from all three districts together *before* the vote to show what collaboration looks like. For union households, guarantee that consolidation doesn't mean pink slips or salary cuts.

* **Acknowledge what consolidation actually costs this community, don't hide it.** If consolidation means the superintendent's house is torn down because the new district headquarters isn't there, say so. If it means the transportation director who's worked there 20 years gets laid off (even with severance), acknowledge it. Communities can live with hard choices — they can't live with being lied to about the choices. The people you're asking to vote for consolidation know who will win and who will lose under the new structure. Honesty about winners and losers builds trust for the other claims you're making.

## Phased Consolidation as a Winnability Hedge

* **Two districts consolidating first creates a proof-of-concept that third district can see working in real time.** Instead of asking all three communities to leap simultaneously, consolidate districts A and B first. Let their residents see: Does transportation actually improve? Do teachers stay? Do programs expand or shrink? Does a retired teacher from the consolidated district speak at town halls in district C and say, "I was scared too, but here's what actually happened"? This is worth more than any consultant report. Community members believe other community members over administrators.

* **Phased consolidation lets third district negotiate from the position of observer, not hostage.** If all three must vote yes simultaneously, district C has maximum leverage but also maximum fear (both other districts could abandon them). If districts A and B consolidate first, district C can watch for 6-12 months, then negotiate knowing exactly what they're joining. This reduces the uncertainty tax — the fear premium that makes residents vote no just to protect themselves.

* **The challenge: legal ambiguity around phased approach needs direct conversation with residents.** Some state education officials may say, "You can't phase it — state law requires all three to merge together." But the community needs to hear what "you can't" actually means: Is it a hard legal bar, or a funding mechanism that incentivizes simultaneous consolidation? If it's a funding issue, what would the districts get/lose by phasing? Be transparent about this constraint rather than letting residents suspect they're being forced. If state officials genuinely prefer simultaneous consolidation, have them explain why on camera to community members.

* **Phased approach introduces new political risk: communities that consolidate first become the "test case."** District A and B residents might feel angry that they're the guinea pigs for district C's benefit. Plan for this. District A and B voters need to feel like they're building something visionary, not beta-testing for others. Frame the first consolidation as "the founding cohort of the new district," not as a pilot program waiting for district C's approval.

## Supermajority Dynamics from Legislative Coalition-Building

* **Map the "early converter" in each district and bring them into the conversation 6 months before the vote.** You need the retired principal who parents still trust, the local pastor, the newspaper editor, the business owner who employs teachers as part-time staff. These people don't follow superintendent announcements. They hear from neighbors. Ask each: "What would you need to understand about consolidation to feel comfortable recommending it to people you know?" Then listen. These early converters will tell you what the real objections are (hint: it's not usually the per-pupil funding formula).

* **Create a "messenger committee" that looks like the community, not like the district administration.** When the superintendent explains consolidation, people hear "top-down mandate." When a teacher from district A (who was skeptical but is now convinced) explains it to parents in district B, people listen. Recruit 3-5 people from each district who've genuinely changed their minds about consolidation. Give them talking points, yes — but also give them permission to say "I was scared about X, and here's why I'm not anymore." That's more persuasive than any official presentation.

* **Sequence your endorsements to create positive momentum: get early yes from union leadership, visible school board members, and superintendents.**  If people see that teachers and school boards (the people closest to the actual work) support consolidation, late-deciding families feel safer saying yes. If teachers and school boards stay silent or divided, families assume there's hidden downside. The union contract negotiations need to happen *before* the public campaign so union leadership can publicly say, "We got a deal that protects our members."

* **Watch for the "cascade of doubt" — if a respected community figure goes public with concerns, it gives permission for other skeptics to organize.** Identify potential credible critics early. Are they actually opposed, or do they have legitimate questions that can be answered? Have private conversations with them *before* they become public opponents. Sometimes this means modifying the proposal based on their feedback. Sometimes it means they stay skeptical, but at least they know you listened.

## Who Must Vote Yes

* **Segment each district's voters by actual concerns, not demographic bins.** One district has fiscal conservatives who'll say yes if the budget math works. Another has parents worried about school closures. A third has union households worried about job security. The minimum winning coalition in each district *looks different*. In district A, you need 60% of fiscal conservatives + 50% of young parents = yes. In district B, you need 70% of longtime residents + 40% of union households = yes. Model this out specifically by talking to residents in small groups, not surveys. Ask: "What would have to be true for you to vote yes?"

* **Identify the persuadable middle in each district and understand what makes them feel safe.** Not everyone who'll vote yes is a true believer. Many will vote yes if they feel like the process was fair, their questions were heard, and someone they trust says it's okay. In small communities, this often means: Did the superintendent hold real town halls? Did she answer hard questions? Did she admit what she doesn't know? Did local leaders endorse it? People vote yes when they feel heard, even if they're still nervous.

* **The elderly and longtime residents in each district have outsized influence on turnout and social permission.** If long-term residents say, "I've been here 40 years and I'm voting yes because I believe in our community's future," that changes the emotional tenor of the vote. But these residents need to be convinced first, not attacked for being backward-looking. They're not opposed to consolidation — they're opposed to losing their community. Show them consolidation strengthens their community, and they become your strongest advocates.

* **Make it safe to change your mind publicly.** Someone who said "I'm against consolidation" last year needs to feel like saying "I'm now voting yes" doesn't make them look foolish or disloyal. Give them an honorable bridge: "The district listened to my concerns and modified the plan. Here's what changed." This is why honest acknowledgment of what the proposal actually costs is so important — it gives persuadable people permission to shift positions.

## Asymmetric Opposition Mapping

* **Don't assume the same opposition message works across all three districts.** In the small district, opposition is identity-driven: "Our superintendent has known every kid's name for 20 years." In the medium district, it's fiscal skepticism: "I don't trust the combined district won't spend the savings on bureaucracy." In the largest district, it's job security: "Teacher layoffs happen in consolidations." Create a distinct opposition counter-argument for each. In district 1, show how the consolidated superintendent can know kids through smaller school hubs. In district 2, show the specific budget controls that protect per-school spending. In district 3, show the contract language guaranteeing no forced layoffs.

* **Surface the opposition arguments in public first, before they're weaponized.** Hold public forums where opponents speak their actual concerns (not strawman versions of opposition). Then respond publicly to those specific concerns. This does two things: it shows fence-sitters that the district is willing to hear criticism, and it prevents opposition from feeling persecuted (which actually makes them organize harder). A suppressed concern becomes underground fuel for opposition. An acknowledged concern can be addressed or accepted.

* **Map past broken promises in each district — this is the opposition ammunition you're not talking about.** Was there a previous consolidation attempt? Did a superintendent promise to keep a building open and then close it? Did a tax increase happen and nobody saw what it paid for? These ghosts determine whether residents believe you *now*. In communities with recent broken promises, you need more transparency, not better messaging. Show detailed plans for facility decisions *before* the vote, not after. Build trust by proving you're different from whoever disappointed them before.

* **Identify which opposition bloc in each district is actually persuadable and which is not.** Some people will vote no no matter what. Focus on the fence-sitters. In many cases, the most vocal opponents create space for quiet supporters to organize in response. Let opposition exist, don't crush it. People in the middle often feel safer saying yes when they see legitimate disagreement isn't being silenced.

## Informal Gatekeepers

* **Before any public announcement, have individual conversations with the retired superintendent, the booster club president, the pastor, and the newspaper editor.** Don't pitch consolidation to them. Ask them: "What keeps you up at night about the future of our schools?" Listen. Then ask: "If consolidation happened, what would you need to believe it was the right choice?" These conversations need to happen 6-9 months before the vote, in person, not via email or group meetings. People trust leaders who ask for their input.

* **Give informal gatekeepers real influence on the proposal, not just a speaking role.** If the pastor says, "Families are worried about after-school care with longer transportation times," modify the proposal to address after-school care. If the retired superintendent says, "New leadership needs to understand the history of how these communities were built," hire a consultant familiar with that history to brief the new superintendent. When gatekeepers see their concerns shaping the actual plan, they move from skeptical to invested.

* **The newspaper editor is both a gatekeeper and a megaphone.** Meet with her regularly. Give her access to real information, not spin. Let her ask hard questions in private so she doesn't need to ask them in print. If she becomes an ally, her coverage shifts from "Is consolidation a good idea?" to "Here's what the new district will look like." If she becomes an opponent, her coverage shifts the other way. In rural counties with one newspaper, this person is extraordinarily powerful.

* **Booster clubs, rotaries, and church leadership are where community members actually hear about schools.** Don't do an advertising campaign. Go to the booster club meeting and let parents ask questions. Go to the rotary and talk to business owners. Go to church and acknowledge the community identity piece from the pulpit. These are where informal permission gets granted. If these spaces hear accurate, honest information from a trusted source, the vote follows.

## State Agency as Ally or Constraint

* **Be explicit with residents about what the state is offering and what it costs.** Don't let state incentives feel like bribery or coercion. "The state offers higher per-pupil funding if you consolidate" sounds like "consolidation is a condition of fair funding." Better framing: "Consolidation allows our district to access funding that recognizes the economies of scale that benefit our students." Residents need to understand that the state isn't forcing consolidation — it's recognizing that consolidation makes fiscal sense and rewarding efficiency.

* **The risk: state support can feel like a top-down mandate imposed on a community.** If residents hear "The state wants us to consolidate," they hear "They don't trust us to make our own decisions." Counter this by having the state officials come to community meetings *after* local leaders have already built grassroots support, not before. Local stakeholders should be able to point to state support and say, "Even the state recognizes we're making the right choice," not "The state is forcing us."

* **Ask the state directly: what happens to a district that consolidates vs. one that doesn't?** More funding? Technical assistance with the transition? Accelerated approval timelines for bond issues? Political cover if the vote fails? Residents need to know the real stakes. Is this a soft incentive (we'll help you) or a hard consequence (we'll withhold support). Be honest about what the state can actually deliver in a consolidation timeframe (usually 18-24 months) vs. what they're promising (sometimes 5+ years).

* **Local leaders need to translate state incentives into community benefits.** "The state will help us negotiate new transportation contracts more efficiently" is administratese. Translate it: "This means we can reduce transportation costs so we have more money to spend on teachers and programs." Residents care about what happens in their community, not about state funding formulas. Every state incentive needs a community translation.

## A Referendum as a Constitutional Convention

* **Reframe the vote as "What kind of learning community do we want to build?" not "Do you want to consolidate?"** Nobody gets excited about consolidation. People get excited about possibility. Instead of asking "Vote yes to merge districts," ask "Vote yes to create a unified district where your child has access to a full range of teachers, programs, and opportunities that no one district alone could provide." The question becomes aspirational, not administrative.

* **Create a community input process before the vote where residents help design what the new district actually does.** Not just who runs it, but what its values are. What should graduates know? What kinds of learning opportunities? How do we honor the identity and history of each community within the new whole? If residents feel like they're authoring the founding vision, they're voting for something they created, not something imposed. This takes time and genuine listening — it's not a checkbox community engagement process.

* **Acknowledge the loss in consolidation alongside the gain.** Consolidation means the loss of a superintendent who knows every family's name, of a school building as the community's gathering place, of the identity of "District A" as a separate entity. These losses are real and shouldn't be minimized. But if the new district creates something bigger — a shared identity as part of a county learning ecosystem, access to more programs, financial stability — the trade-off can feel worthwhile. People can accept loss if they understand what they're gaining and if they had voice in the design.

* **The vote itself needs to feel like a founding moment, not a hostage negotiation.** Decorate the polling place. Have community leaders there to thank people for voting. Make it clear this is a momentous choice that will shape the next 20 years. Afterward, whether the vote passes or fails, the community should feel like it made a genuine deliberative choice, not that it was manipulated or coerced into compliance. That trust is worth more than any individual referendum outcome.
