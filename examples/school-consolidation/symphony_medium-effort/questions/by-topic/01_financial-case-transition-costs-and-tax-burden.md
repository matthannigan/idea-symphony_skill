# Topic Cluster 01: Financial Case, Transition Costs, and Tax Burden

## Questions

1. **Break-even horizon and the savings-mirage stress test**: If the cited $1.2M in annual administrative savings is taken seriously, how do one-time transition costs (severance, contract buyouts, systems integration, staff retraining, three-way union legal fees) shift the breakeven year, how sensitive is that horizon to the accuracy of the savings estimate, and how should planners model the fact that administrative savings function as a one-time structural reduction (not a recurring efficiency that grows over time) — recognizing that each district's administrative structure may reflect locally negotiated constraints, union protections, or statutory obligations that limit actual savings, and that some categories of administrative cost (compliance, coordination, transportation oversight) typically grow with a larger, more complex district rather than shrink?
2. **Per-pupil formula dependency and the long-fuse risk**: The ~12% per-pupil funding increment assumes the state formula remains unchanged across the planning horizon, yet formulas change — what is the risk that the per-pupil bonus is reduced, phased in late, or eliminated once consolidation removes the political rationale for the subsidy, how robust is the financial model to formula risk over a 10- and 20-year window (a structural-governor question about whether the financial rationale itself rests on a moving floor), and what does the per-pupil unit-economics picture look like at 2026 versus 2035 enrollment, including the floor below which even a consolidated district cannot operate sustainably given that mobile families tend to leave first, leaving a remaining population with systematically higher service needs and fewer tax-generating households?
3. **The deferred maintenance debt trap and stranded-cost accounting**: The $4.2M in deferred building maintenance compounds non-linearly — a $200K roof today is an $800K roof in five years — so what framework should guide which buildings' maintenance liabilities are absorbed versus divested, how should stranded costs for closed buildings be accounted in the financial model, and what mechanisms would prevent the inherited backlog from simply being re-deferred under new governance?
4. **Property tax equalization across asymmetric tax bases**: The three districts likely have different tax bases and mill levy histories; what does the range of possible property tax impacts look like for residential and commercial property owners in each district under different equalization scenarios, and which scenario most fairly distributes burden without triggering the political opposition that could sink the consolidation vote?
5. **Hospital-system "affiliate before you absorb" sequencing**: Rural hospital networks have navigated the same triple pressure — declining patient populations, structural deficits, and fierce community identity resistance — and some have developed phased "affiliate before you absorb" models that preserved local branding while achieving back-office unification; what does that structural parallel reveal about sequencing the school consolidation so that communities feel they are joining something rather than losing something?
6. **Municipal bond refinancing and "debt assumption" models**: When cities merge utilities or water districts, the thorniest problem is often not ongoing costs but legacy debt — each entity carries obligations on different timelines with different creditors, and the sequence of assumption matters for credit ratings and voter trust; there's a direct structural parallel with the three districts' separate bond obligations and deferred maintenance liabilities; how might the consolidation sequence (which district's debt gets assumed first, which facility decisions get made before vs. after the vote) be designed to minimize the financial risk that any single community perceives itself as bailing out a neighbor?
7. **Fiscal conservative persuasion path**: Fiscal conservatives who support consolidation in principle may still vote no if they don't trust the promised $1.2M in administrative savings to materialize, if they fear transition costs will wipe out savings for years, or if they suspect the new board will spend efficiency gains rather than returning them to taxpayers — what specific accountability mechanisms (independent audits, sunset clauses on new spending, binding savings targets in the consolidation agreement) would give this bloc enough confidence to vote yes rather than defaulting to skepticism?
