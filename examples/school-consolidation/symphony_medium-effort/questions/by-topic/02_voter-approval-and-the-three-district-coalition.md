# Topic Cluster 02: Voter Approval and the Three-District Coalition

## Questions

1. **The holdout problem, asymmetric incentives, and defining a real win**: A single district voting "no" blocks the entire consolidation, meaning the weakest coalition wins — what would each district's distinct self-interest in the consolidated arrangement need to look like to secure approval, what would it take to construct a proposal that creates genuine winners (not just "less bad outcomes") for residents in each district, and is the current framing treating "all three communities" as a single persuasion target when they may have fundamentally different incentives, opposition profiles (fiscal skeptics, identity defenders, union households), and trust deficits from prior unfulfilled promises?
2. **Phased consolidation as a winnability hedge**: Any proposal requiring simultaneous yes votes in three communities creates a single point of failure — has a phased approach been seriously examined (two districts consolidate first, creating a demonstrated model the third can join), and what are the actual legal, financial, and timing barriers to that path versus the political risk of a single-shot referendum?
3. **Supermajority dynamics from legislative coalition-building**: Political scientists who study legislative coalition formation find that the sequence in which persuadable actors are approached matters as much as the substance of the offer — early converts signal legitimacy to late adopters, while early defectors create cascading doubt; given that voter approval is required in all three districts simultaneously, what sequencing and cross-district coalition architecture would create the strongest positive contagion effect leading into the vote?
4. **Who must vote yes**: The consolidation requires approval in all three districts — what is the minimum winning coalition in each district, which voter blocs are reachable, and what sequence of endorsements or visible commitments would make a "yes" vote feel safe rather than risky to persuadable residents?
5. **Asymmetric opposition mapping**: The three districts almost certainly have different mixes of opposition — fiscal skeptics, identity defenders, union households, and long-term residents who've seen previous consolidation promises fail — so what is the specific opposition profile in each district, and how does the campaign message and coalition sequence need to differ across all three rather than treating the vote as a single unified campaign?
6. **Informal gatekeepers**: Beyond the formal actors (school boards, union reps, county board), who in each of these three communities actually shapes how neighbors think about the school — the retired superintendent, the booster club president, the local paper editor, the pastor whose congregation overlaps heavily with parent groups — and what would it take to bring those informal influencers from neutral to visible supporters before the vote campaign even begins?
7. **State agency as ally or constraint**: State education officials are described as encouraging consolidation, which means they hold both carrots (enhanced funding, technical assistance, political cover) and sticks (withholding support from districts that resist) — what is the realistic range of state leverage available to the county, and how should state-level support be deployed in sequencing so it feels like community empowerment rather than top-down mandate?
8. **A referendum as a constitutional convention**: Voter approval in all three districts is typically framed as a political hurdle — a gauntlet to survive. What if it were reimagined entirely as a founding act, where the vote isn't on whether to merge administrative structures but on whether to collectively author something genuinely new: a charter for a community-owned learning ecosystem that has never existed before and that residents feel they created, not consented to?
