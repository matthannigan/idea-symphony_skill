# Brainstorming Questions from The Storyteller: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## The Morning After the Vote

1. **The night the results come in**: Imagine a parent in the smallest district — the one facing insolvency — sitting at a kitchen table after the consolidation vote. What story do they need to have lived through in the preceding year for them to feel, in that moment, that the process treated their community with respect, even if their school building is scheduled to close?

2. **The teacher who stayed**: Walk through the arc of an experienced teacher who taught in one of these three districts for fifteen years, watched the vote pass, and is still in the classroom two years later in the new unified district. What would have had to happen — in contract negotiations, school assignment, professional culture — to make that person a quiet advocate for consolidation rather than a quiet casualty of it?

---

## The Town That Lost Its School

3. **The empty building at the center of town**: Picture the smallest community in the county two years after the consolidation, its school building now closed. What specific, tangible things would have to be true about how that building is being used, and what the new district does in that town, for residents to tell a story of change rather than a story of abandonment?

4. **The Friday night question**: In rural communities, the school gymnasium is the social center — Friday night games draw half the town. Describe how a family from a community that lost its school building would experience fall sports season in the first year of the consolidated district, and what the new district would have to design into that experience to keep community identity alive across the geography?

---

## The Slow Fracture or the Slow Repair

5. **The two timelines side by side**: Imagine two parallel futures: in one, the county votes yes and consolidation proceeds; in the other, the county votes no and the smallest district reaches insolvency in two years. Walk through what a family with a second-grader today would actually experience — classroom sizes, teacher stability, program availability, building conditions — in each of those stories by the time that child reaches eighth grade.

---

## What the Superintendent Carries

6. **The leader who has to hold it together**: Imagine the first superintendent of the consolidated district three years in, explaining at a community meeting why consolidation was worth it to a skeptical audience. What stories from those three years — moments of visible payoff, small wins, promises kept — would she need to have in hand to make that argument feel earned rather than defensive?

---

## The Child Who Crosses Town Lines

7. **A day in the life, redefined**: Describe a typical school day for an eighth-grader in the new unified district whose family lives at the far geographic edge of the county — the child who now has the longest bus ride, whose daily routine changed most dramatically. What would the district have had to build into transportation, arrival time, extracurricular access, and family communication to make that child's daily story one of expanded opportunity rather than grinding inconvenience?
