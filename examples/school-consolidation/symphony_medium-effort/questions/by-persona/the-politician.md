# Brainstorming Questions from The Politician: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## Voter Coalition Architecture

1. **Who must vote yes**: The consolidation requires approval in all three districts — what is the minimum winning coalition in each district, which voter blocs are reachable, and what sequence of endorsements or visible commitments would make a "yes" vote feel safe rather than risky to persuadable residents?

2. **Asymmetric opposition mapping**: The three districts almost certainly have different mixes of opposition — fiscal skeptics, identity defenders, union households, and long-term residents who've seen previous consolidation promises fail — so what is the specific opposition profile in each district, and how does the campaign message and coalition sequence need to differ across all three rather than treating the vote as a single unified campaign?

---

## Stakeholder Power Mapping

3. **Informal gatekeepers**: Beyond the formal actors (school boards, union reps, county board), who in each of these three communities actually shapes how neighbors think about the school — the retired superintendent, the booster club president, the local paper editor, the pastor whose congregation overlaps heavily with parent groups — and what would it take to bring those informal influencers from neutral to visible supporters before the vote campaign even begins?

4. **Union leverage points**: Three separate teachers' union contracts mean three sets of leadership with different priorities, different relationships with their memberships, and different fears about what consolidation means for seniority, job security, and workplace conditions — which union's support is the most achievable early win, and what concession or commitment would most credibly convert that union from a source of organized opposition into a quiet non-objector or active endorser?

5. **State agency as ally or constraint**: State education officials are described as encouraging consolidation, which means they hold both carrots (enhanced funding, technical assistance, political cover) and sticks (withholding support from districts that resist) — what is the realistic range of state leverage available to the county, and how should state-level support be deployed in sequencing so it feels like community empowerment rather than top-down mandate?

---

## Governance Design as Political Settlement

6. **Board composition and district parity**: The governance structure of the new unified board will be read by each community as a signal of whether their town still has meaningful voice or has been absorbed — what board composition, seat allocation, and transition structure would give each of the three communities credible representation without creating a design so fragmented it prevents coherent governance, and who in each community needs to be consulted before a structure is publicly proposed?

7. **Facility decisions and political sequencing**: The choice of which school buildings to keep, repurpose, or close is likely the highest-voltage political decision in the entire process, because each closure lands as a concrete, visible loss on a specific community — at what point in the consolidation process should facility decisions be surfaced, and should they be made before or after the vote to avoid either poisoning the campaign or setting up a post-approval backlash when communities feel they were misled?

---

## Opposition Strategy and Resistance Management

8. **"Our school is our town" as principled opposition**: Some of the resistance to consolidation is not transactional — it reflects a genuine belief that the school is load-bearing infrastructure for community identity, not just a service provider — and this opposition cannot be neutralized by fiscal arguments alone, so what form of institutional recognition, naming continuity, or programmatic commitment could acknowledge and partially preserve what these communities are actually defending without undermining the operational logic of consolidation?

9. **Fiscal conservative persuasion path**: Fiscal conservatives who support consolidation in principle may still vote no if they don't trust the promised $1.2M in administrative savings to materialize, if they fear transition costs will wipe out savings for years, or if they suspect the new board will spend efficiency gains rather than returning them to taxpayers — what specific accountability mechanisms (independent audits, sunset clauses on new spending, binding savings targets in the consolidation agreement) would give this bloc enough confidence to vote yes rather than defaulting to skepticism?
