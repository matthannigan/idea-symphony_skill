# Brainstorming Questions from The Systems Thinker: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## Enrollment Decline as a Self-Amplifying Dynamic

1. **The shrinking school spiral**: As enrollment drops and school quality perceptions decline, families with options leave — which accelerates enrollment loss, which tightens budgets further. What structural interventions could interrupt this compounding cycle before consolidation, and how does consolidation itself either break or reinforce the spiral?

2. **Who stays, who leaves, and why it compounds**: Families with greater mobility tend to leave struggling districts first, leaving behind a higher proportion of students with complex needs and fewer tax-generating households. How should the consolidated district's planning model account for this demographic shift, and what does it mean for per-pupil cost projections if the remaining student population has systematically higher service needs?

3. **Enrollment projections as a moving target**: The feasibility study will assume a consolidation scenario and a status-quo scenario — but consolidation itself changes the conditions that drive enrollment. What feedback effects (positive and negative) might the act of consolidating generate on enrollment, and which assumptions in the projections are most sensitive to those effects?

---

## Feedback Loops in Finance and Governance

4. **The deferred maintenance debt trap**: Deferred maintenance compounds over time — a roof that costs $200K today may cost $800K in five years. How does the $4.2M in deferred maintenance interact with the decision about which buildings to close, and what happens to the consolidated district's financial health if the building closure decisions are driven by political feasibility rather than maintenance cost curves?

5. **Per-pupil funding as a structural governor**: The state funding formula penalizes small districts, creating a natural pressure toward consolidation. But if the formula changes — as formulas do — the financial rationale shifts. How dependent is the consolidation's long-term viability on the current funding formula remaining stable, and what should the financial model assume about formula risk over a 10- and 20-year horizon?

6. **Administrative savings as a one-time gain**: The $1.2M in estimated administrative savings from eliminating duplicate leadership structures is a one-time structural reduction, not a recurring efficiency that grows over time. Once captured, what new cost pressures does a larger, more complex district generate — and are there categories of administrative cost that typically grow with consolidation rather than shrink?

---

## Community Identity and Institutional Trust

7. **"Our school is our town" as a stabilizing force**: The community identity attachment to local schools isn't irrational — schools often anchor community cohesion, local employment, and civic participation. If consolidation weakens those anchors, what secondary effects might follow in the towns that lose their school buildings, and how might those effects circle back to affect the consolidated district's own political and economic environment?

8. **Trust deficits and the vote**: Voter approval is required in all three districts, which means the weakest coalition wins. Which stakeholder groups are most likely to flip from opposition to support based on specific, credible commitments made during the planning process — and what governance structures or binding agreements would make those commitments believable rather than aspirational?

9. **The sequencing of transparency**: Information released too early (before a plan is formed) generates anxiety without resolution; information released too late triggers distrust of a "done deal." What phased communication strategy maps honestly to where the planning process actually is — and how does the sequence of disclosures shape the trust trajectory rather than just the information landscape?

---

## Transition Costs, Delays, and Second-Order Consequences

10. **The transition cost blind spot**: Implementation costs — technology integration, staff relocation, contract reconciliation, community engagement — often appear after the vote is won, when reversal is difficult. What categories of transition cost are most commonly underestimated in school consolidation feasibility studies, and how should the financial model distinguish between one-time transition costs and structural savings to avoid a misleading net-benefit headline?

11. **Union contract reconciliation as a long-fuse dynamic**: Reconciling three separate collective bargaining agreements is not a one-time negotiation event — it creates an ongoing pressure to level up compensation across contracts, which erodes savings over the medium term. What does the financial model look like if contract harmonization results in teacher compensation converging to the highest existing rate across the three districts rather than to an average?

12. **The governance learning curve**: A newly constituted unified school board will have higher coordination costs, more distributed constituent interests, and a steeper learning curve than three boards operating in familiar territory. How long does it typically take a consolidated district's governance structure to operate as effectively as mature single-district governance — and what happens to operational performance during that transition window?

---

## Leverage Points and Non-Obvious Intervention Options

13. **The smallest district as a forcing function**: The smallest district has approximately two years of fund balance before insolvency. This creates an asymmetric timeline — delay may not be a neutral option for the system as a whole. How does the impending insolvency of one district change the negotiating dynamics among the three, and does it create leverage for more favorable state support or a more structured phased approach that the other two districts would not otherwise accept?
