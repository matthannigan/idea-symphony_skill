# Brainstorming Questions from The Devil's Advocate: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## The Savings Mirage: Are the Numbers Actually Adding Up?

1. **Hidden transition costs**: The $1.2M annual administrative savings figure is frequently cited as the consolidation's financial headline — but what one-time transition costs (severance, contract buyouts, systems integration, staff retraining, legal fees for three-way union negotiation) could consume years of projected savings, and how should planners model the breakeven horizon before claiming consolidation "saves money"?

2. **Deferred maintenance timebomb**: The $4.2M in deferred building maintenance exists across seven facilities today — once consolidation decides which buildings to retain, what mechanisms would prevent the inherited deferred maintenance backlog from simply being re-deferred again rather than addressed, and how could the consolidation governance structure create binding accountability for facilities investment?

3. **Per-pupil formula dependency**: The ~12% increase in per-pupil state funding assumes the formula remains unchanged — but if state officials are already "encouraging consolidation," what is the risk that the per-pupil bonus is reduced or eliminated once consolidation removes the political rationale for the subsidy, and how robust is the financial case if that funding increment disappears?

---

## Voter Math: Can You Actually Win Three Simultaneous Referendums?

4. **The holdout problem**: Voter approval is required in all three districts, meaning a single district voting "no" blocks the entire consolidation — have we considered what each district's distinct self-interest in the consolidated arrangement would need to look like to secure approval, or is the current framing treating "all three communities" as a single persuasion target when they may have fundamentally different incentives?

5. **Identity tax and trust deficit**: If the "our school IS our town" sentiment is strong enough to be named as a primary resistance factor, what evidence do we have that a financial case alone can overcome it — and what would a consolidation proposal need to offer (e.g., guaranteed community facilities, named community schools within the unified district, protected local programming) to make a yes vote psychologically viable for identity-attached voters?

6. **Timing and sequencing risk**: Any proposal that requires simultaneous yes votes in three communities creates a single point of failure — have we seriously examined whether a phased approach (two districts consolidate first, creating a demonstrated model the third can join) might be structurally more winnable even if it takes longer, and what are the actual legal and financial barriers to that path?

---

## Structural Failure Modes: What Happens When This Goes Wrong?

7. **The governance vacuum**: Creating a unified board from three previously independent governance structures raises the question of whether the new board will be large enough to provide meaningful community representation but small enough to function — and what specific design choices (board size, election geography, staggered terms) would prevent the unified board from becoming either unrepresentative or ungovernable?

8. **Union contract reconciliation as a chokepoint**: Reconciling three separate union contracts is framed as a planning task, but in practice it is a multi-year negotiation with three separate bargaining units that could produce either a strike, a settlement that eliminates most administrative savings, or a prolonged limbo that demoralizes staff — what is the realistic downside scenario here, and has it been modeled as a risk rather than treated as a procedural box to check?

9. **Enrollment decline continues regardless**: Consolidation addresses the structural deficit of today's enrollment but does not reverse the demographic trend — if enrollment falls another 15% by 2035 as projected, the consolidated district will face the same pressures in a decade but with fewer levers to pull and communities already having absorbed one disruptive consolidation; what is the long-range plan beyond consolidation, and is there a point at which consolidation merely delays rather than solves the underlying problem?

---

## The Facility Closure Trap: Who Loses and What Breaks?

10. **The political economy of building closures**: Deciding which school buildings to close is not a neutral facilities optimization — it is a decision about which town absorbs a visible symbolic loss, and communities that lose their building may vote against consolidation or campaign to reverse it post-approval; have we considered whether a consolidation proposal should explicitly pre-commit to specific facility decisions before the vote, or whether leaving them ambiguous creates more or less political risk?

11. **Transportation as a hidden equity issue**: A larger geographic district means longer bus routes and longer travel times, which disproportionately affects students from the most remote households — what is the realistic maximum transportation time students would face under different facility scenarios, and at what point does transportation burden effectively exclude participation in extracurricular activities or before/after-school programs in ways that undermine the educational rationale for consolidation?
