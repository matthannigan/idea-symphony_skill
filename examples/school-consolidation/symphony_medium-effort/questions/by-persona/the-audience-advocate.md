# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Rural School District Consolidation

---

## Who Gets a Voice — and Who Gets Silence

1. **Whose table is missing**: Beyond formal stakeholder groups like unions, parent organizations, and fiscal conservatives, which community members are most likely to be affected by consolidation decisions but least likely to show up to a public hearing — and how could the process be redesigned to actively reach them?

2. **The voiceless enrollment statistic**: The 1,800 students in these districts are the people this decision most directly shapes, yet they rarely appear in consolidation deliberations — what mechanisms would let student experiences and preferences genuinely inform decisions about school closures, transportation changes, and new school cultures?

3. **Renters, newcomers, and the non-voter**: Voter approval is required in all three districts, but what about residents who are renters, recent arrivals, or otherwise disengaged from local civic life — how might a campaign for consolidation inadvertently be designed only for long-tenured homeowners, and what does that mean for who decides the outcome?

---

## The Emotional Geography of Community Identity

4. **What "our school is our town" actually means**: When residents say their school is the heart of their community, what specific experiences and relationships are they describing — and how could planners map those emotional anchors before designing facility decisions that could sever them?

5. **The grief that doesn't get named**: Consolidation conversations tend to be framed in fiscal and operational terms, but for many families this is experienced as loss — what would it look like to acknowledge and make space for that grief as a legitimate part of the process, rather than treating it as resistance to be overcome?

6. **Asymmetric loss across the three communities**: The people we serve might experience this as profoundly unequal — one town might keep its building while two others lose theirs — so how can the process surface and address that disparity in a way that doesn't simply let the largest or wealthiest community win?

---

## Accessibility, Burden, and Who Bears the Cost of Change

7. **Transportation as a daily hardship**: For families without reliable cars, students with disabilities, or children in after-school programs, longer transportation routes in a larger rural district are not an inconvenience but a genuine barrier — what does the community actually experience today in terms of transportation, and how would each consolidation scenario change that lived reality?

8. **The working parent and the public meeting**: Public engagement processes are typically built around people who have flexible schedules, reliable transportation, and comfort with formal meetings — what does the engagement process look like from the perspective of a parent working two jobs, and how would you redesign it to meet them where they are?

9. **Special populations and the assumption of sameness**: Consolidation planning often models a "typical student," but what specific experiences do families of students with IEPs, English language learners, students experiencing housing instability, and students in poverty have that could be significantly worsened or improved by consolidation — and are those populations being explicitly tracked in the planning?

---

## Power Dynamics in the Consolidation Process

10. **Who shapes the feasibility study**: The county board commissioned the feasibility study, but the people most affected by its findings rarely shape the questions it asks — what assumptions are baked into the study's framing, and how would the findings look different if community members from each district had co-designed the research questions?

11. **The three unions are not a monolith**: Teachers' unions are often treated as a single stakeholder bloc, but from the perspective of the people we serve — including teachers themselves — the reconciliation of three separate contracts will produce winners and losers — what experience do teachers in the smallest, most vulnerable district have that might be erased in a "harmonized" contract negotiation dominated by the larger districts?

12. **Community trust as infrastructure**: The people being asked to vote yes on consolidation have likely seen past promises about schools, taxes, and services go unfulfilled — what is the history of institutional trust in each of these communities, and how does that history shape what a credible, trustworthy process actually needs to look and feel like?

---

## Dignity and Agency After the Vote

13. **What a "yes" vote commits the district to**: From the community's perspective, a yes vote is not the end of the story — it is the beginning of years of implementation decisions that will determine whether consolidation actually delivers — so what ongoing accountability structures would give community members genuine agency over those decisions rather than leaving them as passive recipients of institutional choices made behind closed doors?
