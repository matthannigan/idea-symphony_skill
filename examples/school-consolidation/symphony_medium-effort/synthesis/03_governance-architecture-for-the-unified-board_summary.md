# Summary: Governance Architecture for the Unified Board

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension across this cluster is that nearly every mechanism that makes a former district feel safe also constrains the board from making the hard decisions consolidation exists to enable. Seat floors, supermajority rules, parity councils, and written covenants all reassure communities that their town will not be erased. Yet each one is also a brake on the closures and reallocations that the county's enrollment math makes inevitable. The design problem is to buy genuine consent in year one without building permanent deadlock into a board that will face shrinking enrollment for the next decade. The strongest resolution the perspectives offer is to make these protections explicitly temporary. Supermajorities and parity councils would sunset after roughly three to five years, by which point a transparent track record has replaced the need for structural guarantees.

The most convergent finding is mechanical rather than philosophical. All four perspectives agree that representation design alone does not prevent the board from drifting toward the largest or wealthiest former district under the cover of "efficiency." What prevents drift is a published decision record that ties every facility, staffing, and budget choice to its geographic impact, paired with hard decision rules and a recurring independent audit. Comparable consolidations that published this data measurably reduced community suspicion. Transparency surfaces favoritism. Only rules constrain it. The two must travel together.

On the question of whether to unify at all, the perspectives converge on a counterintuitive answer: federation is financially weaker than it looks, but it is politically stronger. The resolution is to use it as a phase rather than a destination. A federated shared-services model captures perhaps 40–70% of the administrative savings but forgoes the roughly $4.3M per-pupil funding uplift and tends to fracture within five to ten years on the first contested decision. The load-bearing reframe is to commit publicly to unified consolidation while running a three-year federated transition first, so the make-or-break referendum is decided on proven results rather than projections.

The decisive practical insight on parity is that the choosing process matters as much as the structure chosen. Quietly consulting each community's specific power-holders in small groups before any structure is proposed publicly is treated as non-negotiable. Public town halls are flagged as a trap that hardens grievances into positions. Two further moves are widely endorsed but easy to skip: appointing rather than electing the first board to avoid a launch proxy war, and pre-negotiating the three union contracts as part of the proposal rather than after the vote.

## Key Themes

**Phase before you ask: a federated transition de-risks the unifying vote.** The single most action-changing idea in the cluster is to separate the destination from the timing. Rather than asking voters to approve full consolidation on projected benefits, commit to unified governance as the goal but implement a three-year federation phase first (shared superintendent, merged administration, combined transportation). The year-three referendum then runs on real evidence, shifting the voter's question from "what if this goes wrong?" to "the test run worked." This reframes the false binary of "one district or three" that drives most rural opposition. The broader finding corroborates it: federation captures real near-term savings but fails as a permanent structure.

### Accountability is a published record, not a promise
The cluster's strongest consensus is that no representation formula prevents systematic favoritism on its own. Drift gets disguised in "efficiency" language unless every closure, budget reallocation, and staffing change is logged with its per-district impact and published on a fixed cadence, backed by an independent audit and a remediation trigger if patterns skew. This converts an abstract community fear into concrete, disputable data. Real consolidations that did it cut suspicion measurably.

### Local voice comes from geography, not at-large goodwill
Three of four perspectives prefer geographic subdistricts that force each former district to elect its own members. At-large races in rural counties default to name recognition in the largest town and quietly reproduce the absorption the structure is meant to prevent. The countervailing view—that population-weighted seats may read as fairer to the largest community—is worth holding rather than dismissing, because seat-weighting is where the cluster's central tension surfaces most sharply.

### Protections should be designed to expire
Seat floors, supermajorities on closures, parity councils, and covenants are widely endorsed as the price of year-one consent. Several perspectives are explicit that they are temporary settlements, not permanent features. Sunset clauses (three-year structure review, five-year reversion to simple majority) let the board earn trust through a track record and then shed the constraints before they ossify into deadlock under continued enrollment decline.

### The transition window decides the outcome more than the structure
The period between the vote and the first board meetings is where consolidation is won or lost. Reserving the first six months for procedure and listening with no major facility decisions, governing that window with a transition charter and a three-person transition team, and consulting power-holders before anything is proposed publicly all matter more than the exact seat count.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Run a side-by-side ten-year pro forma for full consolidation versus a federated shared-services model, including accelerated-enrollment-decline scenarios. Quantify the per-pupil funding uplift and federation's fracture risk rather than assuming them.
- Conduct small-group consultations (not public town halls) in each district with the named power-holders: superintendent, board chair, largest teacher-union representative, a selectboard or council member, and the largest parent-group leader. Frame them as "help us understand concerns." Map each community's non-negotiables.
- Draft the accountability architecture before any structure goes public. Define the per-decision impact log, publication cadence, independent-audit interval, and remediation trigger.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Adopt a board design with a guaranteed seat floor per former district plus proportional top-off. Pair it with a time-limited supermajority requirement for closures, boundary or route changes, and major capital decisions.
- Decide the federation-as-phase question. If pursued, publicly commit to unified consolidation as the destination with a three-year federated transition and a defined year-three referendum.
- Stand up a community-controlled design task force with elected representatives from each district to propose and ratify the structure. Pre-negotiate a unified union contract with union representatives inside the design phase.
- Plan to appoint rather than elect the first board. Retain a neutral, time-limited board facilitator for the first 12–24 months.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Operate the published decision log and independent audit from day one. Reserve the first six months post-vote for procedure and listening under a transition charter with no major facility decisions in year one.
- Trigger the sunset reviews on schedule. Reassess board composition at year three and revert supermajority rules to simple majority around year five, contingent on a clean accountability record.
- Track public comment and meeting attendance by geographic origin for the first three years. Confirm participation stays distributed rather than concentrating in the largest town.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- The roughly 12% per-pupil funding uplift (estimated ~$4.3M annually) is a decisive financial argument that federation cannot match. It gives the unifying case a concrete fiscal anchor.
- A three-year federated transition lets the county prove savings and cooperation before the irreversible vote. It converts projections into evidence.
- Published equity metrics turn an abstract fear ("they'll favor the big town") into disputable data. This has measurably reduced suspicion in comparable consolidations.
- The smallest district's near-insolvency creates a natural coalition entry point. It may back full consolidation because the alternative is closure.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- Without hard decision rules and an audit, "efficiency" language will mask drift toward the largest or wealthiest former district, the exact outcome the structure is meant to prevent.
- Public town halls held too early harden grievances into fixed positions that stakeholders cannot retreat from.
- A federation chosen as a permanent structure tends to fracture within five to ten years on the first contested decision, losing both the savings and the relationships.
- The first unified board moving fast on closures or hiring in year one will convince communities their voice was theoretical. This triggers backlash before trust is earned.
- Three separate union contracts left unreconciled until after the vote are a decisive and under-addressed threat to passage.

**Trade-offs**:
- Every protection that reassures a former district (seat floors, supermajorities, parity councils) also constrains the board's ability to make the hard closure decisions consolidation exists to enable. Sunset clauses are the proposed reconciliation.
- Geographic subdistricts secure local accountability but a vocal minority view holds that population-weighted seats may read as fairer to the largest community.
- A larger board (9–11 seats) surfaces conflict earlier and distributes accountability but is costlier and slower. A smaller board is more governable but concentrates dangerous power over closures.
- Federation preserves community identity and lowers the political risk of the first vote. But it leaves money on the table and defers rather than resolves the structural problem.
