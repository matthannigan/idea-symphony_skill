# Brainstorming Responses: Operations, Staffing, and Founder Capacity

---

## Question 16: First Hire Decision

* **Hire a part-time prep cook once weekly gross revenue consistently clears $2,500.** A prep cook removes the 2-3 hours of daily mise en place that currently sits entirely on the founder — chopping, marinating, portioning adobo protein, folding lumpia. At $15-17/hour for 15-20 hours/week, the wage burden is roughly $1,000-1,200/month including payroll taxes. That's justified once revenue is stable enough to absorb it without touching emergency reserves. The key test: if the founder is regularly skipping marketing tasks or skipping rest days because of prep load, the hire is already overdue.

* **Start with a service window hire at a lower revenue threshold ($1,800-2,000/week) if events are the growth driver.** Catering gigs and weekend markets are won or lost at the window — speed, warmth, upselling. A trained window person at 10-12 hours/week around peak shifts lets the founder stay in the kitchen, which is where quality control lives for fusion prep. The tradeoff: this hire doesn't reduce founder hours as dramatically, but it unlocks booking more simultaneous or back-to-back events that would otherwise be physically impossible.

* **Delay any hire until a 90-day revenue baseline is established; instead, trade labor for labor.** Before taking on payroll, recruit culinary school students for practicum credit or offer a "stage" (unpaid apprenticeship) in exchange for structured training — legal in many states if structured correctly, with a written agreement and genuine skills transfer. This provides operational relief at near-zero cost and creates a talent pipeline. Once one or two people have proven reliable, convert the best candidate to paid part-time rather than hiring a stranger cold.

* **Use a staffing agency for the first 90 days to defer HR exposure.** Agencies handle payroll taxes, workers' comp classification, and scheduling gaps. The markup (typically 25-40% above base wage) is real, but so is the founder's lack of HR experience. Treat the premium as tuition — observe what the agency handles, understand what questions arise (tip reporting, break compliance, shift documentation), then internalize that knowledge before running payroll directly. Convert to direct hire after the trial period if the worker fits.

## Question 17: Operational Systems for Quality Consistency

* **Build and laminate a prep Bible before adding any revenue stream.** Document every recipe at batch scale (not single-serving): weight-based measurements for adobo marinade, lumpia filling yield per kilogram of pork, ube extract ratios per liter of horchata base. Photograph the finished product at correct standard. This document lives at the commissary and on the truck. The test: can a competent cook who has never eaten your food reproduce a sellable product from the Bible alone? If not, the system isn't ready to scale. Write the Bible first, test it with one unfamiliar cook, then add catering volume.

* **Implement pre-portioning protocols tied to commissary prep days, not service days.** Schedule two commissary sessions per week. Session A: proteins — marinate and cook adobo chicken/pork in standardized batches, vacuum-seal and label with prep date. Session B: fresh components — lumpia wrappers, slaw, sauces portioned into identical containers. Service days then become assembly days, not cooking days. This compresses the skill required on the truck (anyone can assemble if the components are perfect), which is essential before a second truck operator who isn't the founder enters the picture.

* **Introduce a simple quality audit checklist before any catering booking is accepted.** Before each service, run through 10 items: protein temp, lumpia texture, sauce viscosity, horchata sweetness level, portion weight on one random item. Score it pass/fail. If three or more fail, delay opening until corrected. This sounds rigid but addresses a specific failure mode of fusion cuisine at volume: the components are fine individually but ratios drift under pressure. Having the habit of a pre-service audit in place before adding catering volume means the habit is already embedded when volume stress arrives.

* **Standardize packaging as a quality proxy.** Use consistent container sizes — same 6oz portion cup for all sauces, same burrito foil gauge, same lumpia tray count (6 or 8, never "approximately"). Customers notice inconsistency before they can name it, and packaging discipline forces portion discipline. At catering scale, consistent packaging also enables accurate food cost accounting, which is essential for pricing catering contracts correctly. Buy in case quantities and track usage rate — this becomes your first rudimentary inventory control system.

## Question 18: Founder Skill Gap Prioritization

* **Financial management is the highest near-term risk — address it first via a one-time engagement with a food-service accountant.** A solo founder who doesn't understand cash flow timing, food cost percentage, and break-even analysis can be profitable on paper and insolvent in practice — a common failure mode in restaurants. Spend $300-500 for a 2-hour session with a CPA who has food truck clients. Ask them to set up a simple spreadsheet model for your specific revenue mix (daily vending + occasional catering), show you how to read it weekly, and identify the three numbers that matter most. This is more efficient than a course because it's immediately applied to your actual numbers.

* **Regulatory compliance is the highest risk by consequence if ignored, but mostly manageable via the food truck association.** Missing a permit renewal or vending zone violation can trigger a fine or shutdown. However, this is largely procedural rather than strategic — it can be addressed by joining the local food truck association (which typically maintains a permit calendar and ordinance update list for members), setting calendar reminders, and building a relationship with one inspector. Budget 2-3 hours/month to regulatory compliance administration. Outsource none of it — you need the institutional knowledge yourself because the pending ordinance changes require you to engage, not delegate.

* **Deprioritize marketing skill-building in year one; outsource it cheaply instead.** Hire a part-time social media manager (a local college student with a food photography eye, $200-400/month for 3-4 posts/week) to handle Instagram and TikTok presence. The founder's time is worth more in the kitchen perfecting product than learning content strategy. Marketing is an amplifier — it makes a good product famous, but it can't rescue an inconsistent one. Revisit once the product is locked and a first hire is stable.

* **Hiring is a skill gap with a long tail — address it through process, not study.** Most hiring mistakes happen because founders evaluate on personality rather than on structured criteria. Before making any hire, write a one-page role brief: exactly what the person will do in the first 30 days, what success looks like, and what three questions you will ask every candidate. Give every finalist a paid 2-hour trial shift. This converts "I don't know how to hire" from a knowledge problem to a checklist problem, which is solvable without a course or consultant.

## Question 19: Customer Feedback Loops

* **Deploy a single-question receipt feedback mechanism that costs under $20 to set up.** Print a QR code on every receipt (or a small card) linking to a 3-question Google Form: (1) What did you order? (2) Rate it 1-5. (3) What would bring you back sooner? Offer a $1 off coupon on next visit as incentive (redeemable by showing the confirmation screen). Even 10-15 responses per week creates a signal strong enough to detect a problem — if lumpia tacos consistently score 3.5 while adobo burritos score 4.8, that's a prioritization decision. Review the form results every Monday morning; takes 15 minutes.

* **Run one 20-minute customer interview per week at a predictable venue.** Pick the Tuesday lunch spot or Saturday market as the "interview location." After a transaction, offer a free drink item in exchange for 15-20 minutes of conversation. Ask: what almost stopped you from ordering today? What did you tell someone else about us this week? What price would make you hesitate? These conversations surface the qualitative texture that ratings cannot — you'll hear "I almost didn't stop because I didn't recognize the cuisine" or "I didn't realize you did catering." Five interviews generates more actionable insight than 200 star ratings.

* **Use event debrief as a structured feedback ritual, not a vague post-mortem.** After every catering job or event booking, complete a one-page debrief within 48 hours: revenue vs. projection, food cost vs. budget, items that sold out (and when), items left over (and how much), any service complaints. After 10 events, patterns emerge — certain menu items consistently run out by the third hour, certain events underperform on revenue but generate disproportionate catering inquiries. This is the data that should inform whether to add a catering-specific menu, whether to raise event minimums, and which venues to reprioritize.

* **Track repeat customer rate as the single most important leading indicator.** A simple method: ask customers their first name and note it in the Square or Toast register under "customer." After 60 days, count how many names appear more than twice. If fewer than 15% of customers are returning, the menu or location consistency has a problem no marketing can solve. If 30%+ are returning, you have a loyalty foundation worth building on — which is the right time to introduce a punch card or digital loyalty program. Don't launch loyalty mechanics before you have evidence of latent repeat behavior.
