# Summary: Operations, Staffing, and Founder Capacity

## Executive Summary

This cluster's central tension is timing: the founder needs robust systems and support before scaling, but building those systems requires capacity the founder doesn't yet have. The brainstormer's responses return repeatedly to the same resolution—document first, then hire, then delegate—treating documentation not as administrative overhead but as the prerequisite that makes every subsequent hire and quality commitment defensible.

The strongest through-line across all four questions is the discipline of establishing a baseline before adding complexity. Whether the question is about when to hire, how to ensure consistency, which skill gaps to close, or how to collect feedback, the recommended move is to create a measurable standard first (revenue threshold, recipe Bible, pre-service checklist, repeat-customer rate), then use that standard to make decisions. This approach converts ambiguous founder judgment calls into checklist problems, which are more reliable under pressure and more trainable for future staff.

A secondary pattern is strategic deprioritization: the brainstormer distinguishes between high-consequence risks that require the founder's direct attention (financial management, regulatory compliance, product consistency) and lower-leverage tasks that can be cheaply outsourced or deferred (social media, advanced marketing, formal HR study). This creates a clear sequencing of where founder time goes in year one.

## Key Themes

### Document Before Delegating
The brainstormer returns to documentation as a prerequisite across multiple questions. It appears most explicitly in the "prep Bible" recommendation for quality consistency, but also in the hiring process (role brief before any interview), the feedback loop (event debrief within 48 hours), and the financial management recommendation (a model built from your actual numbers, not a generic course). The pattern is consistent: writing it down converts a founder-dependent process into a transferable one.

### Revenue Thresholds as Hire Triggers
The first hire question proposes specific dollar thresholds ($1,800–2,000/week for a window hire, $2,500/week for a prep cook) rather than vague readiness signals. This specificity also appears in the customer loyalty section, where a 60-day repeat-customer rate below 15% is a diagnostic threshold and above 30% is a loyalty-program trigger. Committing to a number in advance means the decision is made before the stress of the moment distorts judgment.

### Cheap Feedback Beats Deferred Feedback
Two questions address feedback, and both recommend mechanisms that cost under $20 and generate actionable signal within days rather than quarters: a QR-code Google Form on receipts, a weekly 20-minute customer interview for the cost of a drink, a post-event debrief form. The emphasis is on consistent ritual over sophisticated tools. 10–15 weekly survey responses or 5 interviews produces more usable insight than waiting until the sample is statistically significant.

### Outsource to Learn, Not to Avoid
The staffing agency and social media manager recommendations are not pure cost trades; both are framed as ways to acquire institutional knowledge without paying a consultant. The agency markup is "tuition" for learning payroll, break compliance, and tip reporting. A college student social media hire frees founder time for the kitchen, where year-one leverage is highest. Don't outsource anything where you need the knowledge yourself (regulatory compliance is explicitly flagged as non-delegable).

### Pre-Service Standards as a Scale Prerequisite
The quality consistency question recommends a 10-item pre-service audit checklist with a pass/fail trigger (three failures = delay opening) before catering volume is added. Habits formed under low-volume conditions carry forward under high-volume stress. Habits that don't exist yet won't spontaneously appear when stress is highest. This applies directly to staffing—commission prep days at the commissary before hiring assembly staff on the truck.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Write the prep Bible before accepting any catering booking: weight-based measurements, batch yields, plated photos, tested once with an unfamiliar cook. `[recurring]`
- Set a specific hire trigger — $2,500/week gross for a prep cook, $1,800–2,000/week for a window hire — and write it down now so the decision is made before pressure distorts it. `[recurring]`
- Spend $300–500 on a 2-hour session with a food-service CPA to build a cash flow model from your actual revenue mix; leave with the three numbers you track weekly. `[single]`
- Deploy a QR-code receipt feedback form (3 questions, $1-off incentive) and review results every Monday; aim for 10–15 responses/week as a minimum signal threshold. `[single]`

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Implement two commissary prep sessions per week (Session A: proteins; Session B: fresh components) so service days become assembly days — this is the prerequisite for any non-founder truck operator. `[recurring]`
- Before the first paid hire, write a one-page role brief (30-day tasks, success criteria, three interview questions) and require a paid 2-hour trial shift for every finalist. `[recurring]`
- Run one 20-minute customer interview per week at a fixed venue; use the data to surface qualitative insight (unrecognized cuisine, undiscovered catering option) that star ratings miss. `[single]`
- Join the local food truck association for permit calendar access and ordinance updates; budget 2–3 hours/month to compliance administration and keep it non-delegated. `[single]`

### Long-term (1+ years)
- After 60 days of customer name tracking in Square/Toast, calculate repeat rate: below 15% signals a product or location problem; above 30% is the trigger to introduce a formal loyalty program. `[recurring]`
- If using a staffing agency for early hires, treat the markup as tuition — internalize payroll tax, workers' comp, and break-compliance knowledge, then convert to direct hire after one trial period. `[single]`

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- Culinary school practicum partnerships and stage arrangements offer near-zero-cost operational relief while building a talent pipeline before formal payroll begins.
- Standardizing packaging (fixed portion cup sizes, consistent lumpia tray counts) creates a byproduct: rudimentary inventory control and the ability to accurately price catering contracts.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- A founder who is profitable on paper but cash-flow negative due to poor timing of expenses is a common restaurant failure mode — financial management skill gap is highest-consequence in year one.
- Operational systems that exist only in the founder's head cannot be stress-tested until it's too late; a hire made before the prep Bible exists transfers founder dependency, not founder capability.

**Trade-offs**:
- Hiring a prep cook reduces founder hours more substantially but requires a higher revenue threshold ($2,500/week); a window hire unlocks more event bookings sooner but at a lower threshold and less personal relief.
- Outsourcing social media cheaply frees founder time for the kitchen — the right call in year one — but defers building marketing fluency until after product and first hire are stable.

**Conspicuous absences**:
- The brainstormer never names a co-founder, business partner, or informal advisor role as a capacity lever. All relief scenarios involve hiring or outsourcing to strangers or agency staff. For a solo founder, a trusted peer (another food truck operator, a mentor in a food entrepreneur network) who can cover a shift, review a contract, or flag a regulatory change is often the highest-leverage zero-cost support structure. Its complete absence from the responses is a structural gap.
- No question or response addresses founder physical capacity limits: burnout, injury, or illness that makes the founder unavailable for even one service day. The entire operational model is built on founder presence. A single-day contingency plan (who covers, what gets cancelled, what communication goes out) is never raised despite being a practical necessity.
