# Summary: Unit Economics, Menu Design, and Pricing

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension across this cluster is whether to optimize the truck toward conventional fast-casual targets (the 28-32% food cost band, a fast break-even) or to deliberately run higher food costs, premium pricing, and protected unprofitability in service of distinctiveness and durability. The exploration lands decisively on the second posture, while insisting the founder ground it in validated numbers rather than narrative alone.

The strongest convergence is that the inherited 28-32% food cost band is the wrong target for specialty Filipino-Mexican fusion. Immigrant-supplier ingredients (ube extract, calamansi) structurally push signature items to 35-40% food cost, and chasing the band would degrade the very quality that distinguishes the concept. The economics work because a high-margin beverage (ube horchata at 18-25% food cost) and one or two simple fillers subsidize the signature tacos, blending the system toward roughly 54% gross margin. A solo founder carries near-zero labor cost. The capital ceiling becomes a forcing function toward a 3-item core, ruthless procurement discipline, and a moat of genuine excellence that a sprawling, better-funded competitor can't easily replicate.

Pricing is treated as identity, not arithmetic. A $9 taco signals commodity and competes with thirty other trucks; a $14 taco signals craft, filters for loyal customers, and honors the founder's heritage. The decision is framed as a referendum on the founder's own beliefs about what the cuisine is worth. Transparency about sourcing converts a premium price from "greedy" to "justified."

The cluster's most provocative move is reframing break-even as a trap rather than a finish line. Nearly every perspective agrees that the formula producing break-even calcifies into an untouchable routine. The recommended antidote is to budget 10-20% of revenue or prime time as a protected "optionality fund" for experimentation, treating it as investment rather than inefficiency.

Two cautions keep the optimism honest. The fully-loaded daily cost floor is genuinely unknown (estimates span $90 to $760/day), so validating it with local vendor quotes is the single most important pre-launch action. The lean-excellence enthusiasm can slide into "leaner than viable" if it cuts compliance, safety, or the seasonal and supply-shock hedge that a slightly broader menu provides.

## Key Themes

### The benchmark is borrowed, the floor is real
Every persona rejects the 28-32% food cost band as a binding target for specialty fusion, but the same voices insist the true daily cost floor must be validated, not assumed. The pattern across questions 1 and 2: dismiss inherited category benchmarks and replace them with honestly-modeled numbers from actual supplier, commissary, and insurance quotes. The widest factual disagreement in the cluster (a daily floor anywhere from $90 to $760) makes this validation the highest-leverage pre-launch task.

### Constraints reframed as competitive advantage
Specialty ingredient costs, the capital ceiling, the single-window throughput limit, and the tight menu all get reframed from limitations into sources of distinctiveness, focus, and operating discipline. A constraint that forces mastery of a few things produces a moat that a better-resourced but diffuse competitor cannot match. This theme is strongest in questions 1 and 6. A counter-caution: constraint can also mask bad unit economics or push past the point of viability.

### Pricing as identity and self-belief, not cost-plus
Across question 4 the convergence is that price communicates who the food is for and what it is worth, and the founder's heritage points toward an owned premium. Price reflects the founder's internal beliefs: a too-low price reads as an apology and as internalized bias about the cuisine's worth. Transparency about sourcing and story is the consistent mechanism for making a premium price feel earned rather than exclusionary. A tiered structure keeps the offering inclusive.

### Break-even as a trap, and protected unprofitability as the antidote
Question 5 produced the cluster's most unified and generative theme: break-even is the inflection where fear replaces experimentation and the business calcifies. The shared prescription is to deliberately reserve a budgeted slice of revenue or capacity for experiments, loss-leaders, and relationship-building, naming it as investment in optionality. The founder's-joy and capital-payback angles extend this from a financial tactic into a question of what business is worth operating for five years.

### Founder time and emotional capacity as the binding constraint
Across questions 3 and 5, several perspectives converge that the founder's own hours and emotional endurance, not customer demand, set the real ceiling. Vending consumes time 1:1 and exhausts. The math that ignores burnout and treats founder labor as free is misleading. This reframes the channel-mix and growth questions around protecting and imputing a cost to the founder's time, and points to bringing on a co-operator by month 3-4.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Validate the real daily cost floor before finalizing any break-even model. Get actual quotes from the local commissary, insurance broker, and fuel vendor (estimates range from $90 to $760/day; the city's stricter ordinances may raise costs).
- Run a three-week ingredient cost audit on lumpia tacos, adobo burrito, and ube horchata using real supplier quotes (including small-batch minimums and spoilage). Cook 10-20 trial batches to lock actual, not theoretical, food costs.
- Soft-launch at pop-ups and farmers markets with a three-price test ($9/$12/$14) to capture real demand elasticity and validate average order value before committing the truck to a number.
- Design the menu down to a 3-item core (lumpia tacos, adobo burrito, ube horchata) with one or two simple high-margin fillers. Resolve whether the founder owns a premium price stance or a tiered accessible-plus-signature structure.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Stand up a live weekly dashboard tracking covers, AOV, food cost, and labor. By week 4, use it to decide whether the location can hit target covers and adjust location/hours/marketing rather than waiting for a break-even that won't come.
- Plan for an 8-10 month break-even on the bootstrap path and set an explicit month-5/6 pivot-or-capital decision rule tied to specific metrics (e.g., max covers/day observed), with a 12-month cash reserve or external income as backstop.
- Run the three-channel margin analysis twice (founder labor at $0 and at ~$25/hour) to identify which of vending, catering, and events is genuinely economic. Recruit a reliable co-operator by month 3-4 to break the single-window ceiling.
- Budget 10-20% of gross revenue (or one service day per week) as a protected optionality fund for menu, location, and catering experiments. Run the truck at roughly 85% capacity rather than maxing out.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Take the SBA loan only after cover velocity is proven, treating it as runway insurance rather than aspiration. Only take it if it accelerates reach to higher volume rather than funding a padded timeline.
- Track payback period and return on invested capital separately from P&L break-even. Declare the business viable only when payback is on track for 18-24 months.
- Set a second-phase goal that forces growth (e.g., target monthly profit or expansion readiness by month 12, or capacity to hire help by month 14). This prevents the business from calcifying once break-even is reached.
- Build a loyalty or pre-order mechanism (text/Instagram, punch card, weekly special) so the tight menu reads as curated and revenue becomes predictable. This lays the foundation for replication.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- Specialty ingredients function as a genuine competitive moat. Authentic fusion at 35-40% food cost cannot be undercut by commodity-sourced competitors.
- The high-margin beverage (ube horchata, 18-25% food cost) is an under-leveraged profit engine that subsidizes signature traffic drivers and blends the system toward ~54% gross margin.
- The capital ceiling forces the procurement and operations discipline that becomes a lasting flywheel and a scalable, already-perfected model.
- Owned premium pricing plus transparent sourcing narrative builds a loyal, higher-value customer base that no discount strategy reaches.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- The fully-loaded daily cost floor is genuinely unknown (estimates span $90-760/day). Building a break-even model on benchmarks risks insolvency.
- The single-window revenue ceiling is a hard cap. The second hire needed to break it can collapse contribution margin from 20% to 5-8%.
- Founder burnout, not demand, may be the true ceiling. Tight-capital scenarios can let the business bleed slowly toward insolvency without a forcing trigger.
- "Lean excellence" can slide into "leaner than viable," cutting compliance, safety, or the menu hedge against seasonality and supply shock.

**Trade-offs**:
- Premium pricing for distinctiveness versus low pricing for market-share and cumulative margin. A $9 taco at 80 covers/day may beat $14 at 60 over 12 months, so model the curves before committing.
- Events as the margin backbone versus events as a distraction to eliminate in year one. An unresolved fork between leaning into high-per-hour catering/events and protecting the simplicity of vending plus one standing contract.
- Fast break-even versus protected unprofitability. Optimizing hard for the number produces lock-in; reserving slack for experimentation extends break-even but preserves optionality.
- A 2-3 item core for focus and mastery versus 5-6 items for hedge against seasonality and supply shock.
