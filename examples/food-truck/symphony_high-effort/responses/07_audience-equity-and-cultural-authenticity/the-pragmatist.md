# Audience, Equity, and Cultural Authenticity - The Pragmatist

## Q1: Who counts as customer, who is designed around, and accessibility of the experience

- **Start with usage data, not assumptions.** Track where foot traffic comes from during week one; if it's mainly yuppie downtown workers, that's the current de facto customer. Then decide: is that acceptable, or do you need to actively shift audience? There's no shame in each answer—but be honest about which populations your *current design* excludes, because fixing that costs real money and time.

- **Accessibility fixes are sequential.** A practical approach might be: Phase 1 (now) = add a simple cash payment option and printed menu copies. Phase 2 (month 3) = partner with local seniors center for catering weekday lunches. Phase 3 (year 2) = add a seating area if margins allow. Don't try to be fully accessible immediately; you'll fail and go broke.

- **Non-English updates are cheaper than you think.** QR code links to Google Translate, bilingual chalkboard labels, and preprinted cards in Spanish/Tagalog cost ~$200 and reach people with real loyalty potential. The payoff: you're not relying solely on social media discovery (which favors English speakers and younger crowds).

- **Card processing under pressure is real friction.** Consider a simple phone-based pre-order system (even Google Forms works) where regulars text their order 30 minutes ahead, pay via Venmo/PayPal at pickup, no line stress. This alone opens the door for elderly, mobility-impaired, or time-poor customers who'd never queue in the sun.

- **Test assumptions cheaply.** Before expanding delivery or seating, interview 10 people from three target demographics—seniors, low-income families, Filipino community leaders—and ask what would make them a regular. A 30-minute conversation beats spending $5K on fixes that nobody asked for.

## Q2: Cultural authenticity vs. fusion tourism, and the emotional tax of being "exotic"

- **Authenticity is a business decision, not a moral absolute.** You can position this as "fusion by a chef who grew up eating both" (heritage-led) or "playful culinary mashup" (novelty-led), and each attracts different customers with different loyalty. The emotional tax varies accordingly. Be clear internally which lane you're in, because mixed messaging exhausts you and confuses customers.

- **Build supplier relationships as retention insurance.** If you source ube and adobo ingredients from the same three Filipino vendors every month, you're creating mutual economic dependence. They benefit from steady orders; you get supply security and credibility when you say "these spices come from Maria's family supplier in Quezon City." This feels authentic because it is, and it's also cheaper than commodity sourcing long-term.

- **Create a community advisory board (loose, no stipend).** Invite 3-5 Filipino and Mexican community leaders to quarterly lunch meetings—they eat free, you get their honest feedback on menu evolution. This insulates you from accusations of appropriation and gives you real input on which dishes sing and which feel forced. It's also a recruitment pipeline for events catering.

- **Marketing can highlight heritage without fetishizing.** "Founded by a Filipino-Mexican chef who grew up on both" is fact-based and authentic. "Exotic fusion from our mysterious founder" is performative and exhausting. One gets repeat customers who see themselves; the other gets Instagram tourists who vanish.

- **Watch for margin creep on substitutions.** When suppliers raise prices, your first instinct will be cheaper ube powder or bottled adobo. That's the inflection point: do you absorb cost, raise prices, or accept a quality drop? Decide now. If you chose authentic positioning, substitution feels like betrayal—to customers and to yourself. Budget accordingly.

## Q3: Supplier relationships and cultural sourcing as more than commodity inputs

- **Start hyper-local, even if it costs 10-15% more.** A practical first year: identify 2-3 ethnic specialty suppliers within the metro area (Filipino, Mexican, mixed) and commit to them, even if their prices are above Sysco baseline. The payoff is not just quality; it's narrative ("we source from Maria's import shop on Elm Street") and community relationships that survive food truck downturns.

- **Lock in volume commitments, protect them.** Once margins stabilize (month 6-8), offer suppliers a simple deal: "I'll guarantee you $X/month in orders if you commit to 10% better pricing on three key items." This gives them revenue predictability and you cost stability. No formal contracts needed; a handshake and monthly receipt tracking work for small suppliers.

- **Substitution is a three-option conversation, not an executive decision.** When your ube supplier's price jumps or they go out of stock, don't silently swap to a cheaper source. Call them, explain margin pressure, and ask: "Do you have a good alternative at a lower price, or should I source elsewhere temporarily?" They'll often find a creative solution or adjust pricing to keep your business. Treating them as partners, not transactional vendors, builds resilience.

- **Map your critical three.** Not every ingredient needs a relationship. Pick the three that define authenticity to you—ube, a specific adobo blend, nixtamalized corn, whatever. Build those supplier bonds deep. Everything else can be flexible. This reduces decision fatigue and keeps you sane.

- **Track cost and story together.** Each month, note not just the price per item but which supplier you bought from and any quality shifts. In year 2, when you have catering margin data, you'll see exactly which supplier relationships are worth premium prices and which could flex. Data beats nostalgia.

## Q4: The cuisine as cultural infrastructure, flavor commons, and living canon

- **This is year 3+ thinking, not launch-day strategy.** Right now, your job is survival and profitability in a single truck. Reimagining yourself as a cultural institution requires stability, trust, and margin you don't have yet. Park this vision—it's real and it's valuable—but sequence it correctly.

- **Start building the foundation now, though, at near-zero cost.** Post recipe variations, customer riffs, and invited-guest collabs on social media: "tried lumpia tacos with Thai chili—game changer, tried it?" Ask customers for photo submissions of fusion dishes they make at home and reshare them. Collect stories from Filipino and Mexican grandmas about what they'd add to the menu. None of this costs money, but it plants seeds: you're creating a conversation, not a brand.

- **Develop a simple "open-source menu" model for year 2-3.** Once you have three successful truck months under your belt, publish a simple zine or PDF of five core recipes with the tagline "make these at home, add your own twist, show us the photo." This is culture infrastructure: you're giving people permission and method to join the creative process. It costs almost nothing and builds fierce loyalty.

- **Partner with a community cooking class or culinary school in year 2.** Teach a quarterly "Filipino-Mexican home cooking" workshop, charge $40/person, keep the margins thin. Now you're not selling fusion meals; you're stewarding a culinary tradition. Alumni become long-term catering clients and community ambassadors.

- **If you scale to a brick-and-mortar in year 3, design it as a hub.** Small kitchen in back, open counter in front, a community board for other chefs to post recipes/collabs, a shelf for selling products from other Filipino and Mexican food artisans (spice blends, sauces, pan dulce). You're not the only source of fusion anymore; you're the commons. This transforms you from a food truck into a cultural institution—and makes you harder to disrupt.
