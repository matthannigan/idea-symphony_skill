# Audience, Equity, and Cultural Authenticity - The Devil's Advocate

## 1. Who counts as customer, who is designed around, and the accessibility of the experience

- **The design assumes digital natives**: Chalkboard menus and social-media-only updates exclude non-English speakers and those without smartphone access. Mitigation: partner with a community center to post bilingual printed schedules; test card-payment systems that include alternative contact-less options (phone verification); pilot a simple SMS-based location service in partnership with local organizations serving elderly and unbanked populations.

- **Outdoor dining accessibility is overlooked**: Limited seating and standing-only lines exclude elderly and mobility-impaired customers. Have you considered reserving shaded seating near the truck, or designing a "call-ahead micro-order" system that lets accessibility-limited customers skip the line? This could also reduce pressure on the service window.

- **Cultural resonance doesn't guarantee inclusion**: The fusion concept may attract adventurous young professionals while missing the very communities whose cuisines are featured. Mitigation: conduct listening sessions with Filipino and Mexican community organizations early; co-design offerings with them rather than assuming you know what "authentic fusion" means to them.

- **Transactional friction compounds existing barriers**: "Card-under-pressure" ordering creates anxiety for people unfamiliar with the menu or uncomfortable with financial exposure. One approach: pre-ordering through a simple website or phone line, with payment collected flexibly (before or after pickup), removes real-time pressure and opens access.

## 2. Cultural authenticity vs. fusion tourism, and the emotional tax of being "exotic"

- **"Fusion" can mask extraction**: Without explicit community partnership, this reads as cultural arbitrage—taking others' cuisines, rebranding them as trendy, and profiting. The founder's heritage becomes a differentiator, not a shared resource. Mitigation: establish a formal advisory board of Filipino and Mexican community members with decision-making power (not just decorative input); commit to transparent profit-sharing or reinvestment into community food enterprises.

- **The founder's emotional labor is uncompensated**: Marketing personal heritage as "exotic" requires emotional exposure and invites exoticizing commentary ("So unique!"). Have you considered whether this feels empowering or extractive long-term? Framing the cuisine as a cultural commons—"This is *our* food, made for everyone"—shifts the narrative from performer to steward.

- **Authenticity claims collapse under scrutiny**: Fusion itself is historically inauthentic, yet marketing it as "authentic fusion" sets a contradiction. Instead of claiming authenticity, consider owning the creative reimagining: "We're building something new that honors both cuisines." This is more honest and harder to delegitimize.

- **Community validation takes time and resources**: Quick social media credibility won't substitute for real relationship-building. Mitigation: allocate 10–15% of early revenue to community sponsorships, food donations to Filipino and Mexican cultural events, and paid advisory roles for community members. This signals commitment beyond marketing.

## 3. Supplier relationships and cultural sourcing as more than commodity inputs

- **Margin pressure forces substitution faster than expected**: Starting with authentic specialty suppliers is noble, but as volume grows and margins tighten, the pull toward commodity chains is immense. Once you switch to mass-market ube or generic adobo spices, customers may not taste the difference, but the food loses something—and the supplier loses revenue. Have you mapped break-even scenarios that *require* substitution, and if so, negotiated long-term pricing locks with specialty suppliers in advance?

- **"Authentic suppliers" lack supply chain transparency**: Small immigrant-owned spice vendors may have their own sourcing pressures and may not share your values on labor or environmental standards. Mitigation: invest in direct supplier visits; ask questions about *their* sourcing; if scaling, consider co-investing in supply chain stability (e.g., bulk purchasing agreements that give the supplier predictable demand).

- **Relationship-building is expensive upfront**: Smaller suppliers can't always compete on price or volume. Building genuine relationships means tolerating higher costs initially, risking margin compression. One approach: start with 2–3 core specialty suppliers for signature items (ube, adobo spices), and allow commodity substitution only for supporting ingredients; this keeps authenticity where it matters most.

- **Switching costs trap you**: Once customers taste authentic ube horchata, switching to imitation ube will trigger backlash and erode trust. Mitigation: build supply chain resilience before you hit scale—establish relationships with multiple specialty vendors for each key ingredient, and negotiate flexibility on lead times and volumes.

## 4. The cuisine as cultural infrastructure: reimagining this business as a living canon rather than proprietary brand

- **"Living canon" model conflicts with profit maximization**: If you're designing for community contribution and iteration (home cooks adding recipes, other chefs collaborating), the business can't easily monetize or control the narrative. A proprietary brand is defensible; a commons is not. Have you modeled the business case for this? Does it require grant funding, mission-driven investment, or a cooperative structure to be sustainable?

- **Intellectual property becomes murky**: If Filipino-Mexican fusion becomes a "cultural commons," who owns variations? What happens when competitors claim they're also authentically participating in the canon? Mitigation: clarify upfront that the truck is the *initial steward*, not the permanent authority, and establish transparent governance for what gets included in the canon (advisory board votes, community workshops).

- **Community participation requires heavy coordination**: A living canon only works if it's actively maintained and accessible to contribute to. This means regular tasting events, collaborative recipe development, public documentation—significant labor beyond running a food truck. Mitigation: partner with a cultural center or community organization to share curatorial and coordination burden; the truck becomes a node in a larger ecosystem, not the sole steward.

- **Scaling a commons is risky**: What if the canon grows faster than you can support, or attracts contributors with goals misaligned with the original vision (commercialization, appropriation)? Mitigation: establish a written manifesto for the canon *before* it grows; define values, decision-making processes, and how to handle conflict. A small, intentional community can scale safely; an unguided commons can become chaotic.
