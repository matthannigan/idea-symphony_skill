# Capital Structure: SBA Loan, Equipment, and Sequencing - The Pragmatist

## Q1: The SBA loan as values, math, and debt-service load

- **Recalibrate the break-even threshold upward**: At $450–600/month debt service, you're essentially adding a 12–18% surcharge to gross revenue just to service the obligation. For a single truck targeting 60% food cost, you need roughly $18K–20K monthly revenue to hit breakeven. With the loan, push that to $22K–23K. This is feasible but tighter. Run a sensitivity: if month three only delivers $16K, you're burning $3K–4K monthly. That's the real risk.

- **Use debt service as the forcing function for unit economics discipline**: Instead of viewing the loan as "extra capital," treat the monthly payment as a non-negotiable operational constraint. It forces you to hit daily sales targets religiously. This can be a plus—it keeps you honest. But it means zero margin for slow summer months or seasonal dips.

- **Front-load validation before borrowing**: Spend 2–3 months running a stripped-down operating model—prepped meals from a commissary, catering events, festivals—to prove unit economics. Once you demonstrate $18K+ monthly revenue consistently, the SBA loan becomes a strategic accelerant, not a lifeline. The lender will respect this track record and may offer better terms.

- **Debt service should peak early and decline fast**: Structure the SBA repayment to front-load principal, so by month 18–24 your monthly obligation drops and cash flow improves. This aligns with the trajectory: high sales velocity drives payoff faster and de-risks the business faster.

- **Hold 4–6 weeks of debt service in reserve**: Don't let working capital reserves dip below $2,400–3,600. One slow month can't spiral into a missed payment. That's a credit catastrophe.

## Q2: Equipment acquisition — buy vs. lease vs. used, with the sunk-cost exit path

- **New truck ($75–150K): Only if you're confident in supply chain or have a white-label operator relationship**: A new build takes 6–10 weeks, costs $20K–30K in upfits, and ties up $80K+ that could be safety capital. Only viable if you're scaling to three trucks immediately or already have catering contracts to justify the capex. For a first unit, this is premature.

- **Used truck ($20–50K): The practical play with one major caveat**: You get moving in 2–3 weeks, preserve $40K–60K in capital, and maintain optionality. The maintenance risk is real—budget 10% of the truck's purchase price annually for repairs. A $35K truck should reserve $3,500/year ($290/month) for unexpected engine or electrical work. If it fails catastrophically in year two, you've learned the concept is viable and can upgrade with more confidence (or walk away with less sunk cost).

- **Lease ($1,500–3,000/month): Only if you're testing a concept or pivot is imminent**: Leasing preserves capital and transfers maintenance risk, but eats $18K–36K annually. At that burn rate, you need to be clearing $25K+ monthly to justify the cost. Better for events or seasonal testing, not for the core daily operation.

- **The optionality trade-off is the real equation**: A $35K used truck gives you an exit: if the business stalls, you liquidate the truck for $15K–20K, take the loss, and redeploy the capital. That loss is cheaper than 18 months of lease payments ($27K–54K). Psychologically, too, owning gives you skin in the game without overcommitting.

- **Sequence the decision point: 6-month operational test before equipment escalation**: Buy a cheap, reliable used truck once you've proven daily revenue targets through catering and events. This is not "delay and lose momentum"—it's "validate before scaling." You'll make better equipment decisions when you actually know your volume patterns and seasonal swing.

## Q3: Capital allocation sequencing and the $100K that is not what it seems

- **Map the true spend in detail before borrowing a dime**: Truck deposit/down payment ($5K–10K), initial commissary fees and setup ($2K–5K), permits and licenses ($1K–3K), POS/payments infrastructure ($1.5K–3K), initial food inventory ($2K–4K), working capital buffer ($6K–10K), kitchen tools and small equipment ($1K–2K). You're already at $18.5K–37K. Add vehicle insurance ($150–200/month × first 6 months = $1.2K), business insurance, and you're closing on $40K before you even buy a truck.

- **Frontload the commissary and permits—these are hard stops**: You cannot operate without them, so lock in those costs first. Permits can take 4–8 weeks; start the paperwork before you even secure financing. Commissary agreements are month-to-month usually, so nail down the location and kitchen schedule early.

- **Protect the working capital reserve aggressively**: Of your $100K, at least $8K–12K should be untouched operational buffer. This covers two slow months of fixed costs (rent, insurance, commissary) and is the difference between weathering a seasonal dip and defaulting on the truck lease or loan.

- **Sequence around cash flow timing**: Permits and commissary setup take time but cost less. Do those first while you're still working your day job. Equipment and inventory come next, once permits are approved. This staggering means you're not burning capital while waiting for regulatory clearance.

- **The hidden assumption to rebuild: utilization volatility**: Most operators assume trucks are rented or used 80%+ of available days. But a new single-truck operation often runs 5–6 days/week, weather-dependent, with seasonal swings of 20–40%. Your capital budget should include 2–3 months of negative cash flow (losses) absorbed by that working capital buffer. Without it, the first slow summer kills you.

## Q4: Bootstrapping as a credibility signal for the SBA loan decision

- **6 months of proof beats paperwork every time**: Run the operation for two quarters—spring/summer and early fall—without the SBA loan. Demonstrate consistent unit economics, ticket average, customer retention. Then approach the SBA with actual P&L, not projections. You'll get better terms, lower rates, and the lender will see you've already de-risked the core concept.

- **This flips the psychological dynamic**: You're no longer asking, "Will you lend me money to start?"; you're saying, "We've proven the model. Now we want to accelerate growth or add a second unit." The lender's risk calculus changes radically. You might even qualify for a larger, lower-rate loan with less personal guarantee.

- **The timing risk is real, though**: If you bootstrap for six months and the city ordinance passes restrictions in month five, you've lost leverage. But you'll also have real cash on hand, not borrowed money, so you're in a stronger position to pivot or absorb the regulatory hit.

- **Hybrid approach: Seek a line of credit, not a term loan**: Talk to the SBA about a $20K–30K revolving line of credit before you launch. You don't draw it unless you need it—maybe it stays at zero for six months. But having it pre-approved signals seriousness to suppliers and gives you emergency runway. Then, once you've proven six-month unit economics, convert to a term loan for a second truck or brick-and-mortar deposit.

- **The credibility signal cuts both ways**: Bootstrapping shows discipline; taking a strategic loan shows confidence in the model. The key is timing: don't borrow to prove the concept; borrow to scale a proven concept. That's when debt works hardest for you.

## Q5: The venture-funded startup vs. the bootstrapped craft brewery

- **You are neither, and that's actually clarifying**: Venture-funded startups need rapid market saturation to justify capital burn and exit multiples. Craft breweries bootstrap because they're capital-intensive but benefit enormously from owner sweat equity and slow brand building. A food truck is closer to the brewery, but with shorter payoff horizons.

- **The asymmetric failure modes point toward bootstrap-first**: Venture funding assumes some failure is acceptable on the way to one breakout win. You're a solo founder with $60K savings—a failure wipes out your personal runway and damages your credit. Debt (SBA) is worse: it persists even if the concept fails. So the question is not "can I grow fast?" but "can I afford to learn from mistakes?" Bootstrapping lets you afford mistakes; debt doesn't.

- **But growth-at-any-cost is a false frame**: Taking the $40K SBA loan doesn't commit you to venture-style burn. You can take the loan and operate conservatively—deploy it for a second truck once the first proves profitable, not to subsidize low margins or unprofitable expansion. Disciplined borrowing is possible; it's just less common in food service.

- **The sweet spot for your situation**: Bootstrap for 4–6 months to validate the core truck and unit economics. Then take a modest SBA loan ($20K–30K) to fund a second truck or catering infrastructure, *not* to replace your need to hit weekly revenue targets. This hybrid approach combines the learning benefits of bootstrapping with the scaling leverage of strategic debt.

- **Solo founder with no prior business ownership**: This is the key constraint. You're also learning operations, managing people (even one employee), and navigating regulatory landmines simultaneously. Overlevering (the $40K loan from day one) means you're executing a growth plan while learning how to run a basic business. That's two competencies at once. Bootstrap first, add leverage later, once you've built operational muscle memory. Growth without foundations is how first-time founders tank.
