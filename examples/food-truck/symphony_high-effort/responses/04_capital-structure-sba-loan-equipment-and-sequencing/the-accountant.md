# Capital Structure: SBA Loan, Equipment, and Sequencing — The Accountant

## Q1: The SBA loan as values, math, and debt-service load

- **Debt-service math**: A $40K SBA microloan at ~8% APR over 5 years runs ~$740/month. Your break-even threshold (gross revenue needed to cover COGS, labor, truck costs, and debt) shifts from ~$12K/month (debt-free) to ~$13.5K/month. In slow months (30% revenue dip), you're burning $500–800 in cumulative debt while waiting for recovery. The real trap: if the truck averages $10K/month gross in year one, you'll service $450/month debt while barely covering operating costs — this creates cash bleed that forces personal capital injection or credit card use.

- **Debt-service as percentage of revenue**: At $12K/month gross, debt service is 6% of revenue. At $8K/month (realistic for a slow seasonal dip), it's 9% — acceptable but tight. Below $7K/month for sustained periods, you're underwater. The math suggests: *do not borrow unless you're confident in $11K+ monthly gross by month three*. That's a 2–3 location rotation + weekend catering minimum to hit regularly.

- **The psychological lock**: Borrowing from day one psychologically commits you to "make the revenue target happen" — which is sometimes discipline and sometimes panic that drives bad decisions (overpricing, unsustainable hours, cash-flow mismanagement). Bootstrapping forces you to prove unit economics *first*, then scale with confidence.

- **Trapped profit dilemma**: In year one with the $40K loan, your first $740/month of profit goes to debt service. That means by month 12, you've paid $8,880 in debt alone — profit that could have been reinvested in a second truck or catering equipment. If you're debt-free by month six (bootstrapped), that same profit is available for expansion.

- **Rate negotiation and the six-month track record**: SBA microloans at SCORE-affiliated lenders often have fixed rates, but if you apply after six months of proven profitability, you may qualify for a *business line of credit* at better terms (prime + 1–2%) with more flexible draw timing. This is the leverage of the bootstrap play: you get better terms later because you've de-risked the lender's view of the business.

## Q2: Equipment acquisition — buy vs. lease vs. used, with the sunk-cost exit path

- **Total cost of ownership (36-month horizon)**: New truck outfitted ($100–120K) + loan interest + insurance ($150/month) + maintenance reserve (15% annually) = ~$145K total by year three. Leasing ($2,000/month) = $72K over 36 months, plus responsibility for excess wear (potential $2–5K). Used truck ($30K) + higher maintenance reserve (25% annually, $7.5K) + depreciation risk ($5K residual by year three) = ~$50–55K total. Leasing looks cleanest on paper, but you have no asset and lose equity-building that could fund truck #2.

- **Exit value and optionality**: A new truck financed over five years is a liability if the concept underperforms by month 12. You're locked into $100K+ debt with a depreciating asset worth $60–70K. A leased truck can be surrendered with 30–60 days notice and a return penalty (~$2–3K). A used truck bought for $30K cash becomes your "fallback asset" — if revenue dries up, you have a known, modest sunk cost, not a debt trap. *Used is the option-preserving play.*

- **The cash-flow angle**: Leasing preserves immediate cash and spreads cost over revenue-generating months — month one you're fully operational without $30K going out the door. Buying forces a front-loaded capital commitment. For a solo founder with $100K total capitalization, leasing lets you reserve $30K for commissary setup, permits, inventory, and cash reserve — buying front-loads the truck and starves other essential inputs.

- **Maintenance and utilization coupling**: A new truck with a warranty (year one) has predictable costs. A used truck has unknown hidden costs. But if you run the truck 4 days/week (not 7), a used truck's higher maintenance expense (20–30% of truck value annually) is *real* — that's another $6–9K annually. A leased truck's maintenance is usually included; a financed new truck has warranty. *If you can't commit to 5+ days/week, leasing or financing new is cheaper than buying used.*

- **The reinvestment angle**: If you lease and net $3K/month profit by month six, you have cash for catering equipment, a second location deposit, or inventory without taking on additional debt. If you own a used truck free and clear but have no other capital, you're stuck waiting to accumulate funds for expansion — slower compounding, but *no debt risk*. The question is: what's your risk tolerance and expansion timeline?

## Q3: Capital allocation sequencing and the $100K that is not what it seems

- **The vanishing $100K**: Truck lease/purchase deposit (first month + security, $2–5K), commissary kitchen deposit + 3 months prep-time fees ($3–6K), permits/licenses (food handler, truck license, vending permits, ~$1–2K), POS system and payment processing setup ($1–2K), initial inventory (ingredients + smallwares, $3–5K), working capital reserve for slow months (critical: $8–15K to cover payroll + fuel through Q4), and miscellaneous (signage, initial marketing, unforeseen, $2–3K). *Before you sell a single plate, you've spent $25–40K.* You have $60–75K left for all expansion.

- **The hidden assumptions that collapse**: Many operators analogize to established trucks and assume "you'll hit profitability in month two" — but they ignore that those trucks often had owner-supplied free labor, existing food cost relationships, or a pre-existing customer base. Your fusion cuisine is novel; customer acquisition takes 2–3 months minimum. Adjust your reserve from $8K to $15K, and your usable buffer shrinks.

- **Optimal sequencing**: (1) Permits and licenses first ($2K, ~2 weeks) — without this, nothing else matters. (2) Commissary kitchen agreement and initial fees ($5K, locks your food-prep location). (3) Used truck or lease agreement ($2–5K initial, operational by week 3–4). (4) POS and payment setup ($1.5K, week 2–3, in parallel with truck). (5) Initial inventory ($3–4K, week 3). (6) Reserve the remaining $30–35K for working capital, second location deposit, or a catering/event expansion within months 4–6. Do not commit to owned-truck debt until month 4 financial actuals prove $10K+ monthly gross.

- **The danger zone**: If you sequence wrong (new truck first, then scramble for commissary), you'll spend April–May waiting for permits while paying truck lease/financing and building no revenue. Sequence permits → kitchen → truck → inventory. This de-risks each step: you know you *can* operate before you commit capital to a truck.

- **Cash reserve sizing**: With $100K total capital, a conservative reserve should be 15–20% ($15–20K). This covers two bad months at $8K/month operating burn (payroll, fuel, commissary, truck costs). If you spend $85K on the truck and startup costs, you're dangerously under-reserved. A $30–35K reserve is uncomfortable but necessary for a solo founder with no safety net.

## Q4: Bootstrapping as a credibility signal for the SBA loan decision

- **The track record advantage**: Running for six months without debt and achieving $10K+ monthly gross on a consistent basis is *gold* with lenders. You've proven the unit economics, demonstrated personal discipline, and shown you can navigate permitting and seasonal variation. Lenders will refinance or increase credit at better terms because the risk profile has dropped dramatically. You move from "unproven founder" to "owner with demonstrated profitability."

- **Interest rate and term negotiation**: A $40K line of credit (unsecured or equipment-secured) after six months of profitability might run 6–7% vs. 8–9% for day-one borrowing — *that's $80–100/month in savings*. Over 36 months, you're saving $2,880–3,600. More importantly, lenders may offer flexible draw timing (borrow only when you need it for expansion), not a lump sum you're paying interest on while sitting in cash.

- **The strategic signal to investors or partners**: If you expand to a second truck or add catering staff, existing profitability shows you can manage growth organically. You're not desperate; you're *choosing* to accelerate with capital. This shifts negotiating power — you can approach suppliers, partners, or co-founders from a position of strength, not from need.

- **The hidden cost of day-one borrowing**: Taking $40K at day one sends a signal that you *need* it to get started — which may come across to the city, suppliers, or partners as "this founder is under-capitalized or uncertain." Arriving at month six with $10K/month gross and *then* asking for a $40K line says "I've proven the model; I'm expanding it." The psychology matters in loan approval and negotiation.

- **The asymmetric failure mode**: If the business stumbles in months 1–2 (permits delayed, unexpected competition, regulatory changes), you're personally liable for a $40K loan while with zero revenue. Bootstrapping means you take an $8K loss instead of a $40K + loan obligation loss. The downside protection of bootstrapping is worth significant upside delay.

## Q5: The venture-funded startup vs. the bootstrapped craft brewery

- **Venture-fueled model (SBA loan)**: You borrow $40K, operate aggressively, hit $15K/month gross by month four, and lock in a second truck lease by month six. Year one: $120K gross revenue, $40K COGS, $20K labor, $20K truck/commissary/overhead = $40K profit, minus $8,880 debt service = $31K net. By year two, with truck #2, you're at $180K gross, $60K total debt service. This model wins if you have high conviction in the product, customer demand is strong, and you need speed to capture market share before competitors copy the fusion concept.

- **Bootstrap model (craft brewery playbook)**: You operate with $60K, hit $8K/month gross by month two, allocate $2K/month to cash reserve and incremental equipment, and launch catering/events by month four. Year one: $90K gross, $30K COGS, $15K labor, $15K truck/commissary/overhead = $30K profit, all retained. By month 18, you have $50K+ in cash and zero debt, and you launch a second truck with that capital. Year two: $180K gross, $100K debt-free (you own both trucks outright).

- **The asymmetric risk comparison**: SBA model: higher upside if execution is perfect (speed to market, scale), but catastrophic downside if demand is lower than expected (trapped in debt, forced to liquidate). Bootstrap model: lower upside (slower growth), but graceful exit path (you can always pivot, sell the truck, or exit with minimal loss). For a solo founder with *no prior business ownership*, the bootstrap model is rationally defensible. You're trading growth speed for existential safety.

- **The product advantage in your context**: Filipino-Mexican fusion is novel in a mid-size city. If the product resonates, customers will seek you out even if you operate 3 days/week initially. Venture-model speed matters if you're racing against a copycat; bootstrap model works if you have unique positioning. Given that four competitors already exist, you're not the first-mover; you're differentiation-based. Differentiation can grow slowly.

- **The personal-brand angle**: A bootstrapped founder who builds a profitable truck and scales to a second without debt is a stronger personal brand than one who borrowed day one. If you later pitch suppliers, co-founders, or banks for a brick-and-mortar loan, "I self-funded two trucks to $200K revenue" beats "I borrowed $40K and scaled aggressively." The craft brewery model builds the narrative of a disciplined operator, which unlocks larger capital later on better terms.
