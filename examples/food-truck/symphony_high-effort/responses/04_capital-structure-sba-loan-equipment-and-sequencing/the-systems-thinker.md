# Capital Structure: SBA Loan, Equipment, and Sequencing — The Systems Thinker

## Q1: The SBA loan as values, math, and debt-service load

1. **Early debt as a reinforcing tightness loop.** Taking $40K at month one locks in $450–600/month outflows before you've validated menu-market fit or weather patterns. This creates cascading pressure: tighter margins force faster scaling, which attracts less deliberate customer research; slower deliberate scaling now feels irresponsible because debt compounds month-over-month. At month 6, you're trading growth speed for solvency, not optionality. The leverage shifts from "we own our fate" to "the payment schedule owns the timeline." Wait until you've proven unit economics at $4K–5K/week revenue (roughly 8–12 weeks of operation) before borrowing, then the loan funds acceleration, not survival.

2. **Break-even shifts $1,000–1,200/month northward with debt service.** Without debt, break-even on $60K might be ~$2,500/week gross revenue. With debt, it becomes $3,000+/week. That's a 20% higher hurdle during your seasonal valley months (Q4 in many regions). The real cost isn't the interest rate—it's the months you can't operate at a loss during the learning curve. Map actual seasonal demand in your city for food trucks before deciding; if Q4 drops 30%, debt service in November becomes a death spiral.

3. **Debt service at 10% of gross is the illusion of control.** At $6K/week gross, $600/month debt is exactly 10%. But that's before food cost (30%), labor (30%), truck payments/fuel (15%), permits (2–3%), and unexpected breakdowns. You're really at 5–7% of profit, which evaporates with a single slow week or a $1,500 compressor repair. The psychological relief of "being under 10%" masks that you have almost no margin for error or reinvestment.

4. **Interest rate as a leading indicator of real risk.** If the SBA quotes you 7–8%, that rate embeds their assessment of your specific risk profile (solo founder, unproven cuisine in this market, competitive environment). Don't treat the rate as "reasonable" and move on. Use it as data: what is the SBA seeing that they're pricing in 7.5% instead of 4.5%? That gap is where your real risk lives.

5. **Borrow only when revenue validates, then borrow boldly for unit two.** Resist the psychological pull to "secure capital early while you can." Instead, operate 4–6 months on cash, hit $3,500+/week consistent revenue, then take a larger SBA loan ($50–60K) at lower risk and with demonstrated unit economics. Timing this right turns debt from anchor into accelerant.

## Q2: Equipment acquisition — buy vs. lease vs. used, with the sunk-cost exit path

1. **New truck ($75–150K) as a sunk-cost trap with a hidden secondhand market exit.** The system works like this: buying new commits you to ownership psychology (even if financially irrational), which keeps you operating even when unit economics don't improve. The truck becomes an identity anchor, not a business asset. Used trucks ($20–50K, 3–8 years) embed unknown maintenance risk, but more importantly, they preserve the psychological exit: you can walk away from a $35K asset at month 9 if the concept fails, losing $10K as a learning fee. A new $100K truck? You're sunk for 5+ years because the emotional burden of "wasting" a six-figure investment keeps you pushing. Buy used for the first 18 months; that optionality is worth $15–20K in avoided path-dependence.

2. **Leasing ($1,500–3,000/month) trades capital for flexibility, but inverts your incentives.** Over 36 months, a $2,000/month lease costs $72K total—comparable to used-truck purchase. But lease economics create a perverse dynamic: you're paying for someone else's asset, which feels wasteful if volume is low. Owned trucks let you "push through" slow months without the sting of idle lease payments. Used trucks give you both: lower capital commitment ($20–35K) and the option to hold without monthly pressure. Lease only if you expect to grow to unit two by month 18 and want to avoid the hassle of reselling; otherwise, the financial and psychological math favors used purchase.

3. **Maintenance reserve as the invisible true cost.** A new truck costs $500–800/month in deferred maintenance (depreciation math). Used trucks run $300–500/month actual out-of-pocket reserves (worn components fail faster). Many founders forget this entirely when comparing buy vs. lease, then hit month 10 when the compressor dies ($2K–5K repair) and realize they're undercapitalized. Budget used-truck maintenance at 8% of purchase price annually, then shelter that reserve *separate* from working capital. A $30K used truck needs $200/month in maintenance cash you don't touch.

4. **Depreciation and opportunity cost flip the math depending on scaling plans.** If you'll own unit one for 18 months then shift to unit two, a used truck losing 30% of value ($9K on a $30K purchase) is actually cheaper than a new truck depreciating 40% over the same period. But if you plan multi-unit fleet within 24 months, that used truck might be the anchor that slows your transition (you keep operating it instead of consolidating to newer, more efficient units). Model your specific growth timeline before choosing; the buy/lease decision is downstream of the scale decision.

5. **Leasing's real advantage: you hand the equipment-management risk to someone else.** If your focus should be on menu development and customer relationships, not oil changes and brake replacements, a lease absorbs that operational tax. At $2,000/month, you're paying ~$60–80/month per operational issue you don't have to solve. If that marginal cognitive load is your binding constraint (you're the cook *and* the operator), lease. If you have time to manage maintenance, buy used.

## Q3: Capital allocation sequencing and the $100K that is not what it seems

1. **The disappearing act: $100K consumed in predictable order.** Truck deposit or down-payment: $15–30K. Commissary kitchen retrofit/deposit: $2–5K. Permits, licenses, health inspections: $3–5K. POS system, card processing, tech stack: $2–3K. Initial inventory (food, paper, packaging): $5–8K. Working capital reserve (cash to operate 4–6 weeks before revenue stabilizes): $20–30K. Vehicle insurance and bond: $2–3K. Buffer for permits you didn't know existed: $5–10K. Total: $54–84K, and you've barely moved. The remaining $16–46K is *everything else*—and it evaporates in month two when you discover commissary costs $800/month (not $500), permits take 8 weeks not 4, and your first event gig requires $1K in packaging you didn't anticipate.

2. **Sequence for optionality, not efficiency.** Tempting order: truck first (biggest expense, feels urgent). Better order: permits and commissary kitchen access first (4–8 week lead times; de-risk regulatory path before committing capital). Then truck. Why? Because many founders commit $30K to a truck, then discover local regulations changed or permit delays mean no operating windows until Q3. De-risk the regulatory path first using $5–10K of your $100K, confirm you have a operating window, *then* spend $30K on the truck. That resequencing saves $30K in wasted capital 30% of the time.

3. **Working capital reserve as the system shock absorber.** If you allocate $20K to reserve (20% of your total capital), slow months stop being death spirals. Month one slow? The reserve carries payroll and commissary fees. Month two? The reserve buys time to pivot menu or try new locations. But this reserve lives in a separate account *untouched* and psychologically protected. Most founders raid it by month 4 to "optimize cash flow" and then face month 6 with zero buffer. Protect the reserve like a regulatory requirement, not a decision variable.

4. **Hidden assumptions that collapse on closer inspection.** Many founders budget commissary at $500/month (asked one peer). Actual cost in this city: $800–1,200 depending on seasonal rent. Permits assumed $400 total; actual trajectory is $150 application + $200 annual + $100 for each location change. POS system assumed $200 setup; actual is $500 setup + $80/month. Build a six-month detailed budget for *your* city specifically, not by analogy. That usually adds $8–15K to the true pre-launch capital need.

5. **Spend in waves: pre-launch validation, then operational commitment.** Wave 1 ($10–15K): permits, health department pre-clearance, 2–4 week market testing with a borrowed or rented kitchen. Wave 2 ($30–40K): truck and commissary setup once permits and initial sales traction are real. Wave 3 ($20–30K): working capital and scale-focused inventory. This sequencing prevents the $100K from vanishing in parallel commitments; instead, each wave validates the next.

## Q4: Bootstrapping as a credibility signal for the SBA loan decision

1. **Six months of proof outweighs a business plan you haven't tested.** If you operate at break-even or small profit for 6 months—publishing weekly revenue, food costs, operating hours—you've generated the actual data an SBA underwriter uses to calibrate risk. A pre-launch business plan projecting $5K/week by month 3 is a guess. Actual week-by-week revenue history showing you hit $3,500/week by month 4 is a contract with reality. Most SBA loan officers understand this. Demonstrate discipline now, borrow bigger and cheaper later. This is the opposite of VC logic (where you borrow big to prove traction fast); it's craft-business logic.

2. **The credibility signal is self-discipline, not revenue.** You could hit $6K/week by month 3 but blow all profits on a second truck down payment. Or hit $2,500/week but maintain 40% net margins and a full reserve. When you apply for an SBA loan after 6 months, the underwriter is asking: "Did this founder spend money on the right things, or did they gold-plate the operation and run themselves into a corner?" Bootstrapping discipline answers that. Take the $40K loan at month 6 at 8.5% if you've been chaotic; take it at 6.5% if you've been tight.

3. **Delayed borrowing buys you the ability to borrow bigger without dilution.** If you bootstrap 6 months on $60K, hit $3,500+/week with validated unit economics, you can now approach the SBA for $75–100K (rather than $40K) at demonstrably lower risk. The interest rate drop (potentially 1–2%) plus access to $35K more capital is worth the 6-month delay. You're not losing time; you're converting time into credibility that compounds into cheaper capital and more options.

4. **The bootstrap period surfaces hidden costs that a loan would mask.** Months 2–4, you'll discover unexpected expenses, seasonal patterns, or operational friction that no pre-launch analysis predicted. Funding these out of cash forces you to understand your unit economics deeply. If you had the SBA loan, you might paper over these problems with cheap capital instead of solving them. Six months of bootstrapping makes you a better operator *and* a better capital allocator.

5. **Risk: six months of opportunity cost if the concept fails fast.** If you launch, hit week two, and realize the market doesn't want Filipino-Mexican fusion in this neighborhood, six months of bootstrap is a sunk-loss. Taking the $40K loan at month one and pivoting faster saves the time cost. So this strategy only works if you have medium-high confidence in the concept; if you're genuinely uncertain, borrow faster to preserve optionality. Know which situation you're in.

## Q5: The venture-funded startup vs. the bootstrapped craft brewery

1. **Venture speed requires unit economics to improve as you scale; craft businesses survive if they stay small.** A venture-funded model burns $5K/month to acquire customers and market share, betting that unit economics get better at $10K/week revenue (the 3–5x revenue multiplier covers acquisition cost). A bootstrapped model starts profitable or break-even and keeps operating at that steady state. For a solo founder with no business background running one food truck, the venture assumption is *dangerous*: you don't have the organizational complexity to absorb that burn rate. A craft brewery that sells 300 barrels/year at 30% margin stays profitable; the owner isn't tempted to expand because the existing business works. Similarly, a food truck hitting $3,500/week with 25% net margin is *already winning*. The temptation to take the $40K loan and chase $7K/week is venture logic in a craft-business shell, and it often ends in overleverage.

2. **Bootstrap is the better model for solo founders *if* you have enough starting capital to avoid desperation spending.** With $60K in savings, you have a 3–4 month runway at break-even. That's enough to survive learning, validate menu, and hit consistent revenue before making the big equipment decisions. Venture would require $100K+ to fund the burn, and you'd have to move fast. Craft lets you move at the speed of learning. This founder's situation *is* craft-business, not venture, so fight the VC narrative.

3. **Failure-mode asymmetry: venture loses the investor's capital; craft loses the founder's capital and time.** A venture-backed food truck startup that fails after 18 months has spent $90K of someone else's money. The founder still has their skills. A bootstrapped truck that fails has spent $40K of the founder's savings *and* 18 months of their time. The founder's downside is asymmetrically worse. This should bias toward conservatism, not aggression. Only borrow if the validation evidence is very strong.

4. **The SBA loan is a craft-business tool for steady-state scaling, not venture capture.** The $40K SBA loan at 6–8% is perfectly appropriate *after* validation (month 6+) to fund a second truck or a catering commissary. It's not a launch-capital replacement. Using it as launch capital is borrowing venture thinking without venture resources. Wrong structure.

5. **Your unfair advantage isn't capital, it's culinary talent and founder psychology.** If you're an exceptional cook with a unique fusion menu that customers crave, your constraint is operational complexity and customer acquisition, not capital. Bootstrap until you hit a bottleneck you can't solve with time and taste. That bottleneck might be "I need a second truck to serve all the events booked," not "I need a better truck from day one." Solve with leverage, not capital, as long as you can.
