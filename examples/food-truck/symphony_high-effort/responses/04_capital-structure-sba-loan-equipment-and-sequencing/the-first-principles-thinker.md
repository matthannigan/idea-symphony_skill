# Capital Structure: SBA Loan, Equipment, and Sequencing — The First Principles Thinker

## Q1: The SBA Loan as Values, Math, and Debt-Service Load

- **Strip away psychology—math first:** At its core, this is about whether $450–600/month in fixed debt service materially changes the break-even threshold. If gross revenue is projected at $3K–5K weekly, debt service is 3–8% of revenue—not crippling. The real danger is slower ramp-up than projected. The fundamental question: How bad is a six-month slower climb to 100 trucks per day? That reframes the SBA decision from "debt burden" to "ramp-rate risk."

- **Invert the risk:** Borrowing delays nothing; not borrowing loses the startup window and delays growth. If the food truck scene is competitive now but stabilizing in 18 months, the true cost of bootstrapping is market share and positioning, not saved interest.

- **Hidden assumption check:** Most loan models assume debt service is rigid. In reality, if revenue lags, owner income vanishes first. The 10% rule (debt under 10% of gross) is an industry analogy, not a law. What percentage actually preserves operator margin and cash buffer for a one-truck operation running 5 days/week?

- **Threshold clarity:** Monthly revenue needs to hit $4,500–6,000 for debt service to feel comfortable AND preserve working capital and owner draw. Below that, either the debt term extends (higher total interest) or the loan wasn't sized right.

- **The debt-as-option framing:** Debt is not a liability—it's an option on growth. The microloan buys speed. If speed converts to market position and additional unit capacity within 24 months, interest paid was the cost of optionality, not a burden.

## Q2: Equipment Acquisition — Buy vs. Lease vs. Used, with the Sunk-Cost Exit Path

- **Rebuild from purpose, not precedent:** Why do most food trucks own equipment? Habit. Culinary professionals own their knives; the analogy extended to trucks. But fundamentally: what flexibility does the founder value more—fixed-cost predictability or optionality to pivot? That drives buy/lease/used.

- **36-month total cost with hidden costs exposed:** Buying new ($100K) has principal + interest + maintenance reserves ($500/month) + registration/insurance changes over time. Leasing ($2K/month) is $72K over 36 months but includes maintenance and exit at month 13 or 25 at no residual-value risk. Used ($30K purchase) adds $200–400/month maintenance uncertainty and depreciation sink. On paper, lease looks wasteful; in optionality, it's insurance.

- **Exit path as design criterion:** If concept falters in month 10, leased truck walks away with minimal loss. Owned truck (new or used) becomes a stranded asset. Owned vehicle with debt becomes liability + debt. For a solo founder testing fusion positioning, the exit value is worth paying lease premium.

- **Maintenance opacity in used purchases:** A $35K used truck with "full maintenance records" hides 2–3 years of deferred wear. Six months in, surprise $2K compressor failure burns through buffer. New equipment shifts risk to manufacturer; used equipment transfers risk to founder. That's worth quantifying.

- **Second unit optionality:** Leasing preserves capital for a second unit in year 2. Owning one truck locks $100K in equity and debt service. Fundamental question: Is one owned truck better than two leased trucks? For reach and positioning, probably not.

## Q3: Capital Allocation Sequencing and the $100K That Is Not What It Seems

- **Reconstruct the burn line item by item:** Truck (owned or leased deposit): $10–50K. Commissary setup, licensing, permits: $3–5K. POS system, insurance upfront, initial food cost: $5–8K. Vehicle registration, contingency: $2–3K. That's $20–66K depending on buy/lease. The remaining $34–80K is working capital—and that's where most founders underestimate volatility.

- **Minimum viable working capital, not analogized guesses:** Historical rule of thumb says 6 months operating expense. But what's actual operating expense? If food cost is 30% of revenue and overhead (fuel, licensing, commissions) is 15%, then 45% of revenue must cover both. On $3K weekly revenue, that's $1,350/week or $5,400/month. Six months = $32,400 reserved. That's 32% of total capital—not guesswork, derivable.

- **The hidden assumption: pace of ramp:** Most analogized budgets assume 50 customers day one, ramping to 150 by month three. Reality: discovery takes time. If ramp is 30→80→120 over three months, early burn rate drops the safety margin by 40%. Sequencing should ask: What's the minimum spend to stay operational through month 4 if ramp is 50% slower than projected?

- **Right-size the sequence to volatility, not to average:** Food trucks are seasonal. If the first "slow month" hits month 5 (weather shift, school break, permit change), and revenue drops 30%, does the remaining working capital cover that? If not, capital sequencing failed. Spend to survive the valley, not the peak.

- **Reconstruct from unit economics backward:** Don't allocate capital to a truck first, then work out how long it lasts. Calculate: How many service days per month hit break-even gross margin? How many months of that pace fit in $100K? Sequence spending to maximize days to that threshold.

## Q4: Bootstrapping as a Credibility Signal for the SBA Loan Decision

- **Invert the narrative:** SBA lenders want proof of execution, not plans. Six months of real P&L—even modest numbers—tells them more than projections. If the founder runs month 1–6 debt-free and hits 70% of projected revenue, that's not failure; that's calibration. The loan application in month 7 now has actual unit economics, not analogies.

- **The strategic delay logic:** Bootstrap through the worst phase (market discovery, operational learning, regulatory surprises). By month 5–6, the founder knows: real daily customer count, actual food cost ratios, permitting timelines, seasonal volatility. Armed with that data, the SBA loan becomes a scale capital injection, not a startup life raft. Terms and rates improve because risk is quantified, not estimated.

- **Terms improvement pathway:** Month-7 loan application with six months of data might get better terms (lower rate or longer amortization) because underwriting moves from "first-time founder, unproven concept" to "founder with operational track record, validated unit economics." That discount might be 1–2% on interest or six-month grace period—measurable value.

- **Counterargument: speed tax vs. data value:** Waiting six months to borrow sacrifices market position, holiday season revenue, and growth window. If competitors capture 20% more regular customers in that window, the data-driven loan isn't worth it. Speed matters if the market is capturing share now. Data matters if the market is stable and founder learning compounds value.

- **The hybrid path:** Can the founder bootstrap months 1–3 (permitting, initial operations, market discovery) and apply for the SBA loan in month 4, with three months of real data? That splits the difference—proves execution while capturing most of the growth window.

## Q5: The Venture-Funded Startup vs. The Bootstrapped Craft Brewery

- **Strip to the core growth model:** Venture-backed assumes exponential growth via capital, speed, and market share capture. Bootstrapped assumes sustainable growth via strong unit economics and reinvestment. These are different games. Which does a Filipino-Mexican fusion food truck in a mid-size city fit?

- **Asymmetries of failure:** Venture path: burn $100K by month 9, hit revenue target or pivot. Lose capital but preserve time (move to next idea). Bootstrapped path: slow ramp, preserved equity, preserved optionality. Miss the market window but survive. For a solo founder with one shot and personal savings, bootstrapped failure is survivable; venture-style failure is catastrophic (debt + lost capital + time spent).

- **Market structure argument:** Venture works in markets with winner-take-most dynamics (social networks, payment platforms). Food trucks operate in local, relationship-driven markets where the third truck succeeds as much as the first truck, if positioned differently. Venture capital assumes scarcity; food trucks operate in abundance. Bootstrapped growth fits the market structure better.

- **Creative control and iteration:** Venture capital demands growth metrics and exit potential. Bootstrapped operation allows the founder to optimize for margin and satisfaction. If the goal is "sustainable operation with creative control," venture math is wrong. If the goal is "largest revenue and potential acquisition," venture math is right. Which is it?

- **The second-unit inflection:** First truck bootstrapped or micro-loaned proves concept. Second truck (month 12–15) is where growth capital makes sense. By then, the founder has proven execution and the risk profile has shifted. Sequencing: bootstrap proof, then borrow for scale. That's neither pure venture nor pure bootstrap—it's option-sequencing.
