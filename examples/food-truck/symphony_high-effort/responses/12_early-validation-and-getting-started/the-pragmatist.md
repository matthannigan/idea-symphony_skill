# Early Validation and Getting Started - The Pragmatist

## Q1: The smallest experiment that builds confidence before full launch

- **Start with a farmers market pilot (4–6 weeks, $2–5K investment)**: Rent a 10x10 booth on a weekend, pre-prep menu items at a commercial kitchen, and track unit economics in real conditions—actual food costs, prep time, transaction speed, and waste. This costs a fraction of a food truck permit and commissary fees, lets you test 3–4 core items, and gives you hard data on customer price sensitivity and queue management before scaling to full-truck operations.

- **Use the pilot to lock down two metrics**: Calculate actual COGS per item and labor hours per 100 customers. These numbers drive all downstream decisions (pricing, truck size, staffing). If your Filipino-Mexican fusion isn't hitting 60–65% gross margin, something's broken before you finance a truck.

- **Launch a simple TikTok+Instagram schedule (90 days, 10 minutes daily)**: Post farmers market setup, finished dishes, and customer reactions. Food truck discovery is hyper-local and time-sensitive; capturing early organic reach matters. But keep the strategy ruthlessly simple—one device, 2–3 formats, measurable saves (bookings, repeat visits). If video drives zero referrals after 4 weeks of consistent posting, pivot to word-of-mouth and local partnerships instead of burning cycles on content.

- **Validate regulatory and logistical baseline (Week 1–2)**: Confirm commissary kitchen availability, walk through the permit process with the city health department, and identify your three safest vending zones. This costs nothing but prevents costly surprises later (e.g., discovering your preferred location isn't legal until permit rules change).

- **Establish pre-sales baseline before Day 1 of the truck**: Use the farmers market and any catering gigs to build a mailing list and phone contacts. Before you sign a lease or financing agreement, you should have committed customers ready to visit—not hypothetical demand, but real names and repeat orders.

## Q2: The three-year picture, told as a felt story

- **Tuesday routine (Year 3)**: You're running two trucks—one stationed downtown weekday lunch, one at weekend events. You handle morning admin/finances at a small office space (shared with another vendor, cost-split). One truck has a part-time operator you trained; the other runs with you still driving 3 days a week because the menu quality matters to you and you enjoy it. Orders are placed online the night before; you're no longer scrambling for inventory. Regulars know your schedule and show up. Catering brings 30% of revenue and you've hired a dedicated catering coordinator.

- **What regulars say**: "They actually remember my order. The lumpia tacos taste the same every time. They're genuinely Filipino-Mexican, not some watered-down fusion. And they care about the neighborhood—they sponsor the local food truck association's quarterly mixer."

- **What you'd tell someone starting out**: "Don't over-capitalize too early. Prove your unit economics with a single truck for 18 months before touching a second one. Build relationships with other food truck owners—they're your peers, not just competition. When the city council starts talking about regulations, show up. You have a voice. And keep a margin on every item; you'll sleep better, and you'll have cash for opportunities."

- **Conditions that made the growth feel joyful, not stressful**: (1) You brought in a part-time accountant at Month 8, so you weren't drowning in bookkeeping; (2) you negotiated a favorable lease on a backup commissary with another operator, halving overhead; (3) you said no to 40% of catering requests in Year 2 to protect quality and your own sanity—profitability matters more than scale for scale's sake; (4) you built a real friendship with your city liaison, so regulatory changes never blindsided you.

## Q3: The pop-up-to-flagship pipeline

- **Use the food truck as a 12–18 month proof-of-concept, not a permanent business**: Treat it like Palace's pop-ups—the goal is to test menu, location fit, and brand identity under real conditions. Track three signals: (1) Are the same 30–40 customers showing up weekly? (2) Is catering revenue growing, or plateauing? (3) Can you consistently hit 20%+ net margin? If yes to all three after 12–15 months, a brick-and-mortar location is justified.

- **Test neighborhoods with pop-up catering + event bookings first**: Before locking into a brick-and-mortar lease in one area, do 8–10 catering gigs and pop-up events across three different neighborhoods. This tells you where density, demographic fit, and repeat demand actually live—much cheaper than a failed brick-and-mortar guess.

- **The graduation signal**: You're ready for a brick-and-mortar when (1) you have a 12-month waitlist for catering events (not hypothetical, actual bookings); (2) a specific neighborhood shows consistent foot traffic and social media check-ins; (3) you've paid off or significantly reduced truck debt; and (4) you have $40–50K in liquid reserves for buildout and operating cushion. Rush this transition and you'll drain capital quickly.

- **Design the brick-and-mortar as a "commissary + retail hybrid" first**: Before opening a full sit-down restaurant, consider a 800–1200 sq ft space where you operate a counter-service window + prep kitchen + small catering staging area. Lower overhead than a full restaurant, faster to break even, and you can scale up if the demand signal justifies it. Keep the truck running for events and catering—that's still 30–40% of your revenue in Year 3.

- **Avoid the trap of "growth for growth's sake"**: The hygge factor—regulars knowing you, consistent quality, community presence—is what keeps a food business alive long-term. If the brick-and-mortar trade doesn't preserve that intimacy, it's not the right move, no matter how profitable the P&L looks.
