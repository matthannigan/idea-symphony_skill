# Unit Economics, Menu Design, and Pricing - The Constraint Flipper

## Q1: Food cost ratios, menu portfolio, and the 28-32% band

- **Specialty inputs as your moat, not your problem:** Those expensive ube extracts and calamansi imports are a constraint that's actually your competitive armor. Everyone else can source commodity ingredients; you can't be undercut on authentic Filipino-Mexican fusion because your cost structure inherently locks in distinctiveness. What if you leaned into that premium positioning rather than fighting it? Price at 34-36% food costs knowing you're selling an experience that mass-market competitors can't replicate cheaply.

- **Portfolio engineering through contribution tiers:** Instead of averaging to 28-32%, segment deliberately: low-margin signature drivers (the lumpia taco at $11 anchors trust), mid-tier anchors (adobo burrito at $14), and a high-margin accessory tier (ube horchata at $5.50 builds margin). Batch the items so daily revenue mix trends toward 30% naturally. The constraint of "specialty ingredients" becomes the excuse to charge more for complementary items without shocking the core order.

- **Simplification as strategic boldness:** What if you only made the three items you named and made them *exceptionally* well? Eliminate menu sprawl. Your food cost constraint forces focus, which reduces waste, training complexity, and decision fatigue. You'll beat the 28-32% target because you're running at near-factory efficiency on three recipes, not juggling six. That's not a limitation—that's becoming laser-focused.

## Q2: Cost floor per service day and break-even velocity under three capital scenarios

- **The daily nut is your design brief:** Map backward from your worst-case day: commissary $30, fuel $20, permits/insurance pro-rata $18, depreciation $25, wage $80 = roughly $173/day minimum. Now flip it: that $173 floor isn't a problem; it's a constraint that forces you to hit 15-20 covers/day at $12 AOV to survive. That clarity is liberating because you stop guessing. Every decision—location selection, equipment choice, staffing model—now passes a single gate: does it lower the daily nut or increase cover velocity?

- **SBA loan as optionality insurance, not aspiration:** The $100K scenario isn't "better"; it's "less urgent." With $60K bootstrapped, you hit desperate creativity in month two—suddenly commissary relationships matter, location scouting becomes ruthless, menu simplifies. The constraint forces discipline that $100K removes. What if you bootstrap to $70-75K operational ceiling, test the model, and take the SBA loan only if you've proven cover velocity? You'll have earned it instead of spent it upfront.

- **Ramp-at-50% as your longest runway:** Starting at 50% capacity for six months isn't failure—it's building buffer for mistakes. If you design for 50% capacity to break even, reaching 60-70% is gravy. Compare that to launching at full-throttle and collapsing when demand softens seasonally. The constraint of slow ramp actually extends your runway longest and reveals market truth earliest. By month four, you'll know what you're genuinely capable of.

## Q3: Contribution margin per channel and revenue ceiling per service day

- **Events as your margin engine, disguised as "side revenue":** Vending is high throughput, low margin per transaction; catering is lower volume but your time-to-revenue ratio is brutal (setup, breakdown, admin). Events sit in the middle—you're still moving the same truck, but you're selling $150+ entrée bundles, alcohol/premium drinks, and upsells that vending customers never reach. What if events aren't a side channel but your margin backbone, and daily vending is the brand-building traction vehicle?

- **Single-window ceiling as your constraint advantage:** Most trucks cap at $800-1000/day gross because one window can't physically serve faster. But that window constraint is *good*—it forces order curation and prevents discount spirals. Higher-value items per transaction (specialty combos at $16-18) outpace low-ticket volume. What if you designed the vending window to be deliberately slow, curated, and expensive rather than fast and cheap? Fewer transactions, higher margin, less cash-handling chaos.

- **Labor-hour economics reveal the real winner:** Vending locks you in for 8-10 hours. Events grab 3-4 hours of your time (assume 50% overhead allocation). Catering is 2-3 hours on-site. If events generate $400 contribution in 3 hours, that's $133/hour. Vending at $300 contribution across 10 hours is $30/hour. The constraint of limited time actually tells you what to do: events should absorb 40% of your calendar once volume reaches 2-3/month.

## Q4: Pricing as cultural communication, not just unit economics

- **$14 vs. $9 isn't economics; it's identity:** A $9 lumpia taco says "quick, casual, mass-market." A $14 version says "this chef knows the spice, sourced the real ingredients, respects the craft." Which founder are you? If you're the immigrant cooking your family's food, charging $9 tells customers you don't believe in it. What if your pricing is your integrity statement? Price at $14, serve the customers who *get it*, and stop competing on volume with chains that have no soul.

- **Fusion as permission to charge upmarket:** Pure Filipino or pure Mexican street tacos have ceiling prices ($4-6). But fusion gives you narrative permission: you're synthesizing cultures, educating palates, taking risk. A $14 lumpia taco is a "fusion innovation," not a "taco." Rebrand the entire menu around the fusion story. Every item becomes a collaboration, not a reduction. Your constraint (fusion complexity) becomes your price justification.

- **Loyalty pricing beats transaction pricing:** What if instead of optimizing per-transaction margins, you charged premium ($14-15) to build a repeat customer base that subsidizes your other growth channels? Catering clients remember restaurants they trust and love. Word-of-mouth from $14 customers in a mid-size city grows faster than from $9 customers everywhere. The constraint of fewer total transactions becomes your advantage in community building.

## Q5: What if break-even is the trap

- **Deliberately stay "unprofitable" in menu innovation:** Reserve 20% of prime time for tests—new items, seasonal specials, catering menu prototypes. These won't break even; they'll lose $20-50 per day. But they feed your optionality. The trucks that stay trapped in break-even are the ones running the identical menu five years in. What if you budgeted $100/week for creative loss intentionally, knowing it's the seed capital for the next phase?

- **Unprofitable relationships as growth investment:** Vendor relationships, city council dinners, food truck association events—these don't show revenue. But what if you classified them as operating expenses (not profit kills) and protected them? The constraint of "we can't be profitable while building relationships" is actually the thing that stops you from becoming transactional. Profitable solo operators stay solo. The ones that grow build community at a cost.

- **Capacity slack as hidden strength:** What if you engineered for 60% capacity on purpose, knowing that 70-80% covers rarely happen and burn out founders? That slack is your constraint that forces you to grow methodically. You're not grinding toward profitability; you're optimizing for sustainability and scaling readiness. When the second truck arrives, you'll be ready because you never let the first one consume you.

## Q6: $100K ceiling as a forcing function for lean excellence

- **Three-item menu as your permanent competitive edge:** Forced to choose, you build a lumpia taco, adobo burrito, and ube horchata that are *legendary*. You'll know every permutation, every supplier relationship, every micro-cost. Meanwhile, a truck with $200K capital and a sprawling menu is juggling waste, inconsistency, and decision paralysis. What if you never expanded the core three? You'd be the specialist, the one people visit specifically for those items, not the generalist trying to be everything.

- **Constraint-driven operations excellence:** A $100K budget forces you to negotiate hard with suppliers, optimize commissary usage, minimize waste, and pick one or two strategic locations rather than blanket the city. When you're forced to be lean, you build operational discipline that becomes your flywheel. The $200K competitor has slack and never develops that discipline. Your constraint is your training ground for ruthless efficiency.

- **Expand through excellence, not scale:** With a $100K ceiling, your path to the second truck isn't franchising a mediocre model—it's proving the first truck works so well that investors and lenders believe in replication. You're not solving "how do I run two trucks?" by overfunding one. You're solving "how do I run one truck *so well* that growing becomes inevitable?" The constraint forces you to build something worth replicating instead of something desperate to escape.
