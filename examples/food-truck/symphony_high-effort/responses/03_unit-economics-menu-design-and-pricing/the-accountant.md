# Unit Economics, Menu Design, and Pricing - The Accountant

## Question 1: Food cost ratios, menu portfolio, and the 28-32% band

- **The specialty ingredient reality**: Filipino imports (ube extract, calamansi, quality soy) will run 35-40% of base food cost, pushing blended food cost ratio to 32-36% unless menu-engineered deliberately. Lumpia tacos at $9 retail require ~$2.80-3.00 in ingredients (ube wrapper, filling, sauce) to stay inside the 28% band; adobo burritos at $12 can afford $3.60-4.00 in protein and components. At higher cost, you're constraining retail price below what fusion positioning allows.

- **Anchor-and-driver portfolio**: Horchata (high-margin, 18-22% food cost, supports $5-6 retail) offsets taco-margin pressure. Position ube horchata as a signature high-repeat purchase that funds lower-margin tacos as traffic drivers. This portfolio structure (66% contribution on drinks, 48% on tacos) blends to ~54% gross margin systemwide, and the difference between 28% and 54% is the operating leverage you need.

- **The simplification trade-off**: If specialty inputs structurally push above 32%, either accept 30-35% as your target (common in ethnic/fusion fast-casual), or engineer the menu down to 5-7 core items that achieve 28% through volume scale and supplier relationships. A 12-item menu amplifies variance in procurement; a 5-item menu (lumpia tacos, classic taco, burrito, horchata, dessert) locks cost structure and gives you pricing clarity.

## Question 2: Cost floor per service day and break-even velocity under three capital scenarios

- **Daily fixed cost floor (all scenarios)**: Commissary rental ~$25-40/day (assume $30), truck lease/depreciation $30-50/day (using $12K/year on a $60K asset = $33/day), fuel ~$15-20/day, permits and insurance pro-rata ~$12-15/day, owner labor floor (assume unpaid during ramp). Total: **~$90-130 fixed/day** before any variable labor or food. At $10 average order value and 28% food cost, you retain ~$7.20 per order, less $1.50 in variable labor (packaging, payment processing). Net contribution per order: ~$5.70. **Break-even threshold: 16-23 covers/day** depending on cost assumptions.

- **Bootstrapped $60K scenario**: Equipment financed over 36 months (~$1,800/year or $33/day already in the floor). No loan interest, lower insurance baseline. You need **22-25 covers/day** at $10 AOV to cover the $90 daily floor. At 5 service days/week, that's 110-125 covers/week, a realistic opening-month achievable target for a fusion truck in a 250K city with established food truck visibility.

- **SBA loan ($100K fully capitalized)**: Assumes $12K truck lease or purchase, $25K initial inventory/repairs, $40K working capital buffer, $23K remaining. Loan servicing ~$320/month ($10.50/day). **Daily floor rises to ~$105-140**. Break-even at **26-30 covers/day**. This capital cushion buys you 4-6 months of ramp time and room to invest in marketing/location testing; the loan is justified only if it enables reach 30+ covers/day faster, not if it funds a padded timeline.

- **Conservative ramp (50% capacity six months)**: Model covers at 15/day for months 1-3, scaling to 22/day by month 6. Cumulative loss: ~$6,000-8,000 over six months. This consumes the $60K bootstrap entirely and requires either SBA loan backup or a co-founder with income, or second revenue stream (catering events) kicking in by month 3.

## Question 3: Contribution margin per channel and revenue ceiling per service day

- **Daily vending ceiling and dynamics**: Single service window (assuming 11am-2pm + 5pm-8pm, two 3-hour windows = 6 contact hours/day) with one staff member can realistically push 40-50 covers/service in high-traffic locations, max 80-100/day across two windows. At $10 AOV and 57% contribution margin (after food and packaging), that's **$570-750 gross contribution/day**, or **$2,850-3,750/week**. This is your revenue ceiling in pure vending mode before hiring help (which drops contribution % by 15-18% per additional person).

- **Catering and events contribution**: Lower per-order throughput (20-30 orders prepped for a party), but higher order value ($180-300 per event) and predictable timing. One catering event at $250 gross order requires ~$120 in food and prep labor (vs. ~$4.30 in vending). Contribution per event: ~$130 (52% margin). Two events/week = **$260 additional contribution**, but this consumes 8-10 labor hours. On a vending day at 50 covers, you generate the same $285 contribution in 6 hours. **Vending is higher labor efficiency; catering is higher margin % but lower hourly return**.

- **Channel value to founder's time**: Vending at 50 covers/day, 6 service hours, yields ~$5.70 contribution/hour (gross). Catering at $250 per event + 5 prep/service hours yields ~$26/hour (but fewer events). Events are the higher-value channel *per hour*, but depend on irregular bookings. The math favors building vending to 40+ covers/day (sustainable, repeatable income), then layering in 2-3 catering events/week as upside. Combined: ~$700-850/day average contribution, **sufficient for single-operator profitability at low capital cost**.

## Question 4: Pricing as cultural communication, not just unit economics

- **Price as identity signal**: A $9 lumpia taco signals "accessible street food, fusion flavor at casual-dining reach" — this frames the founder as democratizing Filipino cuisine, welcoming to first-time explorers. A $14 taco signals "artisanal, small-batch, justified premium for authenticity and ingredient quality" — this frames the founder as guardian of tradition, built for repeat customers who value depth. The same taco, different price, speaks to different audiences and earns different loyalty.

- **The cost-to-price translation**: If the lumpia taco costs $2.80 to make, a $9 price (69% margin) announces "I can do this at volume, no waste." A $14 price (80% margin) announces "every ingredient is chosen with care, scarcity is part of the value." For Filipino fusion specifically, which occupies a cultural positioning that *could* go either way (accessible everyday food or celebrated craft), pricing crystallizes which direction you're claiming. The founder's identity — immigrant heritage, pride in Filipino culinary tradition vs. excitement about fusion innovation and access — should shape this choice, not just the math.

- **Trust and gatekeeping distinction**: Lower pricing ($9) builds trust through affordability and removes perceived gatekeeping (cuisine is "for everyone"). Higher pricing ($12-14) builds trust through perceived quality and dedication (cuisine is "worth paying for"). Neither is inherently superior; the choice depends on whether the goal is *scale and reach* (lower) or *community and depth* (higher). A pricing structure that honors the cuisine — whether accessible or premium — builds loyalty better than one driven purely by cost minimization.

## Question 5: What if break-even is the trap

- **The profitability lock**: Once you hit 25-30 covers/day, you've optimized the formula: peak-time locations, streamlined 5-item menu, one consistent staff routine, minimal experimentation. The $5,700/month contribution margin *feels* like security, but it's actually a cage — you cannot afford to close the truck for a week to scout catering markets, test new recipes, or rebuild relationships with suppliers. You're locked into the schedule that keeps the lights on, not the schedule that builds optionality.

- **Strategic unprofitability design**: What if months 4-6 operated at a *deliberate* loss (hiring a second person to explore catering, running pop-up events, testing a second truck location) while month 3 banked $8,000-10,000 to fund it? The question is not "what do we cut to stay profitable?" but "what are we not learning because we're locked into profitability?" A food truck that stays intentionally under-deployed on a 40-cover day to train a catering team, or that tests a second location by accepting 4 weeks of red ink, is making *different decisions* than one that optimizes toward the break-even line.

- **The strategic question reframed**: Which decisions get easier — or possible — if you're not optimizing purely for the number to go positive? (1) Relationship investment: catering outreach takes time and requires consistent presence at networking, not just vending days. (2) Supply-chain resilience: experimenting with new vendors or processing methods isn't profitable in month 1, but it's essential by year 3. (3) Team development: training a second owner/operator requires months of mentorship, not profit-per-person optimization. A business designed to stay strategically unprofitable in one corner gives you the option to build optionality in another.

## Question 6: $100K ceiling as a forcing function for lean excellence

- **The menu discipline enforced by constraint**: With $100K startup capital, you can afford: $12K truck (used food cart or minimal equipment), $20K inventory/repairs, $30K working capital, $20K contingency. This **cannot** fund a 15-item menu with multiple protein variations, sauce stations, or experimentation infrastructure. It forces you toward the two or three highest-margin, highest-velocity items: assume lumpia tacos (40% of orders, high repeat, $10 AOV, 69% margin) and ube horchata (35% of orders, high-repeat, $5 AOV, 78% margin), plus one secondary item (adobo burrito, 25% of orders, $12 AOV, 60% margin). This 3-item portfolio is not a compromise — it's clarity.

- **Lean excellence through scarcity**: Constraint *forces* procurement efficiency: you negotiate volume discounts on three ingredients vs. fifteen, you identify the single best calamansi supplier and build relationship depth, you reduce food waste through tight demand forecasting. A $100K truck with a 5-item menu has better unit economics and faster cash conversion than a $150K truck with 12 items. The constraint is not a limitation you apologize for; it's the design principle that makes the model work.

- **The scarcity advantage**: Competitors with more capital often have slower decision-making, higher overhead, and diffused focus. Your $100K ceiling forces you to obsess over the two or three items that matter, test variations ruthlessly (ube horchata: three different sweetness levels, measure repeat purchase rate), and build brand identity around *mastery of a narrow domain*, not breadth. "The best ube horchata and lumpia taco in the city" is a stronger market position than "pretty good Filipino-Mexican fusion across eight categories." The capital constraint is the best possible forcing function for finding and dominating your true differentiation.
