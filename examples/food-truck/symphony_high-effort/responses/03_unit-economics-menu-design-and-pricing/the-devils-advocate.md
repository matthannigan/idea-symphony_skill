# Unit Economics, Menu Design, and Pricing - The Devil's Advocate

## Question 1: Food cost ratios, menu portfolio, and the 28-32% band

- **The premium sourcing paradox**: Sourcing from small immigrant-owned suppliers for authentic ube extract and calamansi is ethically appealing, but it guarantees you'll never hit 28-32% food cost on those items. Have we considered whether "premium fusion" positioned at $14/taco can actually sustain 40%+ food costs, or does the positioning require either volume at scale or accepting 15-18% margins instead of the fast-casual standard? A mitigation: clearly segment—use premium Filipino sourcing on hero items (adobo burrito), commodity spices on volume items (lumpia).

- **Menu portfolio trap**: The plan assumes you can mix high-margin tacos with low-margin signature beverages (horchata) to hit 28-32% blended. But what if customers only buy horchata in warm months, and winter forces you into a narrow taco-only portfolio that can't cross-subsidize? You might address this by pre-testing seasonal buy patterns with pop-ups or event catering before launch, ensuring the portfolio actually balances across all seasons.

- **Specialty input volatility you're not modeling**: Ube extract, calamansi, and Filipino spices face import supply shocks and seasonal price swings that commodity tomatoes and cheese don't. Have we costed in a 15-20% buffer for ingredient price volatility, or are we assuming perfect supply-chain stability? Mitigation: identify 1-2 high-margin anchor items using commodity inputs, and allow specialty items to float in price rather than trying to lock them at cost-plus percentages.

- **The simplified menu as strength**: Instead of engineering a complex portfolio to hit 28-32%, what if you deliberately constrain to 5-7 core items? This forces you to master execution, reduces waste, and makes costing transparent. The trap isn't the constraint—it's trying to serve 15 items and justify food cost overruns as "authenticity tax." Lean into the limits rather than fighting them.

- **Pricing signals authenticity or greed**: If lumpia tacos retail for $14 but the food cost is 40%, are you capturing fair value for your labor and ingredient quality, or are you just inflating price to cover inefficiency? Have we stress-tested whether $14 is defensible because customers perceive genuine distinctiveness, or because you're nervous about hitting lower price points? Mitigation: run a three-price test ($9, $12, $14) with event catering or pop-ups to see where demand and margin actually sit before committing the truck to a price.

## Question 2: Cost floor per service day and break-even velocity under three capital scenarios

- **The "fully-loaded daily cost" is probably higher than modeled**: Commissary rental ($200-400/day), fuel ($40-80/day), truck lease or depreciation ($50-100/day), permits and insurance ($20-30/day), and labor (at minimum, your time plus part-time help $60-150/day) sum to $370-760/day in fixed overhead before you ring a register. Have we pressure-tested this number with actual vendor quotes, or are we using industry benchmarks that may not hold in a mid-size city with stricter regulations and higher commissary costs? Mitigation: spend a month getting real quotes from your local commissary, insurance brokers, and fuel vendors before finalizing the break-even model.

- **The SBA loan scenario may lock you into unsustainable throughput**: With $100K capital, you can afford higher daily losses in early months. But the SBA loan carries debt service—roughly $800-1000/month in repayment. That means break-even isn't the day your revenue equals daily costs; it's when monthly revenue exceeds daily costs plus debt service. Are you modeling $24-30K monthly revenue needed just to service the loan, independent of owner draw? Mitigation: stress-test the loan scenario by simulating months 1-6 at 50% capacity and see when cash flow turns positive, not just when daily P&L breaks even.

- **The 50% ramp assumption is optimistic**: Six months at 50% capacity assumes a smooth, predictable growth curve. But reality is lumpy—you might hit 30% capacity for three months, then 70% suddenly, then drop to 40%. Have we built a Monte Carlo model with seasonal variance, regulatory surprise (new permit fees), and customer acquisition delays? Mitigation: model three scenarios: pessimistic (stay at 40% for 12 months), base case (ramp to 80% by month 8), and optimistic (hit 90%+ by month 4), then identify which one forces you to pivot or take additional capital.

- **"Covers per day" is a vanity metric without checking AOV reality**: If you need 80 covers/day at $12 AOV to break even, but customer data shows Filipino-fusion buyers average $9.50 AOV because they're price-conscious students and service workers, you actually need 100+ covers/day. Have we validated the target AOV with actual market research or pilot sales, or are we working backward from an assumed number? Mitigation: run a 4-week pop-up or event catering blitz, track every transaction, and lock in actual AOV before finalizing the daily break-even math.

- **Bootstrap scenario is actually the stress test**: $60K capital forces a slower ramp and lower burn. But it also gives you more time to fail silently before running out of runway. Have we modeled what happens if month 4 shows that daily revenue maxes out at 60 covers/day—do you have 8+ more months of burn runway, or are you insolvent by month 7? Mitigation: for the bootstrap scenario, explicitly model the "pivot or capital injection" decision point (month 5 or 6) and identify what metrics would trigger a shift to catering-only, part-time operation, or loan pursuit.

## Question 3: Contribution margin per channel and revenue ceiling per service day

- **Single-window revenue ceiling is a hard constraint you're not optimizing for**: A two-window food truck can physically serve ~1.5 orders per minute in peak. At $12 AOV, that's $1,080/hour, $7,560 for a 7-hour service day—but only if you hit 100% window utilization and have zero downtime. Have we observed actual food trucks hitting that sustained volume, or are we modeling a fantasy? Mitigation: spend a Saturday observing a successful food truck in your city, counting transactions per hour, and use that actual ceiling rather than a theoretical maximum.

- **Catering looks high-margin but consumes all flexibility**: Event catering (say, $800/event at 35% margin = $280 contribution) seems attractive, but each event requires menu engineering, setup, transport, and vendor coordination that ate 4-6 hours of your time. Contribution margin per labor-hour for catering might be lower than a straightforward high-volume vending day, especially once you factor in prep and cleanup. Have we actually tracked the true time cost of catering jobs, or are we assuming a lower time allocation than reality? Mitigation: log your time on the next 5 catering gigs and calculate labor-adjusted margin per channel, not just raw contribution margin.

- **"High-volume vending day" may be seasonal myth**: You're planning a food truck in a mid-size city with new ordinance restrictions on downtown hours. Best-case "high-volume" days (Saturday lunch, festival vending) might happen 12-16 days/month, not reliably. On off-season Tuesdays or post-ordinance enforcement, you might drop to 30 covers/day. Have we mapped out realistic vending capacity by season and day-of-week, or are we planning on 60+ covers every day? Mitigation: get a seasonal heatmap from existing fusion or food trucks—ask them directly about Tuesday vs. Saturday throughput, summer vs. winter variability.

- **Events and catering are unpredictable revenue, not baseload**: If catering provides 15-20% of gross revenue but only happens sporadically, you can't count on it to cover fixed costs. Daily vending is your reliable baseload. Have we sized fixed costs (truck, permits, commissary) assuming catering-free months so you're not forced to turn down low-margin vending opportunities to "make up" catering revenue shortfalls? Mitigation: design the business to survive entirely on daily vending at 60% capacity (the realistic floor), and treat catering as upside bonus that improves monthly margin rather than essential revenue.

- **Labor-hour calculation includes your own time at zero cost, hiding true economics**: If you're tracking "founder labor" as free because you own the business, you're not seeing the real economic value generated per channel. Have we assigned an imputed labor cost (say, $20-25/hour for your skilled time) to each channel to understand true contribution? Vending might generate $150/hour net, while catering only $60/hour once fully burdened. Mitigation: run the three-channel margin analysis twice—once with your labor at $0, once at $25/hour—and see which channel is genuinely economic versus emotionally appealing.

## Question 4: Pricing as cultural communication, not just unit economics

- **$14 lumpia taco signals "trust me" in a market you haven't earned yet**: At $14, you're claiming the lumpia is worth 50% more than a standard taco truck's $9-10 offering. Customers in a mid-size city unfamiliar with Filipino-Mexican fusion might interpret $14 as "premium" or "pretentious," not "authentic." Have we validated that your intended audience (food enthusiasts, fusion fans, or diaspora community) is large enough to sustain $14 pricing, or are you asking the broader market to pay a premium for unfamiliar cuisine? Mitigation: launch at $11-12, establish customer trust and repeat purchase, then test a small premium tier at $14 for adventurous customers while keeping core items accessible.

- **Pricing as identity can feel like cultural gatekeeping**: If you price lumpia tacos at $14 to "signal distinctiveness," you might be unintentionally excluding service workers, students, and immigrant families who might be your most enthusiastic cultural audience. Have we considered whether premium pricing actually aligns with your stated intention of celebrating Filipino-Mexican fusion for the communities those cuisines come from? Mitigation: design a tiered offering—signature items at $12 (accessible), plus a limited "chef's special" fusion exploration at $14-15 (premium positioning) so both audiences feel welcome.

- **Low price ($9 lumpia) might build market share faster than margin per transaction**: If a $9 lumpia captures 80 customers/day versus 60 at $14, and you can still hit 32% food cost, the $9 option generates more total margin, brand recognition, and repeat customers. Have we modeled revenue over time (12 months) rather than optimizing single-transaction margin, or are we chasing per-unit profit at the cost of volume growth? Mitigation: simulate 12-month revenue curves at three price points ($9, $11.50, $14), including customer acquisition and repeat purchase rates, to see which pricing floor maximizes cumulative margin, not just per-transaction profit.

- **Authenticity pricing can backfire if the food isn't perceived as authentic**: If customers buy a $14 lumpia taco and find mass-market ube extract and canned adobo flavor (due to supply constraints), they'll feel ripped off. Have we committed to a sourcing standard that justifies premium pricing, or are we hoping to charge premium prices while cutting corners operationally? Mitigation: identify 2-3 non-negotiable authenticity markers (house-made adobo sauce, fresh calamansi juice, small-supplier ube), lock in supplier relationships before setting final pricing, and let sourcing costs inform your minimum viable price.

- **Fusion price signaling is fragile without narrative**: Price alone doesn't communicate "Filipino-Mexican fusion." Customers need to understand what makes this different—the story matters as much as the price. Have we built a coherent narrative (founder's family heritage, culinary research, market positioning) that justifies the price, or are we hoping the menu description alone carries the load? Mitigation: invest in 2-3 clear storytelling touchpoints (truck design, menu copy, social media origin story, personal introduction at high-touch events) to anchor the premium price in customer perception.

## Question 5: What if break-even is the trap

- **Reaching break-even often signals the end of experimentation**: Once the truck is cash-flow neutral, you're tempted to lock in the formula that got you there. But that formula is often based on early customer quirks, favorable conditions, or blind luck—not real strategy. Have we considered that the menu, location strategy, and pricing that produce break-even month 6 might be completely wrong for sustainable growth month 12+? Mitigation: deliberately build a "learning budget" into year 1—reserve 10-15% of margin for menu testing, location experimentation, and pricing pilots, even after break-even, so you don't calcify around an accidental successful formula.

- **The "strategic unprofitability" reframe is useful**: What if you designed one P&L line item to run at a loss on purpose—say, ube horchata at cost or below—specifically to drive traffic, build loyalty, or test market segments? This forces you to make the trade-off conscious rather than stumbling into inefficiency. Have we identified any loss-leader or investment strategy that makes strategic sense, or are we treating every menu item as a profit center? Mitigation: identify one signature item to position as a traffic driver (even if it's 2-3% below target margin), and explicitly budget for that as customer acquisition cost rather than accepting it as accidental inefficiency.

- **Optimization for break-even is actually optimization for stagnation**: If your goal is break-even by month 6, you'll naturally make conservative choices—tight menu, single location, minimal labor. By month 8, you'll be trapped: the formula works, but there's no margin to expand, test multi-truck logistics, or pivot. Have we considered building a different goal structure—say, "reach $40K monthly profit by month 12" instead of "break even by month 6"—that forces growth decisions even when break-even is reached? Mitigation: set a second-phase goal (profitability, expansion readiness, or market footprint) that kicks in once break-even is confirmed, so you don't default to complacency.

- **Break-even ignores capital allocation**: You've invested $60K or $100K upfront. Break-even means P&L is neutral, but your capital is still trapped in the truck asset, not generating a return. Have we modeled the payback period (months until cumulative profit exceeds initial capital) and return on invested capital, rather than celebrating monthly P&L break-even as success? Mitigation: track both break-even (P&L) and payback (capital recovery) metrics separately; only declare the business viable once payback is on track for 18-24 months.

- **The question is really: what decisions change if you plan for sustainable profitability instead of break-even?** You might choose higher starting prices, narrower menu, lower burn rate, or more aggressive catering emphasis if you're optimizing for profit rather than break-even. Have we run a business model under two hypotheses—one optimized for speed-to-breakeven, one for sustainable profitability—and compared the strategic decisions? Mitigation: model both scenarios side-by-side; identify which one builds a business you'd want to operate for 5+ years versus a vehicle to hit one milestone.

## Question 6: $100K ceiling as a forcing function for lean excellence

- **The cap is a feature, not a constraint—if used intentionally**: A $100K budget absolutely forces you to cut scope: single-truck operation, 6-8 core menu items, minimal front-of-house labor in early months. But most founders treat the budget cap as "everything else I'd like to do, but can't afford," rather than "design the business to thrive within this envelope." Have we actually reverse-engineered menu and operating model from the $100K constraint, or are we building the "ideal" business and apologizing for needing to downscale it? Mitigation: start with the constraint, not the vision—work backward from $100K to identify what operation actually optimizes for margin and repeatability under that ceiling, rather than starting with a 20-item menu and cutting it down.

- **Lean forced by budget sometimes means "leaner than viable"**: A $100K cap might force you to cut corners on food safety equipment, permit compliance, or kitchen infrastructure that end up costing you more in rework or regulatory fines. Have we distinguished between strategic lean (fewer menu items, longer operating hours, shared commissary) and dangerous lean (used equipment, expired permits, unsanitary workarounds)? Mitigation: build a non-negotiable compliance and safety budget (permits, licenses, inspections, baseline equipment) and constrain menu scope and labor instead, never safety.

- **"Two or three highest-margin items" might be too narrow for a food business**: A truck selling only adobo burrito and lumpia taco has no insurance against that demographic's seasonal shopping patterns, no hedge against ingredient supply shock, no variety to attract repeat customers. Have we modeled customer churn if the menu is too tight, or are we assuming 30+ repeat customers/day will happily order the same two items every week? Mitigation: identify 5-6 core items where 2-3 are high-margin anchors and the rest provide variety and upsell (sides, beverages, seasonal specials), then ruthlessly cut anything outside that set.

- **The constraint can hide bad unit economics**: If you cap the menu to 6 items within $100K, and one item turns out to have 40% food cost (vs. the 28% target), the tight menu prevents you from engineering a portfolio fix. You just live with the margin bleed. Have we stress-tested the 2-3 anchor items for ingredient stability, supplier reliability, and realistic yield before committing capital, or are we assuming they'll hit target margins once you hit scale? Mitigation: source the ingredients for your top 3 items, cook 10-20 trial batches, and lock in actual (not theoretical) food costs before finalizing the business model.

- **Lean excellence works best with relentless focus on one variable: repeat customers**: The real forcing function isn't the budget—it's the question "What would make someone come back?" With limited menu variety, you can't rely on novelty. You have to nail execution, build relationship, and create loyalty through authenticity or personal connection. Have we centered the business on repeat-customer acquisition and retention, or are we still thinking transactionally about daily covers? Mitigation: design a loyalty or pre-order mechanism (text/Instagram, loyalty punch card, weekly special) that turns daily customers into weekly or bi-weekly regulars, so your revenue becomes more predictable and the tight menu actually feels curated rather than constrained.
