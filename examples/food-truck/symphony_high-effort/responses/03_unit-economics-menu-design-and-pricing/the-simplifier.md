# Unit Economics, Menu Design, and Pricing - The Simplifier

## Question 1: Food cost ratios, menu portfolio, and the 28–32% band

- **Start with the anchor, cut the rest.** Stop optimizing a 10-item menu to hit 28% food cost. Ask: what if you only sold lumpia tacos? Calculate that single item's true cost including waste and ingredient volatility. Once you know what naturally margins well, build *from* that, not *around* it.

- **Specialty inputs are overhead you chose.** Calamansi and ube extract aren't inherently expensive—they feel expensive because you're importing small quantities for a low-volume truck. What if you found suppliers selling larger units, or swapped one specialty item for a more fungible substitute that still tastes authentic? The food cost problem might be a sourcing problem, not a cuisine problem.

- **The 28% target is inherited wisdom.** Reject it if it doesn't fit. You're not a fast-casual chain. What if your food costs are 35%—but your labor is half because you work the truck solo? Blended margin is what matters, not compliance with category benchmarks.

- **Portfolio complicates everything.** A three-item menu (lumpia tacos, one burrito variant, horchata) is easier to cost, replenish, and execute. What if the high-margin items subsidize one signature loss-leader—the adobo burrito at near-cost as pure traffic—and you call that strategy intentional instead of broken?

## Question 2: Cost floor per service day and break-even velocity under three capital scenarios

- **Cut every cost you're absorbing out of habit.** Before modeling three scenarios, audit the daily cost floor: Is the $1,500/month commissary kitchen actually necessary, or are you using 20% of it? Can you start with a shared prep kitchen one day per week? Break-even velocity often drops when you shrink the denominator first.

- **Bootstrap scenario is the cleanest.** At $60K, you hit break-even when fixed costs are minimized. Single founder, shared commissary, used equipment, no fancy POS system—just a till. What if the $100K scenario is the trap, because it locks you into needing higher volume to justify the spending?

- **Covers per day is the real metric.** Forget average order value arithmetic for a moment. If you need 80 covers/day at $15 average to break even, and you're currently hitting 50 covers/day, the problem isn't pricing—it's location or marketing. Don't solve it by raising the AOV; solve it by moving the truck.

- **The six-month ramp is realistic.** Yes to the conservative scenario. If you plan for 50% capacity for six months, you're not surprised when November and January are slow. And when you *do* hit 70% capacity in month seven, it feels like progress, not accident.

## Question 3: Contribution margin per channel and revenue ceiling per service day

- **Kill the illusion of diversification.** You *want* catering and events to work. But if they consume 30% of operational complexity for 15% of revenue, they're not revenue streams—they're distractions. What if you eliminated events entirely in year one and doubled down on daily vending + one standing catering contract?

- **Single-window ceiling is the answer.** There's a hard revenue cap on a two-person food truck window: roughly 15 minutes per order, maximum 40 covers per hour, 4-hour service window = 160 covers. At $12 average, that's $1,920/day before labor. What if you stopped trying to beat that and instead accepted it as your revenue ceiling and optimized around it?

- **Founder time is the scarcest resource.** Catering might have better per-hour margin, but it requires proposal writing, client coordination, equipment setup, and vendor politics. Daily vending is mindless by comparison. What if you measured each channel by founder labor-hours consumed, not gross dollars, and invested only in the channels that give you your time back?

- **Events are seasonality's trap.** Summer events; winter silence. What if you committed to 70% vending revenue + 30% catering and refused to let events crowd the calendar? The steady revenue underwrites the truck; the catering is upside.

## Question 4: Pricing as cultural communication, not just unit economics

- **$14 lumpia taco is a statement of respect for the cuisine.** It says "this ingredient was sourced carefully, this flavor profile took time to develop, you're not eating commodity food." $9 says "competitive." One builds loyalty; the other builds traffic. What if you priced everything at the higher tier and trusted your audience to value it?

- **Price for your real customer, not the imaginary one.** Your founder identity—Filipino-American, culinary background, fusion vision—attracts a specific customer. They're not price-shopping lumpia tacos; they're looking for something they can't get elsewhere. What if your pricing reflected that distinctiveness, and you stopped worrying about the customer who compares you to a dollar menu?

- **Simplify the price structure.** What if there were only two price tiers: small ($10) and large ($14), applied universally? No menu-item-by-item nickel-and-diming. It's easier to explain, harder to second-guess, cleaner to execute.

- **Anchor with your signature item.** Lumpia tacos are the identity. Price them where they need to be ($14+) to work economically. Build the rest of the menu *down* from there—the burrito at $13, the horchata at $5. Customers accept that model; they reject arbitrary per-item variation.

## Question 5: What if break-even is the trap

- **Break-even locks you into the present.** You reach $4K/month costs and $4K/month revenue, the truck pays for itself, and suddenly you can't afford to experiment with new items, new hours, or new locations. You're stuck *optimizing* the formula that broke even, not *evolving* it.

- **What if you designed for controlled unprofitability?** Year one: intentionally underprice one item (adobo burrito at $11 vs. $13 true cost) to build customer base and loyalty. Accept that trade-off. The revenue from loyalty acquisitions elsewhere pays for it. Once the customer base is sticky, you raise the price.

- **The interesting options stay open only if you stay flexible.** What if you committed to running at 85% capacity for year one—enough to stay afloat, not enough to require rigid execution? That buffer lets you try a new location, test a catering angle, negotiate with suppliers, or build community without the truck collapsing.

- **Re-define success away from the number.** Break-even is boring. What if success was "enough revenue to keep the truck rolling + enough margin to hire help by month 14"? That's a different optimization trajectory, and it protects your agency.

## Question 6: $100K ceiling as a forcing function for lean excellence

- **This constraint is your greatest asset.** Most food truck failures come from over-capitalization—too much equipment, too many menu items, too much pride in having "done it right." You can't afford that failure mode. Your constraint forces ruthlessness.

- **Two items, not three.** Lumpia tacos and adobo burritos. Both are distinctive, both margin well, both easy to execute solo. The horchata is the add-on, not a hero item. What if you killed the third item entirely and used that equipment budget for supplier relationships instead?

- **Used equipment, not new.** A used flat griddle and a used fryer run the entire menu. Thousands, not tens of thousands. That freed capital buys you six months of runway or a better commissary location. New equipment doesn't make you faster; efficiency does.

- **Delay the scale investments.** No POS system, no driver tracking, no catering-grade serving supplies. That stuff comes in year two when cash flow supports it. Year one is about *learning*, not *scaling*. What if you accepted that and stopped treating a $60K startup like a $200K operation?

- **The constraint teaches discipline.** Every dollar must serve the core mission: sell distinctive Filipino-Mexican fusion food profitably. Anything that doesn't is cut. What if you took that same discipline into year two, when you actually *have* capital to waste? You'd still say no to most of it.
