# Unit Economics, Menu Design, and Pricing - The First Principles Thinker

## Q1: Food cost ratios, menu portfolio, and the 28-32% band

- **The 28-32% band is borrowed from fast-casual, not first principles.** Start by asking: what does *your* customer actually need? With specialty ingredients from immigrant suppliers, your cost structure may inherently be higher. The question isn't "how do we force ourselves into the band?" but "what margin does your *actual* cost floor require, and what price reflects that honestly?" If ube extract and calamansi cost more, charge more—but only if you're transparent about why. Signature items like adobo burritos can anchor at 35-38% food cost if the narrative justifies it.

- **Deconstruct the portfolio into two tiers: anchors and explorers.** The fundamental truth: you need 1-2 items that run at 25-28% food cost (the reliable margin factory), and you can afford 2-3 items at 40%+ if they drive traffic and brand identity. Lumpia tacos might be your 26% workhorse; ube horchata might live at 42% but create the story. Stop trying to make everything fit the band.

- **Specialty ingredients are not a cost problem—they're a differentiation asset.** The structural question isn't "how do we source cheaper calamansi?" but "does every menu item need calamansi?" Simplify to 3 core items that rely on your imported inputs, then add 1-2 simple, high-margin fillers (rice bowls, drinks, sides) that use standard proteins. This portfolio mix is how you stay profitable with your ingredient identity intact.

## Q2: Cost floor per service day and break-even velocity under three capital scenarios

- **Start with a true, fully-loaded daily cost floor—not what you hope it is.** Commission kitchen: ~$20-30/day. Truck: $40-50/day (fuel + maintenance + depreciation, whether leased or financed). Permits, insurance, labor: $60-80/day for a solo founder. That's $120-160/day in fixed costs before you sell a single item. At 20% net margin (contribution margin after COGS), you need $600-800 in daily revenue to break even. That means ~40 orders at $15-20 average check. This is the baseline.

- **The SBA loan ($40K) doesn't change break-even velocity—it just delays it.** With $100K total capitalization, your truck is paid off faster, but your daily cost floor is identical. What changes is runway: you can absorb 60-90 days of lower volume before capital runs out. At bootstrap ($60K), you have ~40 days. The real first principle: revenue velocity matters more than capital. A $60K truck that hits 40 orders/day reaches stability faster than a $100K truck that averages 25.

- **The 50% capacity ramp is the pragmatic scenario.** Don't assume you hit 40 orders/day immediately. More realistically: month 1-2 at 15-20 orders, month 3-6 at 25-35 orders, month 6+ at 40+. Your true break-even on the $60K bootstrap path is 8-10 months, not 3. Plan for it.

## Q3: Contribution margin per channel and revenue ceiling per service day

- **Vending has a hard window ceiling; catering has a soft ceiling.** At a single service window, you're physically limited to maybe 50-60 orders in a 6-hour shift—that's $750-1200 in revenue before COGS. Catering removes the window constraint: one 75-person event might be $1500-2000 in revenue with lower per-unit labor. The fundamental difference is labor leverage. Vending trades your time 1:1 with revenue; catering trades your prep time against revenue from 50+ covers.

- **Contribution margin per labor-hour reveals the real value.** Vending: $30-50 per hour (revenue minus COGS, divided by hours worked). Catering: $40-80 per hour because you batch the labor (prep once, deliver to one location, minimal service time). Events fall between: $35-70 depending on setup complexity. The insight: *catering is not secondary revenue—it's your highest-margin channel per hour of founder time.* If you're trying to maximize personal income, catering should be 40-50% of your schedule by month 4.

- **The vending revenue ceiling is a feature, not a bug.** You can't scale vending linearly without hiring labor. But that labor hire is where you hit the brutal margin cliff: you need a second person at $15-18/hour, which cuts your net margin from 20% to 5-8% on vending revenue. This is why multi-truck operators eventually go brick-and-mortar: vending stops being the growth lever. Recognizing this early shapes whether you pursue a fleet or a kitchen.

## Q4: Pricing as cultural communication, not just unit economics

- **A $9 lumpia taco says "commodity"—you're competing on volume and speed.** A $14 taco says "craft"—the narrative is about sourcing, technique, and story. At $9, you're racing to the bottom with 30 other trucks; the founder's identity and Filipino-Mexican distinctiveness disappear. At $14, you're saying "I'm curating an experience." The first principle: *pricing is a filter*. It selects for the right audience and repels the bargain hunters who'll never be loyal anyway. Higher price with your authentic story builds brand; lower price to compete on volume guarantees you'll always be grinding.

- **Immigrant-owned ingredients are not a cost excuse—they're a premium positioning.** You source from small Filipino suppliers because the quality is better *and* because supporting immigrant businesses is part of your identity. Price like you mean it. Transparent menu notes ("ube horchata made with fresh extract from our partner supplier in Quezon City") turn ingredient sourcing into brand narrative. Customers at $14+ price points *want* to know where their food comes from.

- **Test your pricing narrative with three tiers.** Offer a $10 "quick lunch" option (simple, fast, standard margins). Offer a $14 "signature bowl" that tells the story. Offer a $18 "family bundle" for groups. Watch which one becomes the repeat purchase. Your real market willingness-to-pay emerges in the data, not in theory. But go in with confidence—not apology—that the higher prices reflect genuine distinctiveness.

## Q5: What if break-even is the trap

- **The trap is real: once you hit break-even, you're locked into defending the formula that got you there.** You're serving the same 40-50 customers/day, in the same location, with the same menu, because disrupting any variable might drop you below that magic number. The first principle: *sustainable profitability often requires staying temporarily unprofitable in one dimension.* What if you allocate one day per week to experimentation—testing new menu items, new locations, catering outreach—knowing it will reduce that day's revenue by 20-30%? You're trading short-term profitability for optionality.

- **Design the business with a built-in loss leader.** Reserve 10% of gross revenue for innovation: new dishes, location scouting, event catering development, community partnerships. This is not waste—it's the investment in preventing lock-in. The founders who thrive are the ones who can afford to be unprofitable in one corner while staying profitable overall. At $700/day break-even revenue, can you reserve $70-100 for strategic unprofitability? If not, you're fragile.

- **The deeper insight: break-even is the inflection where fear takes over.** Before break-even, you're still experimenting and iterating. After break-even, you're managing risk. The best time to pivot, to test a new market, or to invest in crew training is *before* you're dependent on the cash flow. Document this: what experiments will you run in months 3-5 (pre-break-even) that you'll be afraid to run in month 10 (post-break-even)? Build those into your plan now.

## Q6: $100K ceiling as a forcing function for lean excellence

- **Capital scarcity is not a constraint—it's a clarity tool.** With $100K, you cannot afford a 12-item menu, part-time experimentation, or premium equipment. This is perfect. The forcing function is: which 3 dishes represent the most defensible economic and cultural point? Not "what would be nice to offer," but "what is the irreducible core of Filipino-Mexican fusion that I can execute better than anyone in this market?" For this founder, that might be: adobo burritos, lumpia tacos, and ube horchata. Everything else is secondary.

- **Use capital scarcity to identify obsession.** Cheap trucks force you to master a simple menu because you have no room for error. You'll spend 6 months perfecting adobo sourcing and burrito assembly instead of chasing 15 mediocre items. This is how you build moat: genuine excellence in 3 things is harder to replicate than adequacy in 12. The $60K bootstrap founder who nails three dishes will eventually outcompete the $200K founder with a sprawling menu and diffused focus.

- **The ceiling forces efficiency in labor and systems.** You can't afford to hire until you've documented every step of your workflow. You can't afford equipment duplication. You can't afford waste in ingredient ordering. This discipline—born from necessity—is usually worth $50K+ in avoided mistakes for founders with bigger budgets. Reframe the $100K ceiling: it's not a handicap, it's an accelerant toward the lean excellence that scales. When you do expand, you'll scale a machine you've already perfected.
