# Unit Economics, Menu Design, and Pricing - The Pragmatist

## Q1: Food cost ratios, menu portfolio, and the 28-32% band

- **The 28-32% target is real, but specialty fusion is the exception.** Ube extract and calamansi from immigrant suppliers will likely hit 35-40% on signature items. A practical approach: accept premium pricing ($14-16) for signature pieces and build 2-3 higher-margin fillers (simple rice plates, beverages) to balance the blended ratio back to 32%. This avoids menu oversimplification.

- **Conduct a three-week ingredient cost audit before launch.** Map lumpia, adobo burrito, and horchata COGS with actual supplier quotes, accounting for small-batch minimums and spoilage. You'll discover which items you can dial down (e.g., using standard lime instead of bottled calamansi for prep, reserving premium ingredients for plating).

- **Portfolio mix trumps individual item margin.** If lumpia tacos drive traffic at $13 and 40% food cost, but horchata pulls 25% cost at $6, the blended margin covers operational overhead. The key: know your attachment rates (what else does someone buy when they order the lumpia?).

- **Price testing in soft launch avoids guessing.** Before official launch, run 2-3 weeks of pop-up events at farmers markets or community events with variable pricing ($12 vs. $14 lumpia). You'll get real demand elasticity data for your market and audience, plus validation that the price-to-value story resonates.

- **Simplification is not failure—it's positioning.** If you cannot hit 32% with all three core items, deliberately position as "premium fusion" at 35-38% cost and price accordingly ($15+ lumpia). This is clearer than struggling to cut costs and eroding quality. Make the premium story explicit in your brand narrative.

## Q2: Cost floor per service day and break-even velocity under three capital scenarios

- **Bootstrap scenario ($60K): Cost floor is approximately $280-320/day.** Commissary ($150-200/week), fuel ($40/day), truck depreciation/insurance/permits (~$100/day), labor (assume you work solo initially, so implicit). To break even at 40 covers/day, AOV needs to hit $8-10 per cover—too thin. Realistic break-even is 60-80 covers/day at $9-11 AOV, which requires high-traffic location and proven menu-market fit.

- **SBA-funded scenario ($100K total): Cost floor rises to $320-360/day.** You can hire 1 part-time prep/service person ($200-250/day), upgrade commissary arrangement, and absorb slightly higher truck maintenance. Break-even drops to 50-60 covers/day at $10 AOV. The loan repayment (~$400/month) comes out of net, so actual cash-positive is 70+ covers/day. More realistic and sustainable.

- **Conservative ramp (50% capacity for 6 months): Plan for $200/day operational cost and $60/day debt service.** Run 30-40 covers/day for the first 6 months at full staffing and marketing intensity. You'll be underwater but building reputation, location relationships, and menu-market fit. Use this runway to test and refine before pushing volume.

- **Create a live dashboard of daily actuals.** Track covers, AOV, food cost, and labor cost weekly. By week 4, you'll see whether 60 covers/day is achievable in your location. If not, adjust menu pricing, location, or hours rather than plodding toward a break-even that won't materialize.

- **Build a 12-month cash reserve into your scenario.** Even in the SBA case, you need 4-6 months of runway after breaking even operationally to handle seasonal dips, equipment failure, or permitting delays. Bootstrap at $60K likely needs extended family support or part-time external income for the first 8-12 months.

## Q3: Contribution margin per channel and revenue ceiling per service day

- **Daily vending is your margin engine but the absolute revenue ceiling.** A single service window, even at optimal location and throughput, realistically caps at 100-120 covers/day (8-hour service window = 4-5 min per order including payment). At $11 AOV, that's $1,100-1,320/day gross. Minus $320 cost floor = $780-1,000 contribution. Your time is fully consumed; you cannot scale without a second person.

- **Catering and events are margin killers if not priced explicitly.** Same food cost, but lower throughput per labor hour (event setup, breakdown, on-site service). However, 2-3 events/month at $800-1,200 each ($400-600 net profit) are valuable because they diversify revenue and fill mid-week dead zones. Do not underprice events; they are premium positioning, not volume play.

- **The brutal truth: daily vending generates ~$2,000-2,400 per week net (after all costs) at 60-70 covers/day.** After your draw ($500/week minimum), that's $1,500-1,900 for operational reserve and growth. Catering adds $1,200-1,800/month with less time investment. For meaningful scale, you need catering + events to hit $4,000+/week gross.

- **Your time is the scarcest resource.** Quantify it: daily vending is 50 hours/week; two events/month = 8 additional hours; menu prep/procurement = 5 hours/week. That's 63 hours, and you're not profitable yet. Catering and events don't scale without a second person, so prioritize finding a reliable co-operator for the truck by month 3-4.

- **Realistic economic value per channel:** Daily vending = $18-22/hour (including overhead); catering/events = $40-60/hour (lower volume, higher margin, less frequent). Treat them as separate mini-businesses with separate pricing and staffing models, not as add-ons to daily vending.

## Q4: Pricing as cultural communication, not just unit economics

- **A $9 lumpia taco says "affordable street food"; a $14 one says "I'm cooking with intention."** The difference is not just markup—it signals authenticity, ingredient quality, and respect for Filipino-Mexican culinary fusion. At $9, you're competing on price and risk commoditization. At $14, you're competing on story and experience.

- **Your founder identity is your pricing anchor.** You're a solo founder with culinary background launching fusion. That's inherently a "chef's daily creation" narrative, not a volume-optimized fast-casual. Lean into that: $14 taco with visible ube or calamansi, with you visibly prepping. Customers buy the person and the craft, not the efficiency.

- **Test pricing with transparency about your journey.** Share that lumpia, adobo, and horchata come from your family history. Charge for that story: $14 lumpia, $8 horchata, $12 adobo burrito. Early adopters in a mid-size city with 250K population will gladly pay if they feel they're supporting a real chef launching a dream.

- **Pricing structure that builds loyalty:** Consider a "loyalty taco" special ($12) that rewards repeat customers, or a family bundle ($35 for 3 items) that encourages group orders and higher transaction value. This preserves your $14 premium price while giving regulars a way to feel they're getting value.

- **Avoid the gatekeeping trap by owning the premium position clearly.** If you price high but fail to communicate why (story, ingredients, chef background), it reads as greedy. If you price high and lean into cultural identity and craft, it reads as justified. The first 100 customers will tell you which one landed.

## Q5: What if break-even is the trap

- **Break-even is when you lock into unsustainability.** Once you hit 70 covers/day and the truck is self-sustaining, the easiest decision is to maintain that routine. But that routine probably has you working 50+ hours for $1,500-2,000 net profit/week, with no capacity to test new menu items, explore catering, or relocate to a better spot. You're profitable but static.

- **Design deliberate strategic unprofitability into one corner: menu experimentation.** Budget 10% of labor (4-5 hours/week) for testing one new fusion item outside the core three (e.g., Filipino-Korean tteokbokki lumpia, or a plant-based adobo bowl). Let it run at cost or slight loss for 4-6 weeks. If it bombs, you pivot; if it works, you've found a differentiation angle.

- **Another unprofitable corner: location experimentation.** Every 3-4 weeks, spend one service day in a different neighborhood or event space (farmers market, brewery, community festival). Accept lower covers and no profit. You're buying location data and customer discovery. One of these will unlock 20% more traffic than your current spot.

- **The business design question:** If you treat break-even as the finish line, you'll spend 3-5 years maintaining a single truck. If you treat it as a waypoint and deliberately underprioritize profit for 6-12 months, you'll discover whether catering, events, or a second truck is the real growth lever. The unprofitable corners are where insights live.

- **Practically: allocate 15-20% of gross revenue to "strategic slack"—experimentation, location testing, new partnerships—even if it extends your break-even by 2-3 months.** That slack is your optionality fund. Most food truck founders never break this cycle because they optimize too hard for the first break-even.

## Q6: $100K ceiling as a forcing function for lean excellence

- **A capped budget is your greatest asset, not a constraint.** With $60K-100K, you cannot build a sprawling menu, multiple revenue streams, or fancy branding. You must choose ruthlessly: three core items (lumpia, adobo, horchata) and nothing else for the first 90 days. This clarity is what separates you from food trucks that dilute themselves across eight items and confuse customers.

- **Use the constraint to identify your two non-negotiable high-repeat items.** Track which of the three core items gets ordered 2+ times in the same week by the same customer. That's your anchor—price it at $14, make it available every single day, and let it drive 40-50% of your covers. The other two become strategic variety (one anchor, one signature, one loyalty driver). Build your supply chain and menu training around just these three.

- **Lean excellence in operations:** A $100K budget forces you to start with a used truck (not new), a shared commissary kitchen (not dedicated), and solo labor. This is actually ideal. You learn the business in detail. By month 4-6, when you've hit 70+ covers/day consistently and understand your food costs and customer preferences, then you hire help or upgrade equipment. You're building on proven demand, not on assumptions.

- **Menu as competitive moat:** Instead of apologizing for offering only three items, market it as "focused excellence—we do Filipino-Mexican fusion, and we do it right." Competitors with eight items dilute their impact and struggle with consistency. You can afford to run out of lumpia at 6 PM because demand is real, not because you're incompetent. This is a feature.

- **Allocate your $100K this way:** $50K truck (used); $15K commissary deposits, licenses, permits, insurance (first 6 months); $20K ingredient/supplier relationships and initial marketing; $15K contingency and working capital. Zero room for experimentation on equipment or menu until month 3. By then, you'll know whether to invest in a dedicated commissary space, a second menu item, or a second truck. Constraints force prioritization.
