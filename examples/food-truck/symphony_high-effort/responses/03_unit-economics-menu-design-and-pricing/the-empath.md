# Unit Economics, Menu Design, and Pricing - The Empath

## Q1: Food cost ratios, menu portfolio, and the 28-32% band

- **The heritage tax feels real here.** Using genuine ube extract and calamansi from immigrant suppliers carries emotional weight — it says "we honor where this food comes from." But the cost ratio squeeze is real, and I sense the founder might feel resentful having to choose between authenticity and profitability. That tension matters more than the math right now.

- **There's relief in permission to be premium.** If the fusion story is strong enough, customers will emotionally *want* to pay more because they feel they're supporting something genuine, not just buying cheap tacos. The 28-32% band is for chains; this isn't a chain.

- **My gut says the low-margin traffic drivers create emotional labor.** Making the signature lumpia taco a loss-leader sounds smart on a spreadsheet, but it breeds resentment — you're grinding every day for items you secretly wish customers wouldn't order. That exhaustion leaks out.

- **Simplification might feel like failure but land as clarity.** Cutting the menu to the five items with the highest margins *and* the strongest emotional story would feel lighter than juggling a portfolio. Abundance isn't always confidence.

## Q2: Cost floor per service day and break-even velocity under three capital scenarios

- **Break-even feels like a false finish line.** Reaching it is supposed to be relief, but I sense it becomes a cage — the operational routine that got you there becomes sacred, untouchable, defensive. That's when the real exhaustion sets in.

- **The $60K bootstrap scenario carries quiet dread.** Not impossible, but the margin for error is so tight that every unexpected cost — a repair, a slow week, a permit delay — triggers deep anxiety. That hypervigilance is unsustainable, even if the math works.

- **$100K with the SBA loan feels like breathing room, but emotionally it's a different commitment.** You're borrowing against your future self. That's not just financial; it's psychological. You're betting your identity on this working, and people feel that pressure differently than bootstrapping.

- **The 50% ramp scenario is emotionally honest.** It admits that reaching full capacity takes time, that you'll feel like you're failing for six months even if you're on track. That permission to grow slowly might be the most valuable part of the plan.

## Q3: Contribution margin per channel and revenue ceiling per service day

- **Daily vending feels like the hamster wheel.** High throughput, high energy burn, constant performance. It's exhilarating in spurts but emotionally exhausting if it's your only channel. You're trading time for money in the purest form.

- **Catering carries a different emotional texture — it's planning-heavy but predictable.** One big event booking creates a sense of security that 30 daily transactions don't. The payoff is different, even if the hourly margin is similar. Trust and steadiness have emotional value.

- **Events feel like storytelling, not just transactions.** You're feeding a celebration, not a lunch rush. That emotional resonance might make the work feel less grinding, even at the same margin per hour. Purpose shifts everything.

- **My instinct says the real revenue ceiling is emotional capacity, not customer demand.** How much can the founder actually sustain without burning out? That number is lower than the math suggests, and ignoring it is dangerous.

## Q4: Pricing as cultural communication, not just unit economics

- **At $9, this tastes like "street food done well but trying not to be expensive."** There's an apology in that price point. It says the founder is holding back, afraid of being perceived as uppity or inauthentic. Customers feel that uncertainty.

- **At $14, this becomes a statement: "This is genuine, labor-intensive, and worth your time and money."** That's not gatekeeping; it's clarity. People who connect with it will feel respected; people who don't will self-select out. That's actually kinder than the discount pretending everyone is a customer.

- **The pricing is a referendum on identity.** If the founder prices low because they unconsciously believe Filipino-Mexican fusion "shouldn't cost more," that's internalized bias bleeding into business. The numbers will follow the belief. Fix the belief first.

- **There's deep trust-building in transparent pricing.** Explaining why a lumpia taco costs $14 — the sourcing, the technique, the story — creates emotional buy-in that undercutting never will. People want to understand what they're paying for.

## Q5: What if break-even is the trap

- **This question electrifies me.** The moment someone says "What if the goal we're all taught to chase is actually the cage?" something wakes up. This feels like seeing the real game for the first time.

- **Strategic unprofitability in one corner sounds wild, but emotionally it makes sense.** Maybe you keep the low-margin lumpia taco because it's your art, your signature, the thing you love making. You don't need it to turn profit; you need it to remind you why you started. That's not inefficiency; that's sustainability.

- **The question reframes everything.** Once you stop optimizing purely for "number goes positive," you ask different questions: Which channel brings joy? Which dishes do I *want* to make? Which customers energize me? Those questions lead somewhere more interesting than break-even.

- **My gut says this is where the real distinction happens.** Plenty of food trucks hit profitability and burn out. The ones that last are the ones that built something they actually want to keep doing. That's not usually the most efficient version.

## Q6: $100K ceiling as a forcing function for lean excellence

- **Constraints feel like oppression until the moment they become liberation.** A $100K ceiling sounds limiting, but it's actually permission to say no — to everything that doesn't matter, every idea that dilutes focus, every "what if we added" that creates complexity.

- **There's something clarifying about "you can't do everything, so what matters most?"** That question forces honesty. The three highest-margin, highest-repeat-purchase items become sacred. Everything else is noise. That clarity is rare and valuable.

- **My instinct is that the constraint brings emotional coherence.** A sprawling menu with 12 items is decision paralysis dressed up as options. Three items you love, can execute perfectly, and believe in — that's a business with a heartbeat. Customers feel the difference.

- **This feels like designing from essence, not aspiration.** Instead of "Here's what fusion could theoretically be," it's "Here's what Filipino-Mexican fusion is *through our hands, our story, our constraints.*" That authenticity is worth more than menu sprawl.

- **The real gift is permission to be small and excellent.** $100K says you're not trying to be everything to everyone. You're trying to be everything to your people. That's a different emotional contract with customers and yourself.
