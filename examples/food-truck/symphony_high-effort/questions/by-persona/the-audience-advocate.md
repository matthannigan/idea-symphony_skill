# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Food Truck Startup and Expansion Strategy

## Cluster 1: Who Is Actually Being Served — and Who Might Be Left Out?

1. **Who counts as the "customer" here, and who is being designed around**: The Filipino-Mexican fusion concept has obvious cultural resonance for some communities and novelty appeal for others — but who in the city of 250K is most likely to encounter this truck, and are those the people the founder most wants to serve, or just the most convenient audience to reach?

2. **Accessibility of the vending experience itself**: Food truck dining typically means standing in line outdoors, limited seating, cash or card under pressure, and menus on chalkboards or social media — which populations in a mid-size city routinely encounter barriers in that experience (elderly, mobility-impaired, non-English-speaking, unbanked), and how might the concept's physical and transactional design account for them?

3. **Price point as a gatekeeping mechanism**: If unit economics push the menu toward $14–18 entrees, the truck may function primarily as a destination for discretionary-income diners rather than everyday community food infrastructure — what does the founder want the truck to *mean* in the city, and does the pricing strategy align with or contradict that intent?

4. **Cultural authenticity vs. fusion tourism**: Filipino-Mexican fusion could feel genuinely resonant and celebratory to Filipino and Mexican community members, or it could feel like their cuisines have been appropriated for novelty consumption by others — how might the founder build authentic relationships with those communities rather than extract cultural capital from them?

## Cluster 2: The Emotional Journey of First-Time and Returning Customers

5. **The first encounter experience**: A first-time customer walks up to an unfamiliar menu featuring lumpia tacos and adobo burritos — what are they thinking, feeling, and uncertain about in that moment, and what in the experience (signage, staff interaction, sample offers, menu language) resolves or amplifies that uncertainty?

6. **What people say vs. what they actually do**: Customers may say they love adventurous food, but actual ordering behavior often defaults to the familiar — what does the founder's ideal transaction flow assume about customer confidence, and how might the menu or sales interaction be designed to meet people where they actually are rather than where we hope they are?

7. **Building return visits among the people who matter most**: Loyalty programs, social media follows, and email lists tend to reach people who are already comfortable with digital engagement — which customer segments might be highly enthusiastic repeat visitors but systematically excluded from the feedback loops and retention strategies the founder builds?

## Cluster 3: Workers, Vendors, and the Supply Chain of People

8. **Who will work on this truck and under what conditions**: Solo founder with culinary background suggests the founder may work long hours alone or with minimal help — what are the labor conditions being created, and if this grows to a fleet, what kind of employment is being offered to staff, and to whom in the local workforce?

9. **Supplier relationships and cultural sourcing**: Ube, Filipino spices, and specific adobo ingredients may require sourcing from specialty suppliers, many of whom are small immigrant-owned businesses — how might the founder build genuine supply chain relationships with those vendors rather than treating them as commodity inputs?

10. **The commissary kitchen as community infrastructure**: Required commissary kitchen use could mean renting from an established facility, but it could also mean joining a shared kitchen cooperative that serves other small food entrepreneurs — from the perspective of those other entrepreneurs, what kind of neighbor and collaborator might this new business be?

## Cluster 4: Regulatory Process and Civic Voice

11. **Who has voice in the city council ordinance debate, and who doesn't**: The pending food truck ordinance will affect all 30+ trucks operating in the city — but food truck operators, many of whom are immigrant entrepreneurs or people without existing political relationships, may have less voice in that debate than established restaurant lobbies or downtown property owners. What does meaningful participation in the regulatory process look like for operators who lack those connections?

12. **Whose interests does the food truck association represent**: The local food truck association could be a genuine coalition of mutual support, or it could be dominated by the most established operators whose interests diverge from new entrants and those with less capital — from the perspective of someone starting out with $100K total capital and no prior ownership experience, what would make the association genuinely useful rather than performatively welcoming?

## Cluster 5: Growth, Scale, and Who Benefits as the Business Expands

13. **The brick-and-mortar aspiration and neighborhood fit**: A brick-and-mortar location within three years means choosing a neighborhood — that choice carries implications for which communities the business will anchor in, whether it contributes to gentrification pressure, and whether it remains accessible to the communities whose food traditions it draws on. How might the founder think about that choice not just financially but in terms of the role they want to play in the city's social geography?
