# Capital Structure & Financing Decisions - The Pragmatist

---

## The SBA Loan as a Leverage Amplifier in Both Directions

* **Calculate debt service burden first, then decide.** A typical $40K SBA microloan at 10% over five years costs ~$849/month in principal + interest (~$50,940 total). Adding origination fees (10-15%, so $4K-$6K) pushes the effective rate higher. The real question is monthly cash flow: at $6K gross revenue (conservative for a solo truck), debt service is 14% of gross — still manageable. But that assumes consistent volume. A practical approach: run three months of proof-of-concept revenue data, calculate your actual monthly average, then use that as the denominator. Don't borrow on an estimate.

* **The fixed-cost ceiling is the real leverage risk.** The $849 monthly payment must be made whether you do $3K or $10K in revenue that month. This is the two-edged sword. A safer sequencing: bootstrap the first truck for 3-4 months with the $60K, prove you can hit $5K+/month in real revenue, then apply for the loan when you have both collateral (the paid-for truck) and demonstrated unit economics. The SBA will actually like this better because you've de-risked the loan.

* **If the loan is delayed 6-12 months, the $60K path is viable but requires ruthless prioritization.** Spend $20K on a solid used truck (avoid the bottom-tier junk), $8K on commissary/permit setup and initial food costs, $4K on marketing/sampling, leaving $28K operational runway. You'll need to hit $3.5K/week gross to survive on that reserve over six months. It's tight, but not impossible if you launch in a high-foot-traffic zone.

* **The validation-first approach protects you from the wrong debt.** The loan becomes genuinely useful after you've answered: "Do customers actually want this product?" and "Can I execute consistently?" Debt borrowed on assumptions is debt borrowed twice — once in capital, once in stress. Wait until your own numbers justify it.

* **Watch for hidden costs that blow the model.** SBA loans often require personal guarantees (you're personally liable if the business fails), and some require collateral beyond the truck. Factor in legal costs ($500-$1500) to structure this properly, and insurance premiums that may increase if you're carrying debt. A practical next step: get a loan pre-qualification letter, read the terms carefully, and ask specifically about personal guarantee scope and what collateral they're asking for.

## Lease Versus Own Versus Used-Truck Purchase as Risk Distribution

* **The 3-year total cost spreadsheet reveals the true tradeoff.** Used truck purchased outright: $25K truck + $8K maintenance/repairs/tires + $3K insurance + $1.5K registration/tags = ~$37.5K. New truck financed at $60K over five years with 8% APR: ~$1,380/month × 36 months = $49,680 principal + interest, plus maintenance (less in year 1-2, more later) = ~$55K+. Leased truck at $800/month: $28,800 over 36 months + maintenance included. The spreadsheet isn't close — owning a used truck outright preserves the most working capital.

* **Lease shields you from catastrophic repair risk but costs you flexibility and option value.** If the business underperforms, you're still paying $800/month. If it takes off, you own nothing to leverage further. A practical approach: lease is insurance against the "this idea doesn't work" scenario. Own if you're confident in the model after six months of validation.

* **Purchase financing (new truck) is the worst risk-adjusted option for a solo founder in year one.** You're carrying $1,380/month in fixed debt while your revenue is still volatile, and you're betting on five-year stability when you don't know if this business lasts 18 months. Avoid this until you've proven the model and have room to absorb a $1,500 repair without stress.

* **Used truck outright hits the Goldilocks zone for year-one cash preservation.** You get flexibility (you can modify it, use it as collateral later if you need the SBA loan), you own the upside if the business scales, and you keep ~$35K in operational buffer. The maintenance wildcard is real — set aside $100/month ($3K over year one) for unexpected repairs from day one, and if you don't need it, that's profit.

* **The right choice depends on your risk tolerance and access to repair capital.** If you sleep better knowing you're not liable for transmission failure, lease. If you're comfortable with owner-operator responsibility but want to preserve cash, buy used. The worst outcome is financing a depreciating asset while your revenue is unproven.

## Working-Capital Reserve and Equipment-Cost Creep

* **Start with three months of fixed costs as your minimum reserve.** For a solo food truck, that's roughly $2K/month in permits, commissary fees, insurance, vehicle upkeep, and personal draw (you need to eat). Three months = $6K minimum. This is different from operating capital — this is the runway that lets you absorb a slow July or an unexpected $1.5K transmission repair.

* **The annual-versus-monthly payment schedule is a cash-flow lever you can pull immediately.** Paying $1,500 annually for permits is better than $150/month if you have the capital upfront, because it keeps that money working rather than divvied into pieces. However, the practical constraint is: do you have cash on hand in February when the annual permits are due? If not, monthly is your only choice. Pragmatically, aim for annual payments by month 6-9 once you've proven cash flow, because the interest on holding cash is lower than the cash-flow stress of monthly obligations.

* **The 15-20% contingency reserve is the hardest number to hit but the most critical.** On a $60K budget, that's $9K-$12K reserved purely for "bad truck luck." Used food trucks are mechanical lottery tickets. You'll hit something: a fuel pump at $800, a transmission bearing at $2K, or brake work at $1.5K. Reserve this openly in your budget, don't treat it as a "maybe we won't need it" amount. If nothing breaks, you've earned a bonus quarter. If something does, you don't panic.

* **Build a specific trigger system for when to pause expansion.** Define it now: "If cash reserves drop below 45 days of fixed costs AND we haven't hit $5K weekly revenue consistently, we pause any new investments." This removes emotion from the decision in month 7 when you're tempted to buy a second truck because things look good. The trigger is the rule, not the hope.

* **When the first major failure hits, cut marketing and discretionary payroll first.** Keep the truck running, keep food costs covered, keep permits paid. Marketing can be zero for 30 days. Your own draw can be cut. The truck and licenses cannot. That's the hierarchy.

## The Lean Startup's Minimum Viable Product Lens

* **Validation milestones before debt: hit three specific numbers first.** (1) Consistent $5K/week gross revenue over four consecutive weeks — this proves the product sells and you can execute consistently. (2) Food cost ratio below 28% of revenue — this proves your unit economics work. (3) Catering bookings at 1-2 per month minimum with 30%+ margins — this proves a second revenue stream exists. These three numbers, sustained for six weeks, constitute proof of concept. Then borrow.

* **The sequencing advantage is real and quantifiable.** If you can show the SBA that you've already done $5K/week for six weeks with zero debt, your loan application improves dramatically. You move from "hopeful founder with a food truck idea" to "operating business seeking growth capital." You'll qualify for better terms, faster approval, potentially lower rates. The math of waiting is: six weeks of $5K/week revenue (~$30K gross) buys you better loan terms that might save you $2K-$3K in interest over five years.

* **The unvalidated debt bet is expensive insurance on the wrong thing.** Borrowing $40K to validate whether your product sells is like buying full insurance on a rental car before test-driving it. You're paying (in stress, in interest) for information you could get for free. Drive the truck for six weeks with your $60K, answer the three questions above, then borrow on a proven model.

* **Watch for the "false validation" trap.** Weeks 1-4 of a food truck launch are honeymoon weeks — everyone tries the new fusion spot. Weeks 5-8 are the real test: do repeat customers come back, or was it novelty? Make sure your validation runs long enough to capture the novelty fade and the repeat-customer baseline.

* **The loan terms shift when you have data.** A pre-launch loan for $40K might come at 12-15% (high risk). A post-validation loan for $40K might come at 8-10% (lower risk). That 4-5% difference over five years is worth thousands in saved interest. The practical path: bootstrap first, validate second, finance third.
