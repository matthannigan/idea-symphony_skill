# Unit Economics & Break-Even - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Food Cost Ratio For A Fusion Menu

* **Fusion sourcing creates hidden cost drift.** Filipino specialty ingredients (ube powder, adobo spices, calamansi) have volatile wholesale pricing, especially in a mid-size market without established ethnic supplier competition. You may benchmark against the 28–35% industry range, but that baseline assumes commodity-heavy menus. Have you stress-tested what happens if your ube supplier raises prices 30% mid-season, or if a new competitor floods the market with cheaper fusion tacos, forcing you to choose between margin compression or menu reformulation? Mitigation: Build a quarterly cost audit into your pricing model and pre-identify two alternative suppliers for each specialty ingredient now, before launch.

* **The four competitors' "ceiling test" is unreliable.** Other fusion trucks may be operating at different capital structures, with cheaper founder labor, or accepting lower margins as a growth strategy. Just because they charge $12 for an adobo burrito doesn't mean it's sustainable at your cost structure. Have you actually walked their supply chain or spoken to suppliers who know their throughput? Mitigation: Conduct a blind cost build-up for each competitor's signature item from first principles, then add 15% contingency to your own food cost assumptions as a conservative buffer.

* **Blended ratios hide cross-subsidy traps.** If lumpia tacos run 22% food cost and ube horchata runs 18%, but adobo burritos run 38%, and customers end up buying 2:3:1 of those items (different from your forecast), your blended ratio swings. You could hit your target 31% blended cost while individual items bleed money. Mitigation: Set minimum acceptable food-cost margins per item, not just menu-level targets, and monitor actual sales mix weekly. If mix drifts, adjust portion sizes or pricing at that item level before compounded losses stack up.

## Break-Even Architecture At The Truck Level

* **Your fixed-cost estimate is almost certainly too low.** You've listed truck payment, commissary, permits, insurance, fuel, and loan servicing. But you've excluded: unexpected equipment repair (a $3K walk-in cooler compressor failure), health department reinspections, peak-season commissary surcharges, vehicle registration renewal, and unplanned marketing to sustain volume in a competitive market. A realistic fixed-cost floor is likely 15–25% higher than your initial projection. Have you built in a contingency band? Mitigation: Track every cost item for the first 6 months, then project 12-month annualized fixed costs. Add 20% as a safety margin, recalculate break-even with that adjusted baseline.

* **Daily cover count variability is your actual risk.** The math is only as good as your daily transaction assumptions. Mid-size cities have weather volatility, seasonal tourism fluctuations, and local events that create 30–50% swings in foot traffic week-to-week. You might break even at 120 transactions/day in July, but hit 70 in February. Have you modeled a 12-month cash-flow profile accounting for seasonal troughs, not just an annualized daily average? Mitigation: Identify your lowest-expected revenue month and ensure that at that month's transaction count, you don't go cash-flow negative. If you do, either increase your starting capital or line up a credit facility before launch.

* **Average ticket size creep masks fixed-cost dependency.** Your break-even math might assume a $15 average ticket, but if actual tickets average $12.50, your break-even transaction count rises 20%. You might be optimistic about add-on rates (drinks, extra sides) without testing them in a real market. Mitigation: Run a soft-launch week or two at a lower-traffic spot with real pricing and track actual ticket average obsessively. Build your 12-month model using the observed ticket size, not your projection.

## Break-Even As A Moving Target

* **A 20% food cost spike is plausible within 12 months.** If commodity prices (flour, oil, protein) surge, or if your specialty supplier increases prices due to demand, your food cost could jump from 31% to 37%—suddenly eating 6 percentage points of your margin. You're assuming your pricing is sticky, but in a competitive market with four fusion rivals, raising prices 15% may lose customers faster than the cost increase squeezes you. Have you modeled customer elasticity? If price-sensitive regulars defect, your volume drops, and fixed costs become an even larger % of revenue. Mitigation: Lock in long-term pricing for your top 3–4 high-volume ingredients now through supplier contracts, and pre-plan a "tiered menu" where you can offer smaller portions of expensive items rather than raising prices across the board.

* **Licensing fees could change mid-game.** The city council is "currently debating" new ordinances. If new annual licensing fees are $5K–$10K, that's another fixed cost you haven't yet absorbed. Worse, new vending hour restrictions downtown could eliminate your peak-revenue slots (lunch rush in the financial district). You have no control over this risk. Mitigation: Engage with the local food truck association immediately; understand the proposed ordinance detail, build relationships with council members who might vote on this, and identify alternate vending zones outside downtown that are less likely to be restricted. If the ordinance passes restrictively, you pivot vending strategy before it goes live.

* **Margin buffer of less than 12–15% leaves no room for error.** If your contribution margin (revenue minus food and direct labor) is less than 15% before fixed costs, a single quarter of slower business, supplier price spike, or unexpected repair hits you hard. You need a cash reserve that covers at least 1.5–2 months of fixed costs to survive volatility without taking on expensive bridge financing. Mitigation: Don't just break even on the financials—build a cash reserve of $15K–$20K before scaling to a second truck. If you hit break-even but your cash reserve drops below $10K, treat it as a signal to slow growth plans and rebuild reserves, even if the P&L looks positive.
