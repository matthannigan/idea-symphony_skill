# Unit Economics & Break-Even - The First Principles Thinker

## Food Cost Ratio for a Fusion Menu

* **Start with what you actually control.** Food cost percentage is meaningful only if you can reliably execute it. Rather than benchmarking against 28-35%, first determine your actual cost per completed dish by purchasing the exact ingredients you'll use, cooking one unit, and measuring the total material cost. Filipino and Mexican ingredients behave differently in sourcing and shelf-life — ube powder may need bulk purchasing to hit good pricing, while Mexican peppers may be cheaper fresh. The real food cost ratio is revealed by your supply chain, not by industry averages. Once you know actual cost, you can price with confidence.

* **Deconstruct the fusion menu into cost families.** At its core, you have three price tiers: high-margin drinks (ube horchata), mid-margin starches (adobo burritos), and lower-margin wraps (lumpia tacos). Each has a fundamentally different food cost driver — horchata is proportional to dairy and flavoring, burritos are tied to meat cost, tacos to wrapper and filler. Rather than calculating one blended ratio, model each tier separately and see which drives actual profitability. You may find that selling more horchata at 70% margin offsets lower margins elsewhere.

* **The fundamental question isn't your food cost ratio — it's your sourcing lock.** The benchmark range assumes commodity sourcing. If you can get adobo spice paste cheaper by bulk-ordering direct from a Filipino supplier than buying retail, you've just changed your cost structure. Conversely, if that supplier has minimum order quantities you can't hit early-stage, you're stuck with retail pricing. Before committing to menu items, lock down two credible sourcing paths (retail and wholesale) for each ingredient and price both scenarios. Your food cost ratio is only as stable as your supplier relationships.

* **Volume unlocks margin in fusion cooking.** The difference between 32% food cost and 26% often comes from making more of your own spice blends and sauces rather than buying premade components. For a fusion truck with low volume (early months), you'll use retail components and accept 35-38% food cost. As you scale to 150+ daily covers, you move to volume-sourced ingredients and batch-preparation techniques, dropping to 28-30%. Don't optimize for an end-state you can't yet sustain — price for early-stage costs and capture margin improvement as a strategic win later.

## Break-Even Architecture at the Truck Level

* **Begin with the fundamental constraint: how many hours can you actually operate?** Break-even depends on fixed costs divided by margin per transaction. Fixed costs are somewhat knowable, but the true constraint is operator capacity. A solo founder can realistically run 30-40 service hours per week without burning out. That's 5-6 days, 6-8 hours per day. If your market demands 50+ weekly hours to hit transaction volume, you've already failed the model — you'll need to hire labor (which increases fixed costs) or operate fewer days (which reduces transaction count). Start there: assume 35 hours per week, 50 weeks per year (accounting for illness and downtime). That's 1,750 operating hours annually.

* **Map the fixed-cost stack as tiers.** The fundamental mistake is lumping all fixed costs into one number. Instead: (1) Truck payment/lease — non-negotiable, happens regardless of sales. (2) Permits and insurance — regulatory minimums you can't avoid. (3) Commissary rental — tied to kitchen availability, not transaction count. (4) Fuel and basic supplies — semi-variable, scaling with operating hours. (5) Loan servicing — only if you take the SBA loan. Calculate total monthly fixed cost in each tier. With $60K bootstrap, you're likely debt-free, so loan servicing is zero. With $100K SBA, add $300-400/month debt service. The three scenarios have fundamentally different fixed-cost profiles, not just different amounts.

* **The daily transaction count is the weak point; stress-test it aggressively.** Most food truck failures come from overestimating daily cover count. A realistic first-month projection is 40-60 transactions per day (average $12-14 ticket = $480-840 daily revenue). That's not crowded; that's a manageable queue during lunch or dinner rush. Many operators assume 100+ covers immediately — that's fantasy. At 50 transactions/day × $13 average ticket = $650 daily gross. If your variable cost (food, packaging, labor if not you) is 55% of revenue, you have $292 daily contribution to cover fixed costs. If fixed costs are $1,800/month ($900/month rent, $300 insurance, $200 permits, $400 fuel), you need $60 daily contribution × 30 days = $1,800 monthly. You'd break even in month one with 50 covers/day. Sounds good — but what happens in month three when the novelty wears off and you drop to 35 covers/day? Now you're at $1,022 monthly contribution, running a $778 deficit.

* **Stress-test assumptions about the cost variables, not just transaction count.** The dangerous question isn't "what if we get fewer customers?" — it's "what if two assumptions change simultaneously?" What if commissary rent increases 20% AND you hire part-time help (pushing labor from 0% to 15% of revenue)? Fixed costs jump from $1,800 to $2,160, and variable costs jump from 40% to 55%. At 50 covers/day, contribution drops from $292 to $177 daily. You're now losing $300/month. The model is fragile. Build in margin: target break-even at 40 covers/day, not 50. That gives you breathing room when reality deviates.

## Break-Even as a Moving Target

* **The fundamental flaw in break-even analysis is treating it as a static calculation.** Once you open, food costs will move (supplier price increases, ingredient substitution out of necessity), permit fees will move (the pending ordinance), and commissary rates may move (if the city-approved kitchen raises prices). A single break-even point is fiction. Instead, calculate the break-even range: at what transaction volume and ticket size do you break even if food costs rise to 38%, commissary rent rises 25%, and licensing fees add $200/month? That's your realistic worst-case break-even, not today's ideal case.

* **Identify which cost variable is your margin killer.** Food cost is the loudest lever — a 5-point swing from 30% to 35% is noticeable. But the true killer is often fixed costs rising while transaction volume stays flat. If the city adds $2,400 in annual licensing fees (the pending ordinance), that's $200/month additional break-even requirement. At $13 average ticket and 55% variable cost, you need an extra 30 transactions per day just to cover that fee. If your market can't absorb 30 more daily covers, you're structurally unprofitable under the new ordinance — no amount of pricing or cost-cutting fixes it. Engage with city council now (before the ordinance passes) and identify the actual fee impact.

* **Design your pricing strategy around the margin buffer, not the current margin.** If you need 30% margin to stay solvent (after all costs), and food cost is 30% + labor 15% + packaging 5%, your gross margin is 50%. Price aggressively within that 50% gross margin to capture 35%+ of revenue as margin. This gives you a 5-point buffer if food costs spike or a new fee hits. If you optimize for 33% margin (targeting break-even today), a 5% food cost increase bankrupts you. The principle: lock in pricing that delivers 35-38% margin minimum, based on today's best estimate of costs. That buffer is what keeps you solvent through the first year of volatility.

* **Create quarterly checkpoints, not an annual assumption.** At the end of each quarter, recalculate break-even with actual cost data. If food costs are running 32% instead of 30%, adjust prices or menus before Q2 ends. If commissary rent stays stable but a new competitor opens (dropping your transaction count), decide now whether to diversify into catering or events, rather than waiting until month 12 to react. Break-even isn't a number to hit — it's a floor you monitor and defend as conditions change. The business survives not by hitting a static break-even, but by staying above it through active margin management.
