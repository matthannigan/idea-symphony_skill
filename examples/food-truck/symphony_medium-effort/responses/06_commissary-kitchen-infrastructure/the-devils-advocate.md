# Commissary Kitchen Infrastructure - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Commissary Dependency As A Single Point Of Failure

* **Underestimating termination risk.** Most founders assume a 30-day termination clause means 30 days to find an alternative. In reality, you'll lose 3-5 days to discovery, 5-10 days to negotiation with a backup facility (if one exists), and 5-10 days to regulatory paperwork changes with the health department. Your buffer is a week at best. Mitigation: Execute a backup commissary agreement *before* you launch, even if you don't use it. Cost: one application fee and maybe a small monthly hold. This buys certainty that another facility is contractually obligated to take you on 72 hours' notice.

* **Rate escalation eating into unit economics.** The commissary operator knows you've built your pricing model around their current rates. If they raise fees by 15% mid-year (and landlords do this to food service tenants routinely), your margin collapses. You can't raise menu prices fast enough to match in a competitive market. Mitigation: Lock in rates for 24-36 months in your initial contract, with escalation capped at 3% annually. Worst case, it costs you a slightly higher up-front rate — but it protects against a margin-destroying surprise.

* **Operational visibility debt.** You'll never know why the commissary lost its health permit until you show up one morning and they're shuttered. Health department violations often compound silently. Mitigation: Establish a standing monthly check-in with the commissary owner about health inspection status and pending code changes. Make it a friendly relationship-building conversation, not a "gotcha" audit. You're also buying intelligence about their staffing and equipment stability.

* **No exit clause for irreconcilable relationship failure.** Commissary operators can be territorial about customer relationships, ingredient sourcing, or equipment access if you get too successful or if new owners take over. You might need out, but the contract doesn't give you one without penalty. Mitigation: Build in a mutual separation clause that allows either party to exit with 60 days' notice and refund of any prepaid balance, after the first 12 months.

## The Commissary As Strategic Asset And Geographic-Equity Variable

* **Compliance infrastructure as an invisible barrier to underserved neighborhoods.** You see compliance as "just how food trucks work" — but commissaries are geographically sparse. If there's only one commissary in town and it's downtown, you can only service neighborhoods within a 20-minute cold-chain commute. This systematically locks out less-affluent, less-central neighborhoods. Mitigation: During your location scouting phase, map both the neighborhoods you want to serve AND the commissaries accessible from them. If your dream neighborhood is a 35-minute drive from the only commissary, you're not actually serving that neighborhood — you're serving it logistics that are already unprofitable. This isn't a problem you solve by working harder; you solve it by choosing different neighborhoods or finding a different commissary *before* you commit to the location.

* **The commissary relationship as a competitive disadvantage.** If the commissary also services competitors, they see your recipes, your ingredient suppliers, your pricing structure, and your sales rhythm. They have leverage you don't. A competing food truck might get priority access to prep time or premium equipment. Mitigation: Negotiate exclusive prep windows (e.g., 5-6am, Wednesday-Friday) and exclusive ingredient-storage space in your contract. Also investigate whether the commissary owner is friendly with any of your 4 fusion competitors — if they are, understand that relationship dynamic *before* signing on.

* **Missing the platform play entirely.** You're focused on compliance, but the strategic asset is the relationships. If the commissary is where you meet other food operators, exchange supplier intel, and spot partnership opportunities, that's gold. But most founders avoid commissary networking because they view it as competitive risk. Mitigation: Schedule a monthly "operator hour" at the commissary where you and other tenants swap stories about suppliers, permits, and customer trends. The founder who builds relationships at the commissary scales faster than the one who treats it as a pure-cost facility.

* **Assuming equity is a location problem when it's also a commissary problem.** You might decide to rotate your vending schedule through lower-income neighborhoods three days a week. But if those neighborhoods are 25+ minutes from the commissary, your food costs and delivery costs spike, making the rotation economically unsustainable after 4-6 weeks. You'll slip back to central neighborhoods. Mitigation: Identify which neighborhoods you want to serve *first*, then find a commissary (or negotiate for a secondary prep satellite) that's within 15 minutes of those neighborhoods. Equity requires logistics design, not just route planning.

## The Hub-And-Spoke Commissary Model

* **You don't have the capital or expertise to operate a true hub.** The hub-and-spoke model works for Airline X because they have economies of scale, professional logistics staff, and purchasing power across 50+ routes. You're a solo founder with one truck trying to scale to maybe three trucks in three years. The commissary operator also isn't your employee — they're a landlord relation. You can't impose hub-and-spoke discipline on them without them charging premium rates (which kills your margin). Mitigation: Don't try to build a hub-and-spoke model yourself. Instead, find a commissary that *already operates one* — they batch sauces, standardize proteins, and manage inventory across multiple customers. You rent prep space in their system, not the other way around. This gives you hub-and-spoke benefits without the capital or management overhead.

* **Premature optimization locks you into expensive overhead.** If you spend 18 months and $15K building out commissary infrastructure for a three-truck fleet that never materializes (because the regulatory environment shifts or you decide to pivot to catering-only), you've sunk cost that won't recover. Mitigation: Start lean. Use the first 12 months to prove unit economics on a single truck with a basic commissary relationship. Only after you've landed your second truck and validated that the expansion works should you invest in hub-and-spoke infrastructure like shared sauce-batching or equipment standardization.

* **Quality standardization assumes stable staffing and processes you don't yet have.** The hub-and-spoke model in airline networks works because routes are defined and stable — same routes, same demand patterns, same logistics. Your operation isn't stable yet. You're still learning whether Filipino-Mexican fusion resonates at different times of day, whether catering is viable, what your actual food costs are. If you lock in commissary-level standardization now, you'll fight the commissary every time you want to experiment. Mitigation: Keep your commissary relationship deliberately loose for the first 18 months. Use it as a prep facility, not an operational control center. Once you have stable, repeatable processes across two trucks, *then* invest in standardization and hub-and-spoke discipline.

* **Geographic inflexibility baked into the model.** If your hub commissary is downtown but you want to expand into suburbs or satellite neighborhoods, you're stuck with long cold-chain commutes that erode margins. True hub-and-spoke (think ghost kitchen networks) work when they're intentionally distributed. Mitigation: Before you commit to commissary infrastructure investments, map out your three-year expansion geographically. If you're planning to expand north, south, and east, you need a central commissary *or* satellite prep capacity at each spoke. Choose your commissary location with that map in mind, not just where rent is cheapest.
