# Customer Experience, Access & Equity - The Pragmatist

---

## Unfamiliar Cuisine and the First-Transaction Arc

* **Menu clarity as the first lever.** Describe each item in functional terms first — "crispy spring roll with seasoned ground meat and vegetables" before naming it "lumpia." Include one sentence on flavor/texture ("savory and crispy, dipped in sweet sauce"). Test this at your prep kitchen with 10 people unfamiliar with Filipino cuisine. Your goal: they order without asking what it is. The handoff moment is where most risk lives — hand the item in a small white container with a simple card that shows the item name, the sauce on the side (in a tiny sealed cup, not already applied), and one suggested pairing. Cost to implement: $0.15 per item.

* **The staff script for questions.** Train staff with a 30-second answer: "It's like a spring roll filled with meat and vegetables — crispy outside, savory inside. A lot of people compare it to an egg roll, but with Filipino flavors." If they ask about the sauce, you hand it to them in a separate container so they control the amount. This removes the "I got something I don't know how to eat" feeling. Run role-plays with your staff before opening.

* **Portion and pace strategy.** Serve slightly smaller portions than you might otherwise — not as a cost-cutting move, but so first-timers can finish what's on their plate and feel successful. The emotional arc you're designing is: curiosity → order → relief that it's manageable → satisfaction → "I could do that again." A massive burrito that leaves someone half-full at 30 minutes into eating is a loss.

* **A discrete tasting station.** One day per month (Taco Tuesday or whatever day is slowest), offer small free samples of one signature item — but call it "New Customer Tasting" in your signage, making it socially acceptable to try without committing to a full order. This removes the barrier of "what if I hate it and wasted $9?"

## Economic, Dietary, and Mobility Accessibility

* **Price anchoring below the obvious local competition.** Research the 5-7 food trucks currently operating in your vending zones. Price your fusion items at 15% below their comparable items for the first 90 days. Your unit economics still work if your food cost is managed (which it should be as a culinary person). The insight: someone earning $15/hour needs your lunch to cost $7-8 max, and they need to see that number on your menu board from 10 feet away. Price creep is a perpetual risk — anchor low, then raise incrementally, never drop.

* **Seating logistics that don't require capital outlay.** Your truck is in vending zones, likely public spaces. Identify benches, ledges, nearby buildings with outdoor tables, and anything that functions as seating. Scout these during your location-selection phase. If you're in a business district, you're likely near commercial buildings with outdoor seating. If you're near transit, there are bus shelter benches. Your role: know where your customers are eating your food and plan menu items accordingly (things that don't leak, that travel well, that don't need both hands immediately). This costs $0 and solves 60% of the "eating while standing" problem.

* **A standing-friendly menu.** This is where fusion works against accessibility. Design at least 4 items that can be eaten one-handed, standing, without falling apart — think burritos, tacos in secure shells, items on skewers. Your adobo burrito already does this. Build the menu around the physical reality of a food truck customer, not around what's the most impressive dish. Remove anything that requires utensils or two hands.

* **Simple dietary accommodation strategy.** Identify your top three requests: vegetarian, gluten-free, spicy-level adjustment. Train your staff to say: "We have a vegetarian version of the lumpia taco, or a veggie burrito. Both are on the board and cost the same. Which appeals to you?" This takes 10 seconds and removes the awkwardness. Don't try to accommodate every dietary restriction in month one — narrow scope, deep execution. You can expand after you see what your actual customers request.

* **Asking directly about mobility barriers at event catering.** When organizations call to book catering, ask one specific question: "Will this event include people with mobility limitations — aging parents, people in wheelchairs, or standing-for-long-periods challenges?" If yes, your catering model shifts to individual boxes that can be eaten at tables, not standing-with-a-plate format. This signals that you've thought about inclusion and opens the door to catering bookings from community centers, nonprofits, and senior organizations.

## Cultural Legibility and Honoring Origins

* **The origin story on the menu and in conversations.** Every item should have one or two sentences explaining what it is and why you're serving it. Not as academic history, but as personal connection: "Lumpia is my grandmother's recipe, fried spring roll style, packed with her seasoning blend." Not: "Lumpia is a Filipino appetizer with Spanish colonial influences." Your customers will trust the food more if they understand it comes from somewhere real, not from a fusion-concept brainstorm.

* **Staff who can speak to the dishes with authenticity.** Hire or train staff who have personal connection to Filipino and/or Mexican food cultures, or invest time to build that knowledge yourself. When a customer asks "Is this actually Filipino?" you need a staff member who can say, "Yes — my mom made lumpia like this," not someone reading from a script. This is especially important in the first 90 days when cultural perception is being set. Budget for slightly higher-wage staff who can do cultural interpretation, not just order-taking.

* **Menu naming that respects both cuisines without diminishing either.** Your "adobo burrito" is a direct statement: adobo (Filipino) inside a burrito (Mexican form). Don't call it "Fusion Inspiration #3" or "The Diplomat." Name it honestly. A Filipino grandmother who sees "adobo burrito" will know exactly what you're doing — you're using a beloved Filipino stew inside a Mexican vessel. That's legible. Same with lumpia tacos: spring roll filling in taco form. Direct, respectful, no obfuscation.

* **Community relationships as the legibility check.** Before you launch, have 3-4 Filipino and 3-4 Mexican friends or community members try your menu. Ask them directly: "Does this feel authentic to you, or does it feel like a play on your culture?" Their answer tells you whether you're honoring or aestheticizing. If you hear hesitation, iterate. This costs $50 in food and is the best $50 you spend on menu development.

## Discovery Channels Beyond Social Media

* **Printed, physical location strategy.** Your truck is at a specific corner, on a specific day, from specific hours. Get a printed flyer done (500 copies, $40) with the truck's name, the items, a photo of one signature item, the weekly location schedule, and your phone number. Distribute these at: the nearby commissary kitchen, local coffee shops, the city library, nonprofit offices, community centers, gyms, laundromats, bus stops in the vending zone area. Update them monthly. Seniors, working-class people, and recent immigrants rely on this format; social media can supplement, not replace.

* **Commissary kitchen and local food truck network relationships.** Your commissary kitchen is where you prep daily. The staff there interact with dozens of food truck operators and other users. Be friendly, ask about word-of-mouth reputation, ask if they know which trucks draw loyal customers and why. Some kitchens have bulletin boards. This costs nothing and taps into a network of people who understand your operational realities.

* **Local transit partnership or bus-stop presence.** If your truck operates near major bus stops or transit centers, many potential customers are waiting for buses. Partner with the transit authority (if possible) to post a simple A-frame sign at the bus stop showing your location and hours. Or position your truck so it's visible from the bus stop. People on tight lunch breaks will notice a food truck they can reach in 5 minutes.

* **Spot placement near anchor institutions.** If there's a hospital, university, large employer, or government office in a vending zone, position your truck on days and times when those people have lunch breaks. They'll find you by proximity, not digital discovery. Make a relationship with their HR or staff coordinator and ask if they can mention you in internal newsletters. One company event or staff lunch partnership generates awareness.

## The Opening-Day Arc

* **Scenario design from glance to photo-share.** Walk this through as literal choreography. Someone walks past at 12:15 pm. They smell food and see your truck. (Moment 1: signage clarity — they immediately know what kind of truck this is and there's a menu board visible from 15 feet away.) They walk closer. (Moment 2: visual appeal — the truck is clean, the menu is colorful and legible, there's no line, so it feels easy.) They step up to order. (Moment 3: staff greeting — "Hey, haven't seen you before — first time?" with a smile, not judgment.) They give an order; staff repeats it back and asks, "Anything else?" They pay, they get a number and told "about 8 minutes." (Moment 4: wait clarity — they know how long and can sit nearby or step away.) Food comes out, they take a photo of it before eating (that's the photo-share moment). They taste it and their face changes — that's your design goal.

* **Pre-opening dry runs with real strangers.** Invite 10-15 people who are not friends (recruit from Nextdoor, local Facebook groups, offer a $5 discount) to come during a soft opening. Watch what they do. Don't help them. Do they know what to order? Do they ask questions? Do they understand the items? Do they know where to stand while waiting? Their behavior tells you exactly what's ambiguous. Fix those before opening to the public.

* **The moment-by-moment checklist.** Menu board visibility and legibility, staff greeting script, order-repeating protocol, wait-time communication, handoff presentation (is the food hot, is it presented well, is there any leaking?), eating location signage (point to seating or nearby benches), and a way to take a photo that looks good (good lighting, the truck's name visible in the background). Each of these is a designed moment. Executing all seven creates an arc that feels intentional and makes people want to come back and tell others.

## The Invisible-Customer Provocation

* **Menu design for the skeptic.** Your current menu (lumpia tacos, adobo burritos, ube horchata) appeals to people curious about fusion. To convert the person who "finds fusion suspicious," you need one item that's so familiar it's anchoring: a really good carne asada burrito, or a well-executed carnitas taco. Position it first on the menu. Your other items are then "these are like that, but with a Filipino twist." This gives the suspicious person an on-ramp: they can start with something they recognize, feel good about that choice, and then become curious about the lumpia version. Your menu isn't "fusion first" — it's "familiar first, then fusion."

* **Location and timing strategy for the repetitive eater.** The person who eats at the same three places every week is doing so because of proximity and habit. You need to be closer than those three places and easier to decide on than they are. If your truck is parked a block from their workplace every Tuesday at 11:45 am, and your menu has that familiar-first item plus one clearly-explained unfamiliar item, they'll try you by accident of location. Then they'll return because habit is powerful and you're closer. Repetition beats conversion — find the route that puts you in front of the same person multiple times, and proximity beats marketing.

* **Testing the "indifferent" hypothesis early.** After your first month, audit your customers: who are they? Are you getting fusion-seekers or a mix? If you're only getting the already-enthusiastic, you have a discovery and positioning problem, not a food problem. Shift location or timing, adjust menu signage to lead with familiar items, or adjust pricing. The real business risk isn't that fusion is too niche — it's that your current positioning only reaches the niche that already wants fusion. Test the conversion strategy in month two and adjust by month three.

## The Regular's Story

* **Relationship architecture: name, order memory, incremental surprise.** Month one: "Hi, what can I get for you?" Month two, if they're weekly: "Hey, haven't seen you since Tuesday — the usual?" By month four, you know their order, you have it ready while they're still ordering, and it feels like they're not just a customer but someone you think about between visits. This costs nothing and is the deepest loyalty driver. The incremental surprise: once a month, give them a free upgrade ("I made extra ube horchata today, you should try it") or a new item ("We're testing a new sauce, would you be a guinea pig?"). This signals that they matter and are part of your development, not just customers you're selling to.

* **Communication consistency and dependability.** A weekly regular has scheduled their lunch around your location and time. If you're supposed to be at the corner of Main and 5th every Tuesday 11:30-2:00, and you're not there, you've broken the relationship. Create a simple system: Google Calendar for locations/hours (free, sharable, public), text reminders to regulars if you're ever closing (get their number after the first two visits), and a commitment to being there on schedule even on slow days. Reliability is the currency of loyalty.

* **Catering and event extension for regulars.** After someone's been a weekly customer for three months, they're no longer just a lunch customer — they're a brand ambassador. If they work for an organization, mention: "Hey, do you do company events or team lunches? I can cater that if you want to introduce your team to the truck." Many businesses buy catering for staff events. Your regulars can be the door-opener. One catering event pays for dozens of truck days and builds organizational loyalty that outlasts individual employment turnover.

* **Medium-term retention through menu evolution.** After a regular's been coming for six months, they're curious what you'll do next. Have a "new item" every two months. Don't change the core (lumpia tacos, adobo burritos, horchata stay), but rotate secondary items: "This month we're trying crispy tofu lumpia" or "I'm testing a Vietnamese-inspired banh mi burrito." Ask regulars for feedback. This keeps the relationship from going stale and makes them feel like they're helping you build the business, not just consuming it.
