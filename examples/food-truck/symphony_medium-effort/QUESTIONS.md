# Brainstorming Questions: Food Truck Startup and Expansion Strategy

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Business Model Foundations & Identity

**Cluster focus**: Interrogating the hidden assumptions, founder-level constraints, and latent strengths that define what this business fundamentally is before any pricing, permit, or expansion decision is made.

1. **Fusion as differentiator or positioning risk**: Why do we assume Filipino-Mexican fusion is a strategic differentiator in a 30-truck market that already contains four similar fusion concepts — is the brand bet that distinctive positioning attracts a loyal following whose word-of-mouth compensates for a smaller initial market, and what specific evidence from those four competitors, what early signal that the bet is paying off, and what durability-of-advantage test (if a competitor copies the menu) would tell us the concept is durable rather than crowded, versus telling us the differentiation is primarily culinary and therefore vulnerable to positional competition on neighborhood ownership, story, price point, and presence at specific recurring events?

2. **What "sustainable" actually means**: When the goal states "sustainable food truck business," what is the minimum viable definition of sustainability — covering costs month-to-month, paying a livable owner wage, building equity, or something else — and how does the answer change which decisions to make in year one?

3. **The solo-founder operational ceiling**: What if the real bottleneck to reaching a small fleet is not capital or permits but the founder's personal bandwidth — a single founder with no prior business ownership must simultaneously cook, manage inventory, handle bookings, navigate permits, and build a customer base — at what weekly revenue or order volume does this model break down operationally, and what does a path to expansion look like if we design around that constraint from day one rather than treating it as something to hire away later?

4. **Culinary identity as an existing peak-moments asset**: When you have prepared Filipino-Mexican fusion food for others — at family gatherings, pop-ups, or informal settings — what moments made it clear that this cuisine could create something special, what was it about those moments that made the food resonate so deeply with people, and beyond culinary skill, what knowledge, relationships, or experiences from your life and career are already in place that most food truck founders would have to spend years building?

5. **Financial scaffolding that grows with the business**: The $60K in savings and potential SBA loan represent a real foundation — what financial practices (tracking methods, decision rules about reinvestment, criteria for when to hire) would ensure that the first year's hard-won revenue gets channeled into the growth infrastructure that makes year two and three genuinely easier rather than just bigger?

6. **Category invention over category entry**: Imagine this Filipino-Mexican fusion concept didn't exist yet and you were inventing it from scratch today — not as a food truck competing in a 30-truck market, but as an entirely new kind of experience that happens to involve food. What would that look like, and what would it make the truck permit, the commissary kitchen, and the SBA loan mean in that world?

7. **A Tuesday in year one**: Describe what a typical Tuesday looks like for you — from commissary kitchen prep before dawn through breakdown and close-out at night — and at which points in that day does the business feel most fragile, most alive, and most in need of a second set of hands you don't yet have?

8. **The one-truck-forever provocation**: What if the three-year expansion plan — fleet, brick-and-mortar, multi-revenue streams — is not an aspiration but a trap, and the most financially and personally sustainable version of this business is a single truck operated indefinitely at high margins with a small, deeply loyal customer base and no employees? If "staying small on purpose" were the strategy rather than a failure mode, what would be optimized differently from day one, and what does the founder actually want that a growth narrative is currently obscuring? *The real insight: growth plans in food service often reflect cultural assumptions about what success looks like rather than honest modeling of what the founder wants their daily life to feel like at year five — and the business structure you build in year one encodes that answer before you've asked the question.*

## Topic Cluster 02: Unit Economics & Break-Even

**Cluster focus**: Quantifying the food-cost, transaction-volume, and margin-buffer math that determines whether the truck can survive the first year under realistic cost and pricing volatility.

9. **Food cost ratio for a fusion menu**: For the Filipino-Mexican fusion menu (lumpia tacos, adobo burritos, ube horchata), what is the realistic food cost percentage for each item category, and how does the blended menu food cost ratio compare to the industry benchmark range of 28–35% given that Filipino ingredients (ube, adobo components) may have different sourcing profiles than standard Mexican fare — and does the resulting ratio leave enough margin to cover labor, commissary fees, fuel, and overhead at price points that four existing fusion competitors have effectively ceiling-tested?

10. **Break-even architecture at the truck level**: Given estimated fixed costs (truck payment or lease, commissary rental, permits, insurance, fuel, loan servicing if applicable) and variable costs per transaction, at what average ticket size and daily transaction count — and across how many service days per month — does the truck break even, how does that threshold shift under the $60K bootstrap, $100K-with-SBA-loan, and higher-capitalization scenarios, and which assumptions about daily cover count, average ticket, and operating days are most likely to be wrong in the first six months of actual operation?

11. **Break-even as a moving target**: The break-even analysis you calculate today assumes stable food costs, permit fees, and commissary rates — but all three are likely to shift within 12 months — what is the minimum margin buffer that keeps you solvent through a 20% food cost spike or a new annual licensing fee without requiring price increases that lose price-sensitive regulars, and at what food cost increase does the current pricing model become unprofitable without a menu price adjustment?

## Topic Cluster 03: Capital Structure & Financing Decisions

**Cluster focus**: Evaluating the SBA loan, lease-vs-own tradeoff, working-capital reserve, and bootstrap-versus-leverage decisions as risk-distribution choices rather than isolated financing mechanics.

12. **The SBA loan as a leverage amplifier in both directions**: Beyond principal, what is the fully-loaded cost of the $40K SBA microloan (interest rate over the loan term, origination fees, required collateral or personal guarantee exposure), at what monthly net revenue does the debt service become a manageable fraction (under 15%) of gross revenue, how does the loan act as a leverage amplifier in both directions — increasing capacity to invest in equipment, inventory, and marketing while raising the monthly fixed-cost floor so that on a bad month the payment is due regardless — and if the loan is delayed or denied by 6-12 months, which path to profitability remains viable on $60K alone with what minimum launch budget and operating runway before the business must reach cash-flow positive, and does that change whether launching is advisable at all?

13. **Lease versus own versus used-truck purchase as risk distribution**: The key factors in the primary-truck financing decision include monthly cash outflow, maintenance liability, depreciation, flexibility for upgrade, and balance-sheet exposure — what is the 3-year total cost of ownership for a used truck purchased outright from savings versus a new truck financed over five years versus a leased truck (where the core revenue-generating asset can be repossessed if payments slip), which configuration preserves the most working capital in year one when cash flow risk is highest, and which leaves the most options open if year one underperforms?

14. **Working-capital reserve and equipment-cost creep**: Beyond the initial truck and equipment outlay, what is the appropriate working capital reserve — expressed as months of fixed costs — for a solo-founder food truck in its first 12 months, how does that reserve change if commissary fees, permits, and insurance are paid annually versus monthly, and given that used food trucks often require unexpected repairs in year one that can consume 15–20% of initial capital, what contingency reserve is built into the $60K/$100K budget, and if the first major mechanical failure hits, what gets cut first and what specific financial triggers should prompt the founder to pause expansion rather than accelerate it?

15. **The lean startup's minimum viable product lens**: Eric Ries's core insight was that the cost of learning should be minimized before capital is deployed at scale — validated learning is the unit of progress, not features shipped. The SBA microloan question maps directly onto this: borrowing $40K before the first truck has demonstrated its unit economics is a bet on an unvalidated model; borrowing $40K after six months of proof-of-concept data is a bet on a validated one. What specific operational and financial milestones (break-even month, consistent weekly revenue band, catering booking rate) would constitute sufficient validation before drawing on debt, and how does that sequencing affect the loan terms available?

## Topic Cluster 04: Revenue Diversification & Cash Flow

**Cluster focus**: Designing the mix of daily vending, catering, and events so that revenue channels complement rather than cannibalize each other and smooth seasonal volatility.

16. **Revenue-per-hour economics by channel**: Daily vending, catering contracts, and event bookings each carry different revenue-per-hour, food cost, and labor cost profiles — what does the gross margin look like for each channel at realistic volume once labor, travel time, and setup costs are fully loaded, what variables determine whether catering bookings are margin-accretive or margin-dilutive (advance inventory commitment, staff coordination, delayed payment), what minimum per-person catering price justifies a full-day booking over a standard vending day so the founder avoids underpricing contracts that look like revenue but destroy cash flow, and which mix of channels produces the most financially stable monthly cash flow given that daily vending is weather- and foot-traffic-dependent?

17. **The catering–vending cannibalization tension**: Daily vending builds brand visibility that generates catering inquiries, but accepting catering bookings on peak vending days removes the truck from its highest-revenue street locations — how do you design a booking policy that captures catering upside without cannibalizing the brand exposure that made catering possible in the first place?

18. **Seasonal cash-flow swings and the reserve calculation**: A mid-size city food truck likely experiences 30–50% revenue swings between summer peak and winter trough across weather seasons, local event calendars, and school year cycles — what is the minimum cash reserve needed to bridge a 10-week slow season without drawing down capital intended for expansion, what financial instruments or revenue channels (indoor event bookings, catering contracts, pre-sold loyalty programs) could smooth that variance, and how does locking in catering or event contracts in advance change the reserve calculation — or, reframed: could the revenue mix be designed from the start so that the truck's slow seasons are deliberately filled by higher-margin catering work?

19. **Event booking concentration risk**: If event revenue becomes a meaningful share of total revenue, what level of concentration in a single event type or single event organizer creates fragility, and what diversification across event categories (corporate catering, weddings, festivals, farmers markets) reduces single-point-of-failure risk to an acceptable level?

20. **Revenue rhythms that sustain rather than drain**: Given that catering, events, and daily vending each have different energy demands and cash-flow profiles, what combination of revenue streams would let the founder show up creatively every day without burning out — and what structures (booking systems, boundaries, relationships) would make that mix feel effortless rather than exhausting?

21. **The single-revenue-stream provocation**: What if we made it deliberately worse — what if the founder committed, in writing, to never accepting a catering contract, never booking a private event, and only ever vending in a single fixed location on a fixed schedule for the first 18 months? No hedging, no diversification, no "keeping options open." What would this forced constraint reveal about which parts of the revenue diversification strategy are genuine opportunity and which are anxiety dressed up as planning? *The real insight: revenue diversification at an early stage often splits attention before a core identity is established — what is the minimum viable focus that would produce a truck people actually miss when it's not there?*

22. **The winery's dual-revenue calendar**: Wineries in cold climates survive by stacking tasting room revenue (spring/summer/fall) against case sales, wine club subscriptions, and private events (winter), so no single channel carries the whole year. A food truck in a mid-size city faces analogous seasonality — outdoor foot traffic contracts in winter, festivals end, office lunch crowds thin during holidays. What revenue mix (catering contracts, pop-up residencies inside bars or breweries, pre-sold meal kits using the commissary) could buffer seasonal downturns, and what minimum monthly floor does each channel need to cover to keep the business cash-flow positive through its lowest quarter?

## Topic Cluster 05: Regulatory Strategy & Engagement

**Cluster focus**: Treating the pending city ordinance as a dynamic strategic variable — quantifying its impact, hedging against it, engaging the process, and positioning to benefit if regulatory tightening filters weaker competitors.

23. **Ordinance impact, timeline, and location-independent revenue hedge**: The pending ordinances restricting downtown vending hours and adding annual licensing fees represent a quantifiable risk — what is the estimated revenue impact of a downtown hours restriction as a percentage of total location revenue (particularly if highest-revenue downtown slots are cut 30–50%), what is the cost range of new annual licensing fees relative to current permit costs, what is the expected timeline for ordinance passage that should inform go-live planning, and how much of your early revenue should be designed to be location-independent (catering, events, neighborhood routes) specifically as a hedge against regulatory surprise — with what fallback location strategy that doesn't depend on downtown access?

24. **The ordinance as a competitive filter and opportunity**: What if the pending ordinance turns out to be a competitive filter that actually benefits an operator who can navigate it — if the new rules add costs and restrictions, some of the 30 existing trucks will exit the market, and the founder who survives in a stronger cash position may inherit better vending spots and less competition — how would the business strategy differ if we treated the ordinance as an asset rather than a threat, and how do you position early operations so that a regulatory tightening, if it happens, damages you less than it damages less-capitalized competitors?

25. **Engaging versus waiting — advocacy cost-benefit and association leverage**: Why do we assume the right posture toward the city council debate is to monitor and adapt after the fact rather than actively shape the outcome — city officials respond to organized constituency pressure, and the local food truck association is a natural coordination vehicle, but individual operators who participate in advocacy spend time that isn't generating revenue while benefiting from whatever the group wins — how should a solo founder allocate engagement hours between regulatory advocacy and revenue-generating operations, at what point does organizing with other food truck operators create sufficient collective leverage to shift the ordinance outcome, and what is the structural design of an association engagement strategy that makes participation rational for you without becoming a second unpaid job?

26. **The ordinance-gift provocation**: Imagine the opposite were true: the pending city ordinance restrictions — the downtown vending hour cuts, the new licensing fees — are actually the best thing that could happen to this specific business. Work backward. Under what scenario is a regulatory crackdown on food trucks a genuine competitive advantage for a solo founder with a distinctive Filipino-Mexican fusion concept, and what moves would need to be made right now to be positioned to capture that advantage rather than absorb it as damage? *The real insight: incumbents in any market often benefit when regulations raise barriers to entry for new competitors — and a founder who engages the ordinance process proactively may end up shaping the very rules their future competitors must navigate.*

27. **How food carts shaped Portland's street grid**: Portland's food cart pods emerged partly because operators organized collectively, proposed designated pod locations to the city, and framed the ask around economic development and neighborhood activation rather than asking for regulatory exemptions. The pending city ordinance debate is a live process — not a fixed constraint. What would it look like to participate in that process not just defensively (protecting existing vending hours) but proactively (proposing a food truck district framework, co-authoring standards for licensed zones, or presenting unit-economics data that reframes trucks as economic anchors rather than nuisances)?

28. **The regulatory turning point**: Imagine it's six months from now and the city council has passed the new food truck ordinances — whether they're favorable or restrictive. Walk through how a typical vending week would feel differently under each scenario: what would you have wished you had done in the months before the vote, and what relationships would prove to matter most?

## Topic Cluster 06: Commissary Kitchen Infrastructure

**Cluster focus**: Reframing the required commissary relationship from a compliance burden into a strategic asset — while naming dependency risks and geographic-equity implications.

29. **Commissary dependency as a single point of failure**: Requiring a commissary kitchen creates a critical dependency on a third-party relationship — what happens to daily operations if the commissary raises rates, loses its health permit, or terminates the agreement on 30 days' notice, is there a backup commissary already identified, and what would it take to make the commissary relationship strategically generative — a platform for catering capacity, co-branding, or shared equipment access — rather than merely obligatory?

30. **The commissary as strategic asset and geographic-equity variable**: Many founders treat commissary kitchen requirements as a compliance burden — what would it look like to treat the commissary as a strategic asset (a place to build relationships with other food operators, share knowledge about suppliers and regulations, or pilot catering partnerships), and at the same time, from the perspective of customers in neighborhoods that aren't near commissary-adjacent vending zones, what does compliance infrastructure do to geographic equity of access, and is there a version of the expansion plan that deliberately routes toward underserved neighborhoods rather than away from them?

31. **The hub-and-spoke commissary model**: Airline networks solved the problem of serving many destinations from limited hubs by routing everything through high-throughput central nodes. The commissary kitchen requirement is typically seen as overhead, but hub-and-spoke operators in food manufacturing and ghost kitchen networks have turned their central production facility into a strategic asset — a place where sauces are batched, proteins are prepped, and quality is standardized across multiple service points. How could the mandated commissary relationship be designed from the outset as the operational spine of a future fleet rather than a cost of compliance?

## Topic Cluster 07: Customer Experience, Access & Equity

**Cluster focus**: Structuring the vending experience, discovery channels, and menu so that first-time, recurring, economically diverse, dietary-constrained, culturally connected, and mobility-limited customers all find a welcoming path to the truck.

32. **Unfamiliar cuisine and the first-transaction arc**: Filipino-Mexican fusion may be entirely unfamiliar to a large portion of the mid-size city population — what does the experience feel like for someone who has never eaten lumpia or adobo before, what information, framing, or staff behavior helps them feel welcome rather than out of their depth, and since the first visit (order, wait, handoff, first bite) sets the entire relationship, what emotional state do you want that person to leave in and what specific moments most risk producing the opposite (confusion, embarrassment, disappointment) — given that a fast, transactional, high-turnover food truck format may not support the customer education the concept requires?

33. **Economic, dietary, and mobility accessibility**: If the target customer is someone who regularly eats out, what income band does that assume and how does pricing account for neighbors or workers in lower-wage industries who might be nearby but priced out, what does the experience of waiting in a food truck line feel like for someone who is elderly, has a mobility limitation, is managing young children, or is on a strict lunch break — and which friction points could be mitigated through ordering design, shade, seating nearby, or signage without major capital — and what portion of the likely audience has dietary restrictions (vegetarian, halal, gluten-free) such that the absence of alternatives becomes a real barrier to repeat visits or catering bookings from organizations with diverse membership?

34. **Cultural legibility and honoring origins**: For Filipino and Mexican community members who recognize these dishes, how does the menu and brand honor the cultural origins rather than aestheticize them for a non-immigrant audience — what would it feel like to a Filipina grandmother or a Mexican family to see adobo burritos on the menu board, and what would make that feeling pride rather than erasure?

35. **Discovery channels beyond social media**: Someone who wants to try the truck for the first time has to know where it will be, when it will be there, and that it will actually be open — what are the realistic discovery channels for people who are not already active on social media, and how does the announcement strategy reach people who would love this food but would never check Instagram?

36. **The opening-day arc**: Imagine the first person who discovers your lumpia taco truck — they're walking past at lunch, curious about the smell, and they've never heard of Filipino-Mexican fusion. Walk through their entire experience from that first glance to sharing a photo with a friend: what moments along that journey are you designing intentionally, and which ones are you leaving to chance?

37. **The invisible-customer provocation**: What if the food truck deliberately targeted people who would never consciously seek it out — the person who has never heard of Filipino food, who finds "fusion" slightly suspicious, who eats at the same three places every week? What would the menu, the location strategy, and the brand identity look like if conversion of the indifferent was the primary design constraint rather than deepening loyalty among the already-enthusiastic? *The real insight: is the current concept a niche that converts or a niche that consolidates — and which of those is actually the right bet for a mid-size city with a growing but not yet saturated food truck scene?*

38. **The regular's story**: Picture a customer who becomes a loyal weekly visitor six months in — what does that relationship look like, what keeps drawing them back beyond the food itself, and how does your operation need to be structured to make that kind of loyalty feel mutual rather than transactional?

## Topic Cluster 08: Brand, Community & Cultural Identity

**Cluster focus**: Building reputation as a self-amplifying asset, differentiating among similar-concept trucks, partnering with cultural communities, and cultivating the first-fifty relationships that anchor a loyal following.

39. **Reputation as a self-amplifying, compounding asset — and its reverse failure**: Early reviews and social media posts create a self-amplifying pattern where positive word-of-mouth draws larger crowds, larger crowds generate more reviews, and more reviews attract food media attention that draws still more customers — but what is the failure scenario in reverse, how quickly does a bad health inspection or a viral complaint unwind months of accumulated goodwill, and how do you manage reputation as a compounding asset rather than a lagging outcome?

40. **Differentiation in a four-fusion-truck field**: With four trucks already operating similar fusion concepts in the same city, early movers will define the category in customers' minds — how do you differentiate not just in menu but in positioning (neighborhood ownership, story, price point, presence at specific recurring events) so that you are the name customers recall when they think "fusion food truck," rather than one of five interchangeable options?

41. **Cultural events as audience and partnership**: Filipino and Latinx community events are natural audiences for this food — but they also represent communities with existing culinary traditions, vendors, and relationships. What is the difference between showing up at those events as a welcomed collaborator versus an outsider capitalizing on community identity, and what practices would earn the former?

42. **Belonging brand and the first-fifty relationship map**: If this food truck became something customers genuinely felt proud to be associated with — a community touchstone, not just food they like — what would that look like, what stories would customers tell about discovering you, how would those stories spread — and who in the founder's existing network (culinary instructors, neighbors, community members, other small business owners) could become the first fifty loyal customers, catering clients, or informal advisors, and what is one conversation that could happen this week that would turn a connection into a genuine collaborator?

43. **Observing the thriving operators already in the scene**: Among the ~30 food trucks already operating in your city, which operators seem to have built something thriving and sustainable, what do you observe them doing that distinguishes them from trucks that struggle, and what could a respectful conversation with one of them reveal?

44. **The business beyond the truck**: What if the food truck weren't the business at all, but rather a roving proof-of-concept for a cultural brand — where the truck's purpose is to generate belonging and story rather than to serve meals, and revenue flows from that cultural gravity rather than from transactions at a window?

45. **Owned by the people who love it**: What if the path from one truck to a fleet weren't a solo founder scaling up, but instead a community of loyal regulars who co-own the expansion — not through a traditional co-op model, but through some genuinely new form of distributed stake that has no current analog in the restaurant industry?

46. **Filipino-Mexican fusion as a new cultural form**: What if lumpia tacos and adobo burritos weren't fusion food — a genre with decades of precedent — but the seed of an entirely new culinary culture that doesn't yet have a name, a community, or an institutional home? What would the founder need to build (beyond a menu and a truck) to make that culture real, and what would "scaling" even mean in that frame?

## Topic Cluster 09: Expansion Path — Fleet & Brick-and-Mortar

**Cluster focus**: Stress-testing the fleet and brick-and-mortar steps so that growth decisions survive the fixed-cost trap, the system-change-not-system-extension shift, and the default-assumption trap that brick-and-mortar is every food business's endpoint.

47. **Fleet expansion trigger metrics and the fixed-cost trap**: The plan targets growth to a small fleet within three years — what specific financial and operational thresholds (net margin percentage, monthly revenue run rate, operational efficiency metrics, waitlist demand, catering booking rate) should trigger the decision to acquire a second truck, at what minimum monthly net income from truck one sustained over how many months does the economics become defensible, what incremental fixed costs (second permit, commissary slot, hired operator labor) must the model absorb — and because fixed costs don't double linearly at expansion (some scale, some spike, creating a fixed-cost trap), at what revenue threshold does adding a second truck actually improve margins versus just adding complexity, and what criteria prevent the founder from throwing good money after bad by adding a second truck if the first never reaches consistent profitability (say, net margin below 12%) — especially given that a second truck is only one option alongside a ghost kitchen, a licensed product line, or a franchise-style agreement?

48. **Brick-and-mortar as trap versus destination**: Have we interrogated why a brick-and-mortar location is in the three-year plan — is it a genuine business goal, a signal of success for funders or family, or an assumption that "real" restaurants are the endpoint of every food business — and given that restaurant failure rates remain high, capital requirements dwarf food truck costs, and the transition carries build-out, equipment, and lease-deposit outlay on a higher fixed-cost base, what order-of-magnitude investment is required to open a small counter-service or fast-casual space in a mid-size city, what monthly revenue is needed to cover that base, does the truck's brand equity and catering revenue realistically support the transition within three years or does the timeline need to be extended, and is permanence actually the right end state before it becomes a sunk-cost commitment?

49. **Brick-and-mortar as a system change, not a system extension**: Opening a fixed location doesn't just add a revenue stream — it changes the nature of the operation entirely (fixed staff, rent obligation, no mobility to chase demand, year-round cost base regardless of season) — what are the second-order effects on the food truck operation itself when the founder's attention, capital, and energy are split between two operating models, and how would you know you're ready to absorb that complexity?

50. **The three-year dream picture**: Imagine it is three years from now and this venture has become a model that other Filipino-Mexican fusion entrepreneurs are studying. You have a small fleet, a loyal following, and a clear path toward brick-and-mortar. What would a visitor to your city notice first about what you built — and what would regular customers say to their friends about why they keep coming back?

51. **The anti-restaurant provocation**: What if the worst possible outcome for this business is that it succeeds so well it becomes a brick-and-mortar restaurant — and in doing so, destroys the exact thing that made it worth eating at? If permanence is the enemy of the food truck's soul, what would it mean to design a growth strategy that deliberately refuses to settle down? *The real insight: what virtues of impermanence — novelty, mobility, low overhead, street-level intimacy — get quietly sacrificed as the business "matures," and which of those are actually the competitive moat?*

52. **Proof-of-concept before scale**: The early franchise playbook — Ray Kroc didn't build 100 McDonald's locations until the San Bernardino original had proven its unit economics, kitchen choreography, and repeat-customer pull. Given that this business starts with one truck and $100K in capital, what are the minimum proof points (daily revenue threshold, food cost ratio, average transaction size, customer return rate) that should be demonstrably stable before a second truck or catering vertical is layered on — and how does that milestone discipline change the 3-year timeline?

53. **The brick-and-mortar that isn't**: Imagine the three-year goal of a brick-and-mortar location was replaced by something that provides permanence and scale without the fixed overhead — not a ghost kitchen, not a franchise, not a pop-up series, but something categorically different that doesn't yet exist. What would it need to provide emotionally and economically that a physical storefront currently provides, and what would it free the founder from?

54. **The inflection scene**: Fast-forward to the moment you decide to add a second truck or sign a lease on a brick-and-mortar space — what has to be true about the business for that decision to feel like a natural next step rather than a leap of faith, and what does the founder's life look like the week before and the week after that decision is made?
