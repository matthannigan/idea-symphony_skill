# Summary: Unit Economics & Break-Even

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension across this cluster is that the truck's survival depends less on the break-even number calculated today than on the margin buffer held against costs and volume that will not hold still. Building that buffer collides with a price-sensitive market four fusion rivals have already ceiling-tested. Every perspective treats a single static break-even point as fiction. Food cost, permit fees, commissary rent, and daily covers all move within the first year, often together, so the useful output is a break-even range under stress rather than a point estimate.

On food cost, there is firm agreement that the 28–35% industry benchmark is a reference, not an input. The realistic ratio for a Filipino-Mexican menu must be built bottom-up from actual supplier quotes and a test batch, because specialty Filipino ingredients plausibly run 15–25% higher than commodity Mexican fare in a mid-size market with no established ethnic-supplier competition. A single blended ratio is also dangerous on its own. The three product families have different cost drivers, and a shift in sales mix can keep the blend on target while individual items quietly lose money.

On break-even architecture, daily transaction count is named by all four perspectives as both the assumption most likely to be wrong and the dominant risk. Estimates of realistic early volume vary widely, which is the point: the discipline is to model a conservative baseline and survive a drop, not to plan around a target. The SBA loan emerges as a clear directional call. Its added debt service raises the cover-count bar enough that the recommended path is to lease, bootstrap lean for four to six months, prove the volume, and only then take on debt.

On volatility, the cluster's most consequential finding is structural: a new fixed fee with flat volume can be unfixable by pricing. A roughly $2,400 annual licensing fee can demand about 30 additional covers per day that a saturated market may simply not supply, making engagement with the city council before the pending ordinance passes a financial necessity, not a courtesy. The shared defensive posture is to price for an effective 36–38% food cost from day one, predefine repricing triggers and a quarterly cadence, and hold a cash reserve covering one and a half to two months of fixed costs before scaling.

## Key Themes

### Build economics bottom-up from real quotes, then defend the buffer
The benchmark range is treated as a trap by every perspective. The actionable substitute is measured cost per dish from two supplier sources and a test batch, then pricing that bakes in a 4–6 point cushion (effective 36–38% food cost). This prevents a commodity spike from forcing an immediate price increase. The buffer, not the lean current margin, is what keeps the truck solvent through year-one volatility.

### Transaction volume is the fragile variable, so model the trough
Across all questions, optimistic cover-count assumptions are flagged as the most common failure mode. The recurring instruction is to plan break-even at a conservative baseline, validate ticket size and add-on rates in a real soft launch, and ensure the lowest-expected month is not cash-flow negative. Seasonal swings of 30–50% are normal in a mid-size city.

### A single blended ratio hides cross-subsidy traps
Modeling the menu as one number obscures item-level losses. The repeated control is per-item minimum margins plus weekly sales-mix monitoring. This catches a drift toward lower-margin items before it compounds. Drinks at high margin can subsidize lower-margin wraps, but only if the actual mix is tracked rather than assumed.

### Capital structure changes the model, not just the cash cushion
The $60K bootstrap and $100K-with-SBA scenarios are framed as fundamentally different fixed-cost profiles. Debt service shifts the break-even cover count up by several transactions per day. The convergent recommendation is to lease and bootstrap first, treating real operating data as the precondition for taking on debt.

### Regulatory cost shifts can be structurally unfixable
A standout reframe: not every cost increase can be priced away. When a new fixed fee lands while volume is flat, the extra covers needed to cover it may exceed what the market can supply, leaving no pricing or cost-cutting remedy. This converts the pending ordinance from a background risk into a first-order reason to engage the city council before it passes.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Cost the three highest-volume items (lumpia tacos, adobo burritos, ube horchata) from two supplier quotes each (bulk distributor and specialty importer) and run a one-week test batch to fix actual cost per dish.
- Set per-item minimum food-cost margins, not just a blended target, and design a five-item core executable at roughly 32% plus higher-priced specials.
- Build the break-even model as a range under stress (food cost to ~38%, commissary +25%, a new licensing fee), and add ~20% to the fixed-cost floor to cover repairs, reinspections, and registration.
- Engage the local food truck association and city council now to learn the exact proposed fee and vending-hour terms, and quantify the additional daily covers any new fee would require.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Run a soft launch at real pricing to observe actual daily covers and average ticket, then rebuild the twelve-month model on observed figures rather than projections.
- Price the menu 5–7% above the calculated break-even ticket (price for ~36–38% food cost) to create a 4–6 point shock absorber, and pre-identify two alternative suppliers per specialty ingredient.
- Lease the truck and bootstrap lean on the $60K, deferring the SBA loan until the truck demonstrates 70+ daily covers at $14+ average ticket.
- Stand up predefined repricing triggers and a quarterly (day 30/60/90) COGS review with a customer-communication script, plus a firm non-negotiable pricing floor.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Build a $15K–$20K cash reserve (covering 1.5–2 months of fixed costs) before scaling to a second truck, and treat any drop below ~$10K as a stop-growth signal.
- Negotiate longer-term pricing contracts on the top 3–4 high-volume ingredients and shift to volume sourcing and batch prep as covers reach 150+, capturing the move from ~35–38% toward 28–30% food cost as a deliberate margin win.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- Bottom-up sourcing (bulk ethnic suppliers, direct ordering, in-house spice/sauce prep) can move food cost meaningfully below benchmark as volume grows.
- High-margin drinks (ube horchata) can cross-subsidize lower-margin wraps if the sales mix is actively managed.
- Bootstrapping lean and proving the model before taking debt lowers the break-even bar in the riskiest early months.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- Overestimated daily cover count is the single most common failure mode; seasonal swings of 30–50% can push a thin month cash-flow negative.
- A blended food-cost target can mask individual items that lose money once the actual buying mix diverges from forecast.
- The fixed-cost floor is likely 15–25% understated once repairs, reinspections, surcharges, and sustaining marketing are included.
- A new fixed licensing fee with flat volume may be structurally unprofitable, with no pricing or cost-cutting remedy available.

**Trade-offs**:
- Margin buffer vs. price sensitivity: pricing for a 36–38% cost cushion protects against shocks but risks losing regulars in a market four rivals have ceiling-tested.
- SBA loan vs. bootstrap: more working capital raises the break-even cover count and tightens margin, while leasing and bootstrapping limit reach but de-risk the first year.
- Raising prices vs. cutting portions vs. holding: each defends margin during a cost spike but trades against customer satisfaction, volume, or solvency.
- Building the buffer through ruthless sourcing (27–28% target cost) vs. through higher menu prices: sourcing protects volume but demands supplier scale the truck may not have early.
