# Summary: Customer Experience, Access & Equity

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension of this cluster is who the truck is actually priced for. A fusion item at $12-14 reads to a nearby worker making $15-16 an hour as a closed door, and the perspectives do not resolve how to handle that. One position argues you cannot serve the lower-wage and middle-wage bands at once and should own a single price position explicitly to prevent sticker shock for everyone. Another argues for a genuinely affordable tier—a $5-6 single, an $8 rice plate—carried alongside the fusion menu so price signals inclusion rather than exclusion. Whichever way the founder resolves this, the choice cascades into menu design, location, and brand identity, so it is the first decision to make consciously rather than by default.

Beneath that tension, the perspectives converge strongly on a single diagnosis: for an unfamiliar cuisine, the binding constraint on the first visit is social risk, not flavor. The dangerous moment is a customer's fear of ordering wrong, looking ignorant, or holding up the line. Almost every recommendation flows from lowering that risk. A menu board that describes ingredients in plain language and is legible from fifteen feet, a trained staff hook that anchors lumpia to "an egg roll" and explicitly welcomes beginners, a slightly smaller and generously flavored first bite engineered to feel like a manageable success, and cheap physical fixes (shade, a bench, a pre-order pickup lane) all keep the line from excluding the elderly, caregivers, and the mobility-limited.

The cluster also reframes the question of who to target. Rather than choosing between deepening loyalty among fusion enthusiasts and converting skeptics, the strongest synthesis treats the choice as empirical. Lead the menu with one genuinely familiar hero item, locate near where skeptics already are so habit and proximity do the conversion work, and then audit who actually shows up in month one. If only fusion-seekers appear, that is a positioning problem to fix by month three, not evidence the concept is too niche.

Two themes carry unusual weight for a startup. First, cultural integrity is load-bearing and cannot be retrofitted. Community members from both cultures should shape the menu and naming before launch, dishes should be named honestly rather than as gimmicks, and the food itself must taste rooted in tradition. Second, the warmth that drives loyalty must be built as a system, not left to individuals, because food trucks turn over staff and founders step back during scaling. Recognition, reliability, and explicit loyalty terms are what convert a six-month regular from a creature of habit into an advocate and catering door-opener.

## Key Themes

### Social risk is the real barrier to an unfamiliar cuisine
Across every first-visit question, the personas converge that the threat is embarrassment, not taste. A customer afraid of ordering wrong freezes at a board of unfamiliar names, and a high-turnover transactional format gives them no graceful exit. The central design move is to engineer permission to be a beginner—plain-language descriptions, a familiar reference point, and staff who read discomfort and redirect rather than just processing people through.

### Accessibility wins are cheap, and most are being left on the table
Shade, a bench, scouted nearby seating, a multilingual handout, and a text/QR pre-order pickup lane each cost between nothing and a few hundred dollars. Each converts a barrier—the line, the standing wait, the cognitive load of an unfamiliar format—into a loyalty lever. The current implicit model excludes the elderly, caregivers, and mobility-limited customers by default. Small deliberate choices reverse that.

### Cultural integrity must be built in before launch, not retrofitted
Honoring the source cuisines is treated as non-negotiable and time-sensitive. Bring Filipino and Mexican community members into menu and naming decisions as collaborators with real authority. Name dishes seriously rather than as gimmicks, ground the flavor in actual tradition, and be transparent about the founder's own relationship to the cuisines. These choices shape the business from day one and generate diaspora word-of-mouth that marketing cannot buy.

### Discovery is a routine and trust problem, not a posting problem
Reaching people who never check Instagram depends on reliability and physical presence. A fixed posted schedule people discover by walking past, printed flyers in libraries and bodegas and laundromats, anchor-institution and workplace partnerships, in-person event presence, and remarkable-enough experiences so satisfied customers carry the word. A truck in the identical spot goes invisible after two weeks, so reliable anchor days need periodic fresh announcements layered on top.

### Loyalty must be systematized to survive turnover
Recognition drives loyalty more than discounts, but the warm relationship will not survive staff turnover or the founder stepping back during scaling unless it is captured as a system. Documented name-and-order notes, a public reliable schedule, stable pricing, and explicitly named loyalty terms all matter. Built this way, regulars become evangelists and the single highest-leverage source of catering bookings.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Decide and commit to a pricing posture before launch: either own one explicit price position or design a genuinely affordable tier (a $5-6 single, an $8 rice plate) alongside the fusion items, and make the chosen number visible on the board from ten feet.
- Convene 3-4 Filipino and 3-4 Mexican community members (roughly $50 in food) to taste the menu and answer directly whether the names and framing feel like celebration or appropriation; change the menu if the feedback is real.
- Write and rehearse a 20-30 second staff hook that anchors each unfamiliar item to a familiar reference point and welcomes beginners; run role-plays before opening.
- Rebuild the menu board to lead with functional descriptions and one visually large hero item with a photo; test it on ten unfamiliar people until they order without asking what it is.
- Add at least one full-strength vegetarian anchor per category and clarify meat/halal sourcing on signage.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Stand up the cheap accessibility kit: a pop-up canopy, a few visible seats, scouted nearby benches, a multilingual "what to expect" handout, and a text/QR pre-order pickup lane.
- Establish a fixed posted weekly schedule (public Google Calendar plus printed flyers, 500 copies for ~$40 updated monthly) and commit to being on location even on slow days.
- Build relationships with 5-10 workplaces and anchor institutions near vending zones for proximity placement, lunch specials, and internal-newsletter mentions.
- Audit month-one customers: if you are reaching only fusion-seekers, adjust location, signage, and a familiar-first hero item to test skeptic conversion by month three.
- Document a name-and-order recognition system and explicit loyalty terms (punch card, order-ready-on-arrival, "you choose the next special") so the relationship survives staff turnover.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Route established regulars into catering leads, since one event pays for dozens of truck days and outlasts individual employment turnover; ask diverse organizations directly about mobility and dietary needs so the catering format includes everyone.
- Maintain a stable core menu with a rotating special every two months developed with regular feedback, keeping loyalty fresh without sacrificing the comfort of a known order.
- Track skeptic-conversion metrics distinct from foodie metrics (repeat-customer rate, office-location dollar volume, conservative-corporate catering) so slow, quiet progress doesn't tempt a premature pivot back to chasing enthusiasts.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- Cheap, high-impact accessibility and seating fixes that competitors are likely ignoring. The line turns from a barrier into a loyalty lever.
- Diaspora word-of-mouth from genuinely honoring both cuisines and hiring from both communities. This builds faster than paid marketing.
- Regulars as a near-free catering pipeline and the most credible discovery channel the truck has.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- Cultural exoticization or gimmick naming that produces negative word-of-mouth no marketing can overcome.
- Dietary invisibility (only meat options) that quietly kills $300-500 catering bookings from diverse organizations.
- Loyalty that lives only in the founder's head and dies when staff turn over.
- Skeptic-conversion being slow and invisible, tempting a premature pivot back to the easier-to-please enthusiast audience.

**Trade-offs**:
- Pricing for one income band versus spanning them: a single explicit position is clear and prevents sticker shock but excludes lower-wage neighbors; a split tier is inclusive but risks pleasing neither band cleanly. This is the cluster's unresolved core tension.
- Conversion of skeptics versus consolidation among enthusiasts: one view treats these as two different businesses that fail if pursued together; another treats them as mutually reinforcing through word-of-mouth. The proposed resolution is to test empirically rather than decide a priori.
- Customer education versus throughput: the staff hooks and curated handoffs that welcome first-timers add time per order and can collapse lunch-rush throughput, pushing the education burden onto the menu board and pre-seeded visual descriptors instead.
- Menu stability versus novelty for regulars: an unchanging menu goes stale, while constant change removes the comfort of knowing what to order; a stable core plus a rotating special threads the needle.
