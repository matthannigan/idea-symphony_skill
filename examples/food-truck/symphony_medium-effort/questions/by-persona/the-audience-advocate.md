# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Food Truck Startup and Expansion Strategy

---

## Who Actually Shows Up — and Who Gets Left Out

1. **The habituals vs. the curious**: The food truck scene draws regulars and impulse visitors — but Filipino-Mexican fusion may be entirely unfamiliar to a large portion of the mid-size city population. What does the experience feel like for someone who has never eaten lumpia or adobo before, and what information, framing, or staff behavior would help them feel welcome rather than out of their depth?

2. **Economic accessibility of the menu**: If the target customer is someone who regularly eats out, what income band does that assume, and how does the pricing strategy account for neighbors or workers in lower-wage industries who might be nearby but priced out — especially if the truck vends in downtown or near business districts?

3. **Language and cultural legibility**: For Filipino and Mexican community members who recognize these dishes, how does the menu and brand honor the cultural origins rather than aestheticize them for a non-immigrant audience? What would it feel like to a Filipina grandmother or a Mexican family to see adobo burritos on the menu board — and what would make that feeling pride rather than erasure?

---

## The Vending Experience as a Lived Journey

4. **The line as a barrier**: What does the experience of waiting in a food truck line feel like for someone who is elderly, has a mobility limitation, is managing young children, or is on a strict lunch break — and which of those friction points could be mitigated through ordering design, shade, seating nearby, or signage without requiring major capital?

5. **Finding the truck before you find the food**: Someone who wants to try the truck for the first time has to know where it will be, when it will be there, and that it will actually be open. What are the realistic discovery channels for people who are not already active on social media, and how does the announcement strategy reach people who would love this food but would never check Instagram?

6. **The first transaction as the whole story**: For a first-time customer, the first visit — the moment they order, the wait, the handoff, the first bite — sets the entire relationship. What emotional state do you want that person to leave in, and what specific moments in that transaction most risk producing the opposite — confusion, embarrassment, disappointment?

---

## Catering and Events: Whose Celebrations Get Served

7. **The catering clientele assumption**: Catering and event bookings often skew toward corporate clients, weddings, and private parties — customers with planning budgets and existing vendor relationships. What would it take to make catering accessible to community organizations, neighborhood associations, school fundraisers, or cultural festivals that want something beyond standard fare but have limited budgets?

8. **Cultural events as both audience and partnership**: Filipino and Latinx community events are natural audiences for this food — but they also represent communities with existing culinary traditions, vendors, and relationships. What is the difference between showing up at those events as a welcomed collaborator versus an outsider capitalizing on community identity, and what practices would earn the former?

9. **Dietary access across the menu**: The fusion concept centers on meat-heavy dishes (lumpia, adobo). What portion of the likely audience has dietary restrictions — vegetarian, halal, gluten-free — and at what point does the absence of alternatives become a real barrier to repeat visits or catering bookings from organizations with diverse membership?

---

## Regulatory Changes and Who Bears the Costs

10. **Downtown hour restrictions fall unevenly**: If the pending ordinance restricts downtown vending hours, the impact falls differently on different customers — workers who can only grab lunch near their office, people without cars who rely on downtown pedestrian access, and evening customers in the downtown dining corridor. Whose access to this food gets cut off if the business pivots to suburban or event-only vending, and is that a loss worth naming?

11. **The commissary kitchen as invisible infrastructure**: Commissary kitchen requirements are regulatory facts, but they also shape where the truck can operate and how early it can launch each day. From the perspective of customers in neighborhoods that aren't near commissary-adjacent vending zones, what does compliance infrastructure do to geographic equity of access — and is there a version of the expansion plan that deliberately routes toward underserved neighborhoods rather than away from them?

---

## Growth and What It Means for the People Being Served

12. **Scaling without drifting**: Food trucks often change character — menu, pricing, vibe — as they grow into fleets or brick-and-mortar. From the perspective of the early customers who found this truck when it was new, what would feel like a betrayal of the original promise, and what would feel like a natural evolution they'd celebrate?

13. **A brick-and-mortar changes who can come**: A fixed location introduces a different access equation than a mobile truck — a specific neighborhood, fixed hours, a different price point expectation, possibly a more formal atmosphere. Which current or potential customers would a brick-and-mortar location serve better, and which ones — particularly those who encounter the truck at their workplace, their neighborhood, their event — would effectively lose access?
