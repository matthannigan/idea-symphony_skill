# Brainstorming Questions from The Provocateur: Food Truck Startup and Expansion Strategy

---

## What If You Burned the Truck Down (Metaphorically)

1. **The anti-restaurant provocation**: What if the worst possible outcome for this business is that it succeeds so well it becomes a brick-and-mortar restaurant — and in doing so, destroys the exact thing that made it worth eating at? If permanence is the enemy of the food truck's soul, what would it mean to design a growth strategy that deliberately refuses to settle down? *The real insight: what virtues of impermanence — novelty, mobility, low overhead, street-level intimacy — get quietly sacrificed as the business "matures," and which of those are actually the competitive moat?*

2. **The wrong-problem provocation**: What if the SBA loan question, the pricing spreadsheets, and the permit negotiations are all distractions from the only thing that actually determines whether this business survives year three — and that thing has nothing to do with money or regulations? Imagine the opposite were true: what if financial discipline and legal compliance are necessary but entirely insufficient conditions, and the singular make-or-break variable is something no one in the plan has named yet? *The real insight: what is the irreplaceable, non-replicable core of this particular business that four other fusion trucks in the same city cannot copy, and is that core protected or accidentally exposed by the current strategy?*

## Inverting the Customer Relationship

3. **The invisible customer provocation**: What if the food truck deliberately targeted people who would never consciously seek it out — the person who has never heard of Filipino food, who finds "fusion" slightly suspicious, who eats at the same three places every week? What would the menu, the location strategy, and the brand identity look like if conversion of the indifferent was the primary design constraint rather than deepening loyalty among the already-enthusiastic? *The real insight: is the current concept a niche that converts or a niche that consolidates — and which of those is actually the right bet for a mid-size city with a growing but not yet saturated food truck scene?*

4. **The life-or-death stakes provocation**: Here's something absurd, but stay with me — what if this food truck had to operate in conditions where every decision had life-or-death stakes for the community it served? Not metaphorically: actual food security, actual cultural lifeline, actual crisis infrastructure. What decisions would change? What would you refuse to compromise on, and what "strategic" choices would suddenly seem frivolous? *The real insight: which parts of the current business model are genuinely load-bearing for the community and which are load-bearing only for the P&L — and do those overlap as much as the founder assumes?*

## Making the Strategy Deliberately Worse

5. **The single revenue stream provocation**: What if we made it deliberately worse — what if the founder committed, in writing, to never accepting a catering contract, never booking a private event, and only ever vending in a single fixed location on a fixed schedule for the first 18 months? No hedging, no diversification, no "keeping options open." What would this forced constraint reveal about which parts of the revenue diversification strategy are genuine opportunity and which are anxiety dressed up as planning? *The real insight: revenue diversification at an early stage often splits attention before a core identity is established — what is the minimum viable focus that would produce a truck people actually miss when it's not there?*

## Regulatory Reality Inversion

6. **The ordinance gift provocation**: Imagine the opposite were true: the pending city ordinance restrictions — the downtown vending hour cuts, the new licensing fees — are actually the best thing that could happen to this specific business. Work backward. Under what scenario is a regulatory crackdown on food trucks a genuine competitive advantage for a solo founder with a distinctive Filipino-Mexican fusion concept, and what moves would need to be made right now to be positioned to capture that advantage rather than absorb it as damage? *The real insight: incumbents in any market often benefit when regulations raise barriers to entry for new competitors — and a founder who engages the ordinance process proactively may end up shaping the very rules their future competitors must navigate.*

## The Fleet Assumption

7. **The one-truck-forever provocation**: What if the three-year expansion plan — fleet, brick-and-mortar, multi-revenue streams — is not an aspiration but a trap, and the most financially and personally sustainable version of this business is a single truck operated indefinitely at high margins with a small, deeply loyal customer base and no employees? If "staying small on purpose" were the strategy rather than a failure mode, what would be optimized differently from day one, and what does the founder actually want that a growth narrative is currently obscuring? *The real insight: growth plans in food service often reflect cultural assumptions about what success looks like rather than honest modeling of what the founder wants their daily life to feel like at year five — and the business structure you build in year one encodes that answer before you've asked the question.*
