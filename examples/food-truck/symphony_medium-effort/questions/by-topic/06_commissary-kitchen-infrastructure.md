# Topic Cluster 06: Commissary Kitchen Infrastructure

## Questions

29. **Commissary dependency as a single point of failure**: Requiring a commissary kitchen creates a critical dependency on a third-party relationship — what happens to daily operations if the commissary raises rates, loses its health permit, or terminates the agreement on 30 days' notice, is there a backup commissary already identified, and what would it take to make the commissary relationship strategically generative — a platform for catering capacity, co-branding, or shared equipment access — rather than merely obligatory?
30. **The commissary as strategic asset and geographic-equity variable**: Many founders treat commissary kitchen requirements as a compliance burden — what would it look like to treat the commissary as a strategic asset (a place to build relationships with other food operators, share knowledge about suppliers and regulations, or pilot catering partnerships), and at the same time, from the perspective of customers in neighborhoods that aren't near commissary-adjacent vending zones, what does compliance infrastructure do to geographic equity of access, and is there a version of the expansion plan that deliberately routes toward underserved neighborhoods rather than away from them?
31. **The hub-and-spoke commissary model**: Airline networks solved the problem of serving many destinations from limited hubs by routing everything through high-throughput central nodes. The commissary kitchen requirement is typically seen as overhead, but hub-and-spoke operators in food manufacturing and ghost kitchen networks have turned their central production facility into a strategic asset — a place where sauces are batched, proteins are prepped, and quality is standardized across multiple service points. How could the mandated commissary relationship be designed from the outset as the operational spine of a future fleet rather than a cost of compliance?
