# Topic Cluster 04: Revenue Diversification & Cash Flow

## Questions

16. **Revenue-per-hour economics by channel**: Daily vending, catering contracts, and event bookings each carry different revenue-per-hour, food cost, and labor cost profiles — what does the gross margin look like for each channel at realistic volume once labor, travel time, and setup costs are fully loaded, what variables determine whether catering bookings are margin-accretive or margin-dilutive (advance inventory commitment, staff coordination, delayed payment), what minimum per-person catering price justifies a full-day booking over a standard vending day so the founder avoids underpricing contracts that look like revenue but destroy cash flow, and which mix of channels produces the most financially stable monthly cash flow given that daily vending is weather- and foot-traffic-dependent?
17. **The catering–vending cannibalization tension**: Daily vending builds brand visibility that generates catering inquiries, but accepting catering bookings on peak vending days removes the truck from its highest-revenue street locations — how do you design a booking policy that captures catering upside without cannibalizing the brand exposure that made catering possible in the first place?
18. **Seasonal cash-flow swings and the reserve calculation**: A mid-size city food truck likely experiences 30–50% revenue swings between summer peak and winter trough across weather seasons, local event calendars, and school year cycles — what is the minimum cash reserve needed to bridge a 10-week slow season without drawing down capital intended for expansion, what financial instruments or revenue channels (indoor event bookings, catering contracts, pre-sold loyalty programs) could smooth that variance, and how does locking in catering or event contracts in advance change the reserve calculation — or, reframed: could the revenue mix be designed from the start so that the truck's slow seasons are deliberately filled by higher-margin catering work?
19. **Event booking concentration risk**: If event revenue becomes a meaningful share of total revenue, what level of concentration in a single event type or single event organizer creates fragility, and what diversification across event categories (corporate catering, weddings, festivals, farmers markets) reduces single-point-of-failure risk to an acceptable level?
20. **Revenue rhythms that sustain rather than drain**: Given that catering, events, and daily vending each have different energy demands and cash-flow profiles, what combination of revenue streams would let the founder show up creatively every day without burning out — and what structures (booking systems, boundaries, relationships) would make that mix feel effortless rather than exhausting?
21. **The single-revenue-stream provocation**: What if we made it deliberately worse — what if the founder committed, in writing, to never accepting a catering contract, never booking a private event, and only ever vending in a single fixed location on a fixed schedule for the first 18 months? No hedging, no diversification, no "keeping options open." What would this forced constraint reveal about which parts of the revenue diversification strategy are genuine opportunity and which are anxiety dressed up as planning? *The real insight: revenue diversification at an early stage often splits attention before a core identity is established — what is the minimum viable focus that would produce a truck people actually miss when it's not there?*
22. **The winery's dual-revenue calendar**: Wineries in cold climates survive by stacking tasting room revenue (spring/summer/fall) against case sales, wine club subscriptions, and private events (winter), so no single channel carries the whole year. A food truck in a mid-size city faces analogous seasonality — outdoor foot traffic contracts in winter, festivals end, office lunch crowds thin during holidays. What revenue mix (catering contracts, pop-up residencies inside bars or breweries, pre-sold meal kits using the commissary) could buffer seasonal downturns, and what minimum monthly floor does each channel need to cover to keep the business cash-flow positive through its lowest quarter?
