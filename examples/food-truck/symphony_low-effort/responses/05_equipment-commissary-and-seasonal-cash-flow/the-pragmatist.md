# Equipment, Commissary, and Seasonal Cash Flow - The Pragmatist

## Lease Vs. Purchase and the Capacity Trap

* **The break-even math favors purchasing — but only after Year 2.** A $45K used truck financed over 5 years at 8% runs roughly $900/month in debt service. A lease at $2,000/month costs $24K/year with nothing to show at the end. The crossover is roughly $48K in cumulative payments, which hits around month 24. Before that point, leasing preserves cash and exit flexibility. A practical approach: lease for the first 12–18 months while you validate the concept, then finance a purchase when revenue data makes the loan application stronger and you know the specific truck you want.

* **The 30% revenue shortfall test reveals a survivability gap on the ownership path.** If monthly revenue drops from a target $25K to $17.5K, fixed debt service doesn't move. The leasing path loses $2K/month in unavoidable payments; the ownership path loses roughly $900/month in debt service — but also has no exit: you can't return a truck you own. A practical modification is to structure the purchase with a 6-month balloon deferral or a longer amortization (7 years) to lower the monthly floor. That gives the ownership path a lower fixed burden during the trough without forfeiting the long-term equity benefit.

* **Truck #2 economics change dramatically depending on how the first truck was financed.** If you own truck #1 outright (or nearly), you can pledge it as partial collateral on truck #2's financing, lowering the interest rate. If you're leasing, a second lease adds a full fixed cost with no offsetting asset. A more accessible starting point for fleet growth is the hybrid: own the primary truck, lease a second as a trial before committing. This tests the operational capacity to run two trucks without locking up twice the capital.

* **The capacity trap is really a scheduling trap.** Two trucks need two operators, two commissary time slots, and twice the permit coverage — none of which scale automatically with the second lease payment. Before signing truck #2, identify the commissary scheduling ceiling (see Question 2) and whether a second employee is already trained. If not, the capital decision is premature regardless of which financing path looks cheaper.

* **Minimum viable fleet test: a trailer or cart first.** Before committing to a second full truck ($30–60K), consider a permitted catering trailer or event cart at $8–15K. It handles private events and festivals without requiring its own commissary slot or vending permit, and it generates data on whether demand actually supports fleet expansion. If it does, the second full truck decision is much easier to justify.

## Commissary as Fixed Cost, Shared-Resource Ceiling, and Community Ecosystem

* **Model the commissary fee as a compliance tax, not a production cost, and budget accordingly.** At $400–800/month for a shared commissary in a mid-size city, it's unavoidable and non-negotiable — it doesn't scale with how many tacos you sell. The practical implication: commissary cost belongs in your fixed overhead calculation, not your variable cost per unit. Once it's modeled correctly, the question shifts from "how do I reduce it" to "how do I maximize what I extract from the hours I'm already paying for."

* **Scheduling conflicts are a real ceiling — get the contract details before signing.** Some commissaries operate on a first-come, first-served reservation model; others sell dedicated time blocks. Catering and event work typically requires early morning prep (4–7 AM) in addition to daily prep. If two other tenants hold those slots and there's no upgrade path to a dedicated block, your catering ceiling is effectively set by the commissary schedule, not by demand. Negotiate for first-right-of-refusal on additional time blocks, or identify a second commissary as backup for overflow events.

* **The people at the commissary are a low-cost distribution channel.** Other tenants are operators who know which event promoters pay on time, which vending spots are newly available, and which catering clients are worth pursuing. A practical approach: show up in person for prep, not just to cook and leave. The informal network inside a shared commissary can surface opportunities faster than any marketing spend.

* **Rate increase risk is real and undermodeled in most food truck business plans.** Commissary operators face the same cost pressures as everyone else, and annual rate increases of 10–20% are common in high-demand markets. Build a 15% annual commissary cost escalator into your 3-year model. If you're projecting on flat commissary costs and rates jump, your break-even analysis is wrong. A practical hedge: ask for a 2-year rate-lock clause in the commissary contract at signing — many will agree to this for reliable tenants.

* **Long-term, commissary dependency is a liability for a multi-truck operation.** At $600/month per truck and three trucks, you're spending $21,600/year on commissary access with no equity. A practical pathway to consider at the 3-year mark: investigate shared commercial kitchen co-ops or subleasing space in a restaurant's off-hours kitchen. These options require relationships built over time — another reason to invest in the commissary community now rather than treating it as a transactional cost.

## Seasonal 12-Month Cash Flow and Reserve Sizing

* **Model the revenue curve explicitly before launch, not after the first winter.** In a cold-climate mid-size city, a reasonable working assumption is: peak months (June–August) at 100% of target revenue, shoulder months (April–May, September–October) at 65–75%, and trough months (November–March) at 30–45%. At a $25K/month target, that's roughly $7,500–11,000/month in winter — against fixed costs that don't compress. Build this curve in a spreadsheet before you spend the first dollar of your $60K.

* **Identify the specific fixed obligations that persist through the trough.** The non-negotiable monthly floor in a down month includes: loan/lease payment ($900–2,000), commissary ($500–700), commercial auto + general liability insurance ($300–500), fuel and maintenance baseline ($200–400), and any employees on reduced but not zero hours ($1,500–3,000). That floor is roughly $3,400–6,600/month before any food cost. A practical approach: calculate the exact monthly floor for your specific contracts and build the reserve target around that number, not a generic rule of thumb.

* **Three months of operating reserves is the minimum; four is safer given your profile.** As a solo founder without a co-founder's salary backstop, a single bad month has no cushion from a second income. At a $5,000/month fixed floor, four months of reserves is $20,000. After equipment ($30–40K for a used truck), permits and licensing ($2–5K), initial food inventory and supplies ($3–5K), and marketing/launch costs ($2–3K), your $60K arrives at roughly $10–20K in remaining cash. That's a tight reserve — which makes the $40K SBA microloan less optional than it might appear.

* **Seasonal revenue smoothing via catering and events is the most actionable lever.** A single corporate catering contract at $3,000–5,000/month fills roughly half the winter revenue gap. The practical approach: spend the summer months — when daily revenue is strong and cash is flowing — actively pitching corporate lunch programs, holiday party catering slots, and indoor event bookings for Q4 and Q1. Don't wait until November to start that sales effort; the best catering clients book 2–3 months out.

* **Reserve sizing and the SBA decision are linked — treat them as one question.** If you bootstrap without the SBA loan and your reserve falls below two months of fixed costs, you're one slow week away from a cash crisis. The SBA microloan at $40K, even at 6–8% interest, adds roughly $400–450/month in debt service — but it also means you can maintain a $20K reserve buffer while fully funding launch costs. A practical framing: the loan doesn't fund the truck; it funds the survival margin. Presented that way, the risk calculus shifts.
