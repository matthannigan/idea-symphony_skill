# Capital Structure and the SBA Decision - The Pragmatist

## SBA Debt Service, Strings, and What the Loan Really Buys

* **Model the monthly obligation before you sign.** A $40K microloan at 10% over 6 years runs roughly $740/month. At a 65% food cost plus commissary and fuel, you need approximately $185K in annual gross revenue to cover debt service alone — about $506/day on a 365-day calendar or $630/day on a realistic 295 operating days. Know that number cold before you accept the funds.

* **The use-of-proceeds list tells you exactly what the loan is for.** Build a simple two-column table: every expenditure you're planning, and whether it survives if the loan disappears. What's left in the "survives" column is the business you can actually launch. What's in the "loan-dependent" column is a checklist of decisions you haven't fully made yet — it's not a shopping list, it's a list of deferred problems.

* **Soft months and debt service interact in a specific, predictable way.** If revenue in months 3–6 runs 30–40% below projection, the math forces one of three moves: draw down your cash reserve, take lower-margin catering gigs to fill the gap, or skip slow vending days to cut food waste. The first erodes runway, the second risks brand dilution, the third reduces visibility during the period you most need it. A practical approach: model the "soft launch" scenario explicitly and decide in advance which lever you'll pull — then the decision isn't made under stress.

* **SBA reporting obligations are real but manageable if you build for them from day one.** Lenders typically require quarterly financials and receipts matching approved uses. That's not burdensome if you have a bookkeeper or use a tool like Wave from the start. The risk isn't the reporting — it's starting with a shoe-box system and trying to reconstruct records retroactively.

## Working Capital Reserve and Minimum Self-Funding Runway

* **Estimate your cash burn before revenue with actual line items.** A practical starting budget: commissary ($600–900/month), food inventory for opening stock ($2,500–4,000), fuel ($300–500/month), permit and licensing fees ($1,500–3,000 one-time), liability insurance ($150–250/month), and payment processing setup. Total pre-revenue outlay before you serve your first customer is likely $6,000–10,000 on top of the truck itself. Know that number, not a round estimate.

* **A 90-day cash operating buffer is a minimum, not a target.** At $3,000–4,500/month in fixed operating costs, that's $9,000–13,500 kept liquid and untouched. If you're using the SBA loan for equipment and build-out, the $60K savings needs to absorb this buffer plus any gap between loan approval and disbursement. A practical approach: treat the buffer as a separate bank account you do not draw from for equipment purchases.

* **Self-funding timeline should be anchored to a real revenue model, not optimism.** If break-even requires $500/day in gross sales and you're realistically doing 3–4 vending days per week at first, week-over-week self-sufficiency probably takes 4–6 months. Plan for 6. If you hit it in 4, you have a cushion. If you plan for 4 and it takes 6, you're making bad decisions from a position of financial stress.

## What If You Ran the Business as If the SBA Loan Will Never Arrive?

* **Force every decision through a $60K-only filter.** A used truck in serviceable condition can be found for $20,000–35,000. That leaves $25,000–40,000 for everything else. At that scale, a short menu of 6–8 items isn't a compromise — it's an operational advantage. Fewer SKUs mean lower inventory complexity, less waste, and faster service times. The constraint surfaces a better product design.

* **The staffing model is where the loan was doing the most hiding.** At $100K startup, hiring even a part-time helper from day one is plausible. At $60K, you're a solo operator for the first 6–12 months. That forces you to design your workflow, your service window, and your menu for one-person execution. That discipline — knowing every station intimately before adding labor — is actually a competitive advantage most founders skip because they funded their way around it.

* **Equipment leasing unlocks the $60K scenario.** A full kitchen buildout on a used truck can run $15,000–40,000. Leasing key equipment (refrigeration, fryer, griddle) instead of buying shifts that to $500–900/month and keeps more cash liquid. The loan doesn't make the business viable — leasing does. The loan question becomes secondary once you've designed a capital-efficient equipment model.

* **Identify the one thing that actually requires the loan.** After running the $60K-only model honestly, you'll likely find the real gap is a specific item — a commercial espresso machine, a custom wrap, a POS system with catering integration. That's a much better SBA conversation: "I need $15K for this specific asset that my cash flow model shows pays back in 14 months," not "I need $40K for startup costs."

## Asset-Light Touring Economics

* **Calculate your revenue-per-operating-day against your fixed daily overhead.** If fixed costs run $120/day (truck note, insurance, commissary amortized) and you operate 260 days/year, you need $120 in gross margin before labor before you break even on overhead — every single day. That's the touring band's break-even-per-show number. Know yours, then every booking decision (is this farmers market worth $80 in travel and setup?) becomes a simple comparison, not a gut call.

* **Lease versus own is a ratio question, not a balance-sheet question.** If a truck lease runs $1,200/month and ownership costs (note + maintenance reserve + insurance delta) run $900/month, ownership looks cheaper — until you factor in one major mechanical failure in year one eating $4,000–8,000 from a thin cash reserve. A practical approach for year one: lease if you can, build a $5,000 maintenance reserve if you own, and never conflate "I own this asset" with "this asset is generating returns."

* **High-utilization days are your revenue-per-day metric, not revenue-per-month.** Touring acts track per-show income because that's where they can optimize. Your equivalent is revenue per vending shift. A Saturday festival at $1,800 gross is a different asset deployment than a Tuesday lunch spot at $400 gross. When you know your cost-per-day, you can rank every booking by margin, not by gross revenue — and that changes which opportunities you pursue.
