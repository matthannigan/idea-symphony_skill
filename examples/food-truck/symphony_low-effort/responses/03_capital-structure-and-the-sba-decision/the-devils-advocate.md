# Capital Structure and the SBA Decision - The Devil's Advocate

## SBA Debt Service, Strings, and What the Loan Really Buys

* **Summary.** Debt service on a $40K microloan at 10% over five years runs roughly $850/month — before you've sold a single lumpia taco. Have we considered that this fixed obligation arrives regardless of whether month four brings a rainy season slump or a city permit delay? If soft revenue in months 3–6 forces you to accept low-margin catering gigs to cover the note, you may train your brand as the cheap option before you've had a chance to establish a premium identity. Mitigation: model two break-even scenarios — one with and one without loan service — so you know exactly at what daily revenue threshold the loan becomes operationally coercive rather than enabling.

* **Summary.** SBA microloans come with lender oversight obligations that most founders underestimate: use-restriction attestations, periodic financial reporting, and lender-relationship management. One potential challenge is that a solo founder already juggling permits, commissary scheduling, event bookings, and a pending city ordinance has limited bandwidth for compliance paperwork. If reporting slips, the lender relationship deteriorates at exactly the moment you might need a covenant waiver during a slow quarter. Mitigation: before signing, ask the lender for the full compliance calendar and stress-test whether you can meet it with zero administrative staff.

* **Summary.** Have we considered what the declined-loan list actually reveals? If the activities you'd defer without the SBA money are things like "branded signage," "a second prep day," and "a reserve fund," the loan is partly funding optionality and comfort margin — not survival. That's not inherently wrong, but it means the loan's psychological value (reduced anxiety) may be its primary function, not its stated operational one. Mitigation: treat the declined-loan exercise as a hard filter — only pursue the loan if at least one item on that deferred list is genuinely existential to the business model in year one.

* **Summary.** The regulatory environment here is actively shifting: the city council is debating restrictions on downtown vending hours and new annual licensing fees. One potential challenge is that the SBA loan's use restrictions may prevent you from pivoting capital quickly if regulations force you out of your planned vending locations. Borrowed money tied to a specific truck or equipment purchase doesn't flex when your revenue geography has to. Mitigation: negotiate the broadest possible use language upfront, and build a contingency into your loan scenario analysis for a 30% reduction in downtown vending access.

* **Summary.** Have we considered the timing mismatch risk? SBA microloan approvals can take 30–90 days, and first draws may lag further. If you've committed to a commissary lease, a truck purchase deposit, or a launch event date on the assumption the loan closes by a certain time, a processing delay converts a capital plan into a cash crisis. Mitigation: treat the SBA funds as unavailable until the wire actually clears, and design your launch timeline around the $60K base with the loan as upside, not prerequisite.

## Working Capital Reserve and Minimum Self-Funding Runway

* **Summary.** The assumption buried in most food truck financial plans is that revenue "stabilizes" within 60–90 days. Have we considered that stabilization in a city with 30 active trucks and a shifting vending ordinance may take twice that long? If commissary fees ($400–800/month), fuel, and inventory are drawing down your cash buffer at $8–12K per month before meaningful sales, a 90-day reserve is actually a 3-month runway to failure, not a cushion. Mitigation: model a pessimistic ramp — assume 50% of projected revenue for the first three months — and make sure the remaining liquid capital still covers 60 days at that reduced pace.

* **Summary.** One potential challenge is that "working capital reserve" and "emergency fund" tend to collapse into the same pool the moment the first unexpected expense hits — a truck breakdown, a failed health inspection requiring a refit, or a commissary that raises rates. If you draw from your operating buffer for a $3K repair, your true runway shrinks by more than $3K because you've also lost the float that was covering the next inventory cycle. Mitigation: treat the reserve as genuinely off-limits for capital repairs by pre-negotiating a repair credit line or a small equipment insurance rider before launch.

* **Summary.** Have we considered that the minimum self-funding threshold — the daily revenue level at which the business covers its own operating costs without further capital draws — is almost certainly higher than the back-of-envelope estimate? Food cost ratios for fusion concepts with specialty inputs (ube, achiote, quality proteins) often run 32–38% rather than the 28–30% in textbook models. At 35% food cost on a $600 average day, you're netting $390 before labor, fuel, and commissary. Mitigation: price-test your menu against a 35% food cost assumption rather than an optimistic 28%, and recalculate break-even accordingly before finalizing the capital buffer target.

* **Summary.** The city's pending ordinance changes introduce a scenario most working capital models ignore: forced idle time. If new downtown hour restrictions reduce your vending window by two hours on peak days, you don't just lose revenue proportionally — you lose it disproportionately because setup and breakdown costs are fixed regardless of hours open. One potential challenge is that your minimum runway calculation assumes current operating conditions persist, but the regulatory floor may drop mid-ramp. Mitigation: add an "ordinance shock" scenario to your cash flow model — what does runway look like if peak-day revenue drops 20% due to hour restrictions starting in month four?

## What If You Ran the Business as If the SBA Loan Will Never Arrive?

* **Summary.** Have we considered that "we'll solve that with capital" is most dangerous not when the problem is obvious but when it's invisible? The SBA assumption likely masks at least one structural issue in the business model — possibly that the unit economics only work at a volume the truck physically cannot achieve in available vending hours. Forcing a $60K-only design exposes that ceiling before you've borrowed against it. Mitigation: build the full $60K-only operating model and explicitly identify every constraint it surfaces; those constraints are the actual business design problems, not funding gaps.

* **Summary.** One potential challenge with the bootstrap-only frame is that it can drive decisions that are penny-wise but operationally fragile — buying a used truck with deferred maintenance issues, skipping the commissary with better equipment, or launching with a menu so pared down it can't differentiate from competitors. Constraint is clarifying, but there's a version of it that just front-loads problems rather than eliminating them. Mitigation: distinguish between constraints that reveal genuine efficiencies (smaller menu, fewer vending days) and constraints that create downstream costs (unreliable equipment, inferior prep capacity). Not all cuts are equal.

* **Summary.** Have we considered that the $60K-only thought experiment is only useful if it's honest about what "deferred" really means? If the answer to "what do we cut without the loan?" is "the reserve fund," you haven't discovered a leaner business — you've discovered a business with no margin for error. That's not a design insight; it's a risk that will surface the first time anything goes wrong. Mitigation: if the $60K model only closes by eliminating the operating buffer, the honest conclusion is that this business requires more capital, not that it can run lean — and the SBA loan becomes genuinely necessary rather than optional.

* **Summary.** The $60K-only exercise can also reveal which parts of the three-year expansion plan — fleet growth, brick-and-mortar — are contingent on the first truck generating capital to fund them, versus on external financing at each stage. Have we considered that if the bootstrap model shows the single truck can't generate surplus capital above living expenses, the expansion plan is actually a sequence of future funding rounds, not organic growth? Mitigation: model the truck's surplus-generating capacity explicitly and see whether the three-year plan is self-funding from operations or whether it requires the loan (or future loans) at every stage.

## Asset-Light Touring Economics

* **Summary.** The touring musician analogy is compelling, but have we considered where it breaks down? A touring act can cancel dates, reroute, or park the van for a month when revenue doesn't justify the road. A food truck operation has commissary commitments, staff expectations, permit obligations, and brand momentum that make "parking it" far more costly. Lease payments on a truck don't pause when you take a week off. Mitigation: if adopting the asset-light lease model, explicitly negotiate pause clauses or minimum-use thresholds into any equipment lease before signing, so you retain the flexibility the analogy promises.

* **Summary.** One potential challenge with modeling the lease-vs.-buy decision as "fixed overhead to revenue-generating days" is that it optimizes for cost per operating day but may ignore the strategic value of ownership equity. A purchased truck — even a depreciating one — can be collateralized for future financing, sold to recover capital if the business fails, or handed to a second operator as the fleet scales. A leased truck builds no balance sheet asset. Mitigation: run the ratio model as directed, but add a terminal-value column: what is the owned asset worth at year three versus the leased asset? The difference may justify higher fixed costs.

* **Summary.** Have we considered that the "revenue-generating days" denominator in the touring model is more variable for a food truck than for a touring act? A band can schedule every day of a tour. A food truck is subject to weather, permit zone availability, health department inspections, commissary scheduling conflicts, and — in this city — an actively shifting ordinance environment. If your lease-vs.-buy ratio assumes 22 operating days per month but you actually average 16, the fixed-cost ratio changes materially. Mitigation: build the model with a conservative 16-day month and stress-test at 12, not just the optimistic case.

* **Summary.** The asset-light philosophy also carries a less visible risk: lessors of commercial kitchen equipment and specialty vehicles have leverage at renewal time that they don't have at signing. If the business is profitable at year two, the lessor knows your switching costs are high — a new truck requires rebranding, re-permitting, and operational disruption — and can price accordingly. Have we considered that the "low fixed cost" of leasing in year one may become a "high renewal cost" in year three, right when you're trying to fund fleet expansion? Mitigation: negotiate multi-year rate caps or purchase options into any lease from day one, when your leverage is highest.
