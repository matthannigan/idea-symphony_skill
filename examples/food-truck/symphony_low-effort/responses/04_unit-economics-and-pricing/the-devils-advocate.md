# Unit Economics and Pricing - The Devil's Advocate

## Food Cost Ratios by SKU and Margin Weight

* **The specialty-ingredient penalty compounds invisibly.** Ube, in particular, is a seasonal and import-dependent ingredient in most U.S. mid-size cities. A 28–32% food cost target assumes stable wholesale prices, but a single supply disruption — a bad Philippine harvest, a shipping delay, a local Asian grocery that stops stocking — can push ube-based item costs above 40% overnight. Have we considered that building the truck's margin engine around a specialty horchata variant creates a fragile single point of failure? A mitigation: identify a second-source supplier or a shelf-stable ube extract early, before it's a crisis.

* **The "margin weight" framing may assign the burden to the wrong items.** It's tempting to designate high-volume basics (lumpia tacos) as the margin workhorses and treat premium items (ube horchata) as addons — but what if customers come specifically for the specialty drink and treat the food as secondary? If the horchata carries the foot traffic but gets cost-suppressed to hit margin ratios, you may end up underpricing your actual draw. One potential challenge here: conducting a customer motivation survey or watching which items sell out first in the first 60 days before locking in a margin-weight hierarchy.

* **A 28–32% food cost target is a steady-state number, not a launch number.** At low volume, waste, trial batches, and portion calibration errors routinely push actual food cost to 38–45% in a truck's first three months. If the break-even model assumes 30% from day one, the cash position will be worse than projected. This doesn't mean abandon the target — it means model the launch phase honestly with a 40% food cost assumption and build a cash reserve to absorb it.

* **Fusion ingredients may resist bulk-purchase economics.** A standard taco truck can buy tortillas, ground beef, and salsa in restaurant depot quantities at rock-bottom prices. Lumpia wrappers, adobo proteins (if using quality pork belly or chicken thighs), and specialty sauces don't always scale the same way. Have we confirmed that the local supply chain actually allows bulk purchasing at the volumes a single truck generates, or is this truck permanently stuck buying at retail-adjacent prices?

## Daily Break-Even Transaction Volume

* **Fixed cost estimates almost always undercount the first year.** The standard list — commissary rental, truck depreciation, fuel, permits, labor — omits several real costs: credit card processing fees (typically 2.5–3% of gross, which at a $15 ticket adds up meaningfully), food waste and spoilage (often 5–8% on a new menu with uncertain demand), and the founder's own unpaid labor. If the break-even model treats owner draws as zero, it's not measuring business viability — it's measuring survival. A more honest model should include at least a minimum owner draw as a fixed cost.

* **The daily cover target may not survive weather and location variance.** At a $13 average ticket and 32% food cost, a truck might need 80–100 covers per service period to go net-positive after fixed costs. That's achievable at a busy lunch spot — but what about a Tuesday in February after a permit zone cancellation? Have we stress-tested whether the truck survives 20 consecutive below-break-even days, which is not an unusual scenario in year one? A mitigation: model a "bad week" scenario and ensure the cash reserve can absorb it without requiring the SBA loan as emergency funding.

* **Commissary kitchen costs are frequently underestimated for fusion menus.** Filipino-Mexican fusion involves multiple prep stages — marinating proteins, frying lumpia, building sauces — that take significantly longer than a simpler menu. If commissary access is billed by the hour and prep runs long, daily fixed costs can creep well above initial estimates. One potential challenge: clocking actual prep time in a commissary kitchen before finalizing unit economics, not after.

* **"Net-positive vs. break-even" is not a binary.** A day that covers variable costs but not fixed costs is often treated as a loss, but a more useful framing is contribution margin per service day. If the truck is consistently positive on contribution margin but negative after fixed cost amortization, the problem is fixed cost structure, not sales volume — and those have different solutions. Have we built the model in a way that distinguishes between these two failure modes?

## Price Elasticity and Economic Access

* **The $8-lunch segment and the $14-item are not just incompatible — they're in active tension for a "community" brand.** There's a specific reputational risk here: if the truck explicitly markets itself around Filipino-American culture and community connection but prices out the demographic it claims to serve, it will attract a backlash that no amount of good marketing can fully defuse. Have we considered that pricing above $12 per item in a mid-size city with a working-class Filipino-American community could read as commodifying the culture for a wealthier audience? A mitigation: a deliberate access mechanism — a lunch special, a rice bowl option, a loyalty discount — that maintains the community signal even at premium price points.

* **Price elasticity evidence from "comparable" trucks may not transfer.** Food truck pricing benchmarks typically come from coastal cities or established markets where customer expectations and spending norms differ. A mid-size city with a growing-but-young food truck scene may have customers who have mentally anchored to a $9–11 price point. Testing a $14 item launch in that market without a price sensitivity experiment (even informal — two price points, two weeks) is risky. One potential challenge: confirming that "comparable market evidence" is actually comparable before baking it into the model.

* **Premium pricing locks in a customer segment that is inherently less loyal to a food truck.** Higher-income customers who can afford $14–16 per item also have more dining options, shorter lunch windows, and higher expectations for consistency. They are more likely to abandon the truck for a bad experience (long wait, a sold-out item, a parking spot that disappeared) than the $10-lunch crowd who has fewer alternatives. Relying on premium customers for margin recovery means the truck becomes more fragile, not less, as prices rise.

* **"Optimizing for both volume and margin" may be a false target.** The question frames these as simultaneously achievable, but at a new truck in a price-sensitive mid-size market, there's a genuine tension. Higher margin often requires lower volume; high volume often requires pricing that compresses margin. Have we identified which of these actually matters more in year one — surviving (cash) or building the customer base (volume) — and priced accordingly? A mitigation: consider launching with a deliberately lean menu at moderate prices to build traffic, then testing premium items as additions once a repeat-customer base is established.
