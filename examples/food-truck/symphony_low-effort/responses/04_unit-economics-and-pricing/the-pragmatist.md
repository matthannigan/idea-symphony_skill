# Unit Economics and Pricing - The Pragmatist

## Question 1: Food Cost Ratios by SKU and Margin Weight

* **Model the SKU economics before finalizing the menu.** Before the truck rolls, build a simple spreadsheet with every ingredient in each item priced at actual supplier quotes. Ube powder, fresh lumpia wrappers, and achiote are not commodity ingredients — expect 8–12% higher raw food cost per item than equivalent beef-taco components. A lumpia taco using ground pork with a ube-tinted wrapper could realistically land at 30–32% food cost; an adobo burrito with braised chicken and rice at 26–28%. That difference in margin profile matters for which items you push.

* **Designate one or two explicit margin anchors and protect them.** A practical approach is to identify the ube horchata and one high-volume hand-held (the lumpia taco, most likely) as the items that must carry margin weight. Beverages in particular can hit 18–22% food cost with good supplier relationships. If those two items are strong, slower weeks on the entrée side are survivable. Build your marketing, signage, and upsell language around those anchors.

* **Set a 28% food cost target, not 32%, as your operating ceiling.** Many operators use 30% as the "acceptable" ceiling and then consistently run 33–35% once waste, over-portioning, and comping are factored in. Starting with a tighter internal target gives you real buffer. Run a weekly actual-vs.-target food cost report from the beginning — even manually from receipts — to catch drift before it becomes structural.

* **Test price elasticity on specialty ingredients with limited-time items before locking the menu.** If ube and lumpia wrappers are going to be sourced from a specialty distributor, pilot a small batch at a pop-up or farmers' market first. You'll learn actual yield, waste rate, and prep time — all of which affect effective food cost — before you're locked into a commissary schedule and daily production targets.

* **Consider a tiered menu structure that lets lower-cost items underwrite the signature ones.** A practical minimum viable menu might be: two flagship fusion items (higher food cost, higher price, high margin in dollar terms), one comfort-accessible item like a rice bowl at $10–11 (lower food cost, lower price, volume-driven), and the ube horchata. That structure gives you margin anchors, a price-accessible entry point, and a signature drink that travels well on social media.

## Question 2: Daily Break-Even Transaction Volume

* **Build a daily P&L model before opening week, not after.** At a $13 average ticket and 30% food cost, each transaction generates roughly $9.10 in gross margin. To cover fixed daily costs — commissary rental ($40–60/day amortized), truck depreciation or lease payment (~$70–90/day), fuel and generator ($25–35/day), permit amortization (~$10/day), and a single part-time labor hour or two — you're looking at approximately $145–195 in daily fixed costs before any owner draw. That implies 16–22 covers just to reach zero before paying yourself. Target 40–60 covers per service to generate meaningful net income on a typical day.

* **Map service hours to realistic cover rates by location type.** A busy lunch spot might yield 50–70 covers in a two-hour peak window. A park or neighborhood rotation may yield 20–30 over three hours. These are structurally different businesses even at the same daily total. A practical approach is to scout your top three candidate locations for foot traffic before opening and estimate covers-per-hour conservatively — then see how many days per week you actually need to operate to hit your income target.

* **Define your "net-positive day" threshold explicitly and track it weekly.** Once you know your fixed daily cost floor, set a simple internal threshold: for example, "net-positive day = 35 covers or $455 gross." Post it where you can see it at service. After 60 days of operation, you'll have real data to refine the number — but having a concrete target from day one prevents the common trap of feeling busy without knowing whether the day was actually profitable.

* **Factor in the commissary relationship as a controllable cost lever.** Commissary kitchen rental is a fixed-ish cost that can sometimes be reduced by sharing time with another operator or negotiating a monthly rate rather than daily. On a slow-week cash flow basis, even a $200/month reduction in commissary costs shifts your break-even by roughly 20–25 covers per month. Negotiate the contract with that math in mind.

## Question 3: Price Elasticity and Economic Access

* **Price to cover costs first; then test elasticity through observed behavior, not surveys.** Research on food truck price sensitivity in mid-size markets suggests the $10–13 range maximizes both volume and margin for fusion concepts — but that's aggregate data, not your specific customer. A practical approach: open at $11–12 for your core items and watch repurchase rates and basket size for the first 30 days. If customers are consistently upgrading to add-ons and returning weekly, you have room to move to $13. If you're seeing one-time trial visits with no return, hold the price and look at other variables.

* **The $8 lunch customer is not your primary addressable market at launch, and that's okay to acknowledge explicitly.** Being honest about this in your own planning is more useful than trying to serve everyone. A $12–14 item can still reach economically diverse customers — especially if you locate strategically near mixed-income lunch destinations, participate in community events with a $9 small plate option, or offer a loyalty structure that rewards repeat visits. But pretending the math supports a $9 entrée when your food cost is $3.50 per item will sink the business before you can serve anyone.

* **Create a community access mechanism that doesn't require discounting your core menu.** A practical middle path: a $9 rice bowl (lower food cost, high volume, explicitly positioned as the accessible option) and periodic "pay it forward" or SNAP/EBT participation if the city's mobile vending rules allow it. This lets the primary menu hold at margin-positive pricing while still embedding the truck in the community it wants to serve — without subsidizing every transaction and burning through capital.

* **Consider a sliding context strategy: premium pricing at catering/events, competitive pricing at street vending.** Catering clients and corporate events have higher willingness to pay — $16–18 per head is common — and those revenues can partially subsidize keeping street vending prices at $12–13 rather than $15. This isn't charity; it's revenue segmentation. Build the catering margin into your overall business model so it does structural work, not just supplemental work.
