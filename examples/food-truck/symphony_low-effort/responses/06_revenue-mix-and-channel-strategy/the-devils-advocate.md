# Revenue Mix and Channel Strategy - The Devil's Advocate

## Revenue-Channel Contribution Margin and Sequencing

* **The catering margin illusion.** Catering is often presented as high-margin, but the math collapses when you fully load it: a $3,000 catering job that requires 10 hours of prep, 4 hours of service, a commissary rental block, two helpers, and an event insurance rider may net less per hour than a busy lunch shift. Have we calculated the true all-in cost per catering dollar — including the unpaid hours spent on proposals, client calls, and follow-up? One mitigation: build a catering rate card that enforces a minimum net margin threshold before accepting a booking, so you're not filling the calendar with events that feel lucrative but quietly drain capacity.

* **The silent-month trap is worse than the projections suggest.** A year-one revenue model that shows catering, street vending, and events all contributing each month is probably wrong. Catering pipelines take 3–6 months to build from cold; event organizers book 6–12 months in advance; street vending in month one is brand-building, not reliable revenue. The honest picture may be that months 1–4 are almost entirely street vending — with all its weather, permit, and traffic variability. Have we stress-tested a scenario where catering doesn't generate meaningful revenue until month 7? If that's the likely case, the $100K total capital needs to last 7 months of near-single-stream operation before diversification kicks in. That changes the loan decision and the burn rate math entirely.

* **Festival booth economics are frequently negative for solo operators.** Festival booth fees in mid-size markets can run $500–$2,500 per event, often requiring 10–14-hour days with a helper. After booth fees, event insurance, staffing, and the higher spoilage from volume-prepped food, many food truck operators report festival shifts as break-even or slightly negative — with enormous physical cost. The brand exposure argument is real, but have we calculated how many festival bookings per year are genuinely profitable versus how many are "brand investment" that we're calling revenue? One approach: treat festivals as a marketing budget line for year one, accept only the highest-traffic events, and measure carefully before expanding the festival calendar in year two.

* **Building catering early may cannibalize the street presence that justifies the catering price.** The catering desirability for Filipino-Mexican fusion rests substantially on brand recognition — clients need to know the truck before they'll book it for a corporate lunch. If catering bookings pull the truck off the street on key lunch days in months 4–8, you may be trading the brand-building moment for revenue that isn't yet at scale. Have we considered holding a hard cap on catering days per month in year one — say, no more than four — to protect street presence during the identity-formation period?

## The Distraction Question

* **Three channels in year one is operationally three businesses.** Each channel requires distinct sales motions: street vending is daily and reactive; catering requires proactive outreach, proposals, and contracts; events require months-ahead relationship-building with organizers. A solo founder with a culinary background and no prior business ownership is simultaneously learning to run a commercial kitchen, manage permits, handle cash flow, and build a customer base. Adding two more sales pipelines in year one doesn't diversify risk — it multiplies execution risk at the worst possible time. One possible frame: treat year one as a single-channel year with opportunistic catering only (no active outreach, just inbound inquiries), and build the catering pipeline deliberately in year two when operations are stable.

* **Diversification can become a narrative for avoiding the hard work of one channel.** Have we honestly examined whether the appeal of "three revenue streams" is partly a way to hedge against the discomfort of fully committing to street vending — which requires showing up every day, grinding through slow weeks, and building a loyal customer base through repetition? The food truck businesses that reach fleet scale typically did so by becoming exceptional at one thing first. Pursuing catering and events simultaneously may be a way of avoiding the discipline that single-channel focus demands, while telling ourselves it's strategic.

* **The relationship-building required for each channel conflicts with the time available.** Catering requires cultivating corporate HR contacts, event planners, and venue managers — relationships that take months of lunches, follow-up emails, and tastings. Festival organizer relationships require attendance at vendor fairs and early-season applications. Neither happens while you're running a lunch service. For a solo founder without a business partner or sales support, the relational overhead of three channels may not be humanly manageable in year one. Mitigation: identify one secondary channel only — likely catering, since it has the highest margin potential — and assign a fixed weekly time budget to developing it, rather than pursuing all three simultaneously.

## Revenue Mix as Resilience

* **Resilience through diversification only works if each channel is actually ready to absorb volume.** The argument that catering and events smooth regulatory and seasonal volatility assumes those channels are developed enough to flex when street vending drops. In reality, a catering pipeline with two clients and three festival relationships cannot absorb a six-week downtown vending restriction. True resilience comes from depth in one channel, not surface-level presence in three. Have we considered whether the resilience goal is better served in year one by building a robust commissary-based catering operation — one channel, deeply developed — rather than hedging thinly across three?

* **The "lean into what you do best" framing may be avoiding a hard constraint.** The food, the experience, and the story are real assets — but they are assets that require a stable platform to deploy. If the business is simultaneously managing three channels with insufficient capitalization, the quality of the food and experience will suffer first: prep shortcuts, tired operators, inconsistent execution. Have we considered that the most resilient path might actually be the most boring one — street vending only, five days a week, the same locations, until unit economics are genuinely proven — before adding complexity?

## Whose Celebrations Get Served

* **Filipino and Latino communities may already have established catering relationships that a fusion brand disrupts, rather than serves.** A family planning a quinceañera or a Filipino cultural organization planning a heritage event likely has existing relationships with caterers who specialize in the cuisine authentically — not a fusion interpretation. The fusion framing that works as a street brand ("lumpia taco" is intriguing to a lunch crowd) may read as inappropriate or gimmicky to a community organization that wants traditional food as a cultural anchor. Have we honestly stress-tested whether the Filipino-Mexican fusion concept is actually the right fit for Filipino or Latino cultural events — or whether pursuing those events creates an awkward mismatch that damages community relationships rather than building them? Mitigation: before pursuing community event catering, do direct outreach to Filipino and Latino community organizations to ask explicitly whether the fusion format would serve their events or not.

* **Holding capacity for community events at reduced rates is a values-driven choice with real financial consequences that should be made explicitly, not aspirationally.** Reserving 20% of catering capacity for community events at 60% of market rate sounds admirable, but it means roughly 12% of catering revenue is discounted — and catering capacity is already constrained by a solo operator. Have we built that discount explicitly into the financial model, or is it an intention we're assuming won't materially affect the numbers? If the business is marginally solvent in year one, community discounts may not be sustainable without a deliberate subsidy structure — perhaps a separate "community rate" fund built from a small surcharge on corporate bookings. The point isn't that community service is wrong; it's that conflating community values with revenue projections obscures both.

* **The "existing social capital" critique of high-margin clients is valid but shouldn't become a reason to avoid them.** Corporate lunch clients, wedding reception clients, and festival organizers do tend to have more social capital and disposable income than community organizations. But those clients are also the ones whose revenue funds the capacity to serve community events at lower rates. If the business deliberately underweights high-margin clients out of a values-based discomfort with serving people with more resources, it may undermine the financial foundation that makes community service possible at all. Have we considered that the most mission-aligned structure might be one that aggressively pursues high-margin corporate and event catering precisely in order to cross-subsidize community bookings — rather than treating those client categories as being in tension?
