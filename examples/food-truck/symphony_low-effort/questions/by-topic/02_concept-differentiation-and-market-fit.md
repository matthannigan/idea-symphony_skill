# Topic Cluster 02: Concept Differentiation and Market Fit

## Questions

1. **Durable differentiation and identity lock-in risk**: In a Tuesday-at-lunch scenario (not in marketing language), what is the one thing this truck does that the four existing fusion trucks cannot easily copy in six months, how confident are we that the target market — not just adventurous early adopters — can understand and trust lumpia tacos and adobo burritos quickly enough to drive repeat visits at break-even volume, what data points (price point, location patterns, social following, event presence) best reveal whether the fusion segment is oversaturated, and if early crowds skew heavily toward one narrow segment (say, the Filipino diaspora community), does that self-selection create an identity lock-in risk that progressively narrows the customer base rather than broadening it — including the plan for holding volume without eroding margins if the existing four respond with price cuts, menu mimicry, or aggressive positioning at the same vending zones?
2. **Fusion as an asset — the moment of conversion**: Filipino-Mexican fusion is an unusually compelling pairing with real cultural and flavor logic behind it. When have you seen a dish or concept surprise people — convert a skeptic, spark a conversation, create a moment of genuine delight — and what was it about that experience that made the connection land?
3. **If the fusion concept is ahead of the market**: If local appetite for Filipino-Mexican fusion turns out to be thinner than expected in year one, what does the truck do — pivot, educate, or wait — and has that contingency been named and budgeted for rather than left as a vague fallback?
4. **Community footholds already in place**: What relationships, networks, or goodwill do you already have in this city — with neighbors, food communities, potential regulars, or local institutions — that could serve as early anchors for the business even before the truck hits the street?
5. **What if nobody actually wants fusion?**: Imagine the Filipino-Mexican menu is not a unique selling point but an active liability — customers come once out of curiosity and never return because the flavors don't map onto any existing craving. What does that reveal about the difference between being interesting and being *wanted*, and how would you build a repeat-customer business around a concept nobody has a pre-existing appetite for?
6. **The dish that defines them**: Of all the menu items — lumpia tacos, adobo burritos, ube horchata — which one is most likely to become the thing customers describe to friends in a single excited sentence, and what is it about that dish's story (its origin, its surprise, its sensory experience) that makes it stick?
7. **The truck as organism, not vehicle**: What if the food truck weren't a fixed culinary offering that moves through space, but a living concept that continuously reshapes its identity based on where it lands — not just adapting the menu, but becoming a different cultural institution at each stop, something that has no current name?
