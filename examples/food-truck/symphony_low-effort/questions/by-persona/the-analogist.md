# Brainstorming Questions from The Analogist: Food Truck Startup and Expansion Strategy

---

## Growth Sequencing: The Franchise and Microbrewery Parallel

1. **Proof-of-concept before scaling**: Craft breweries typically treat their taproom as a low-capital laboratory — refining recipes, building brand loyalty, and testing pricing before committing to wider distribution. How might you design your first truck's first 12 months as a deliberate proof-of-concept phase, identifying which menu items, locations, and revenue streams have earned the right to anchor the expanded fleet?

2. **Hub-and-spoke logistics**: Regional airline networks solved the economics of thin routes by routing passengers through high-volume hubs. Could your commissary kitchen serve as a production hub that supplies multiple future trucks with pre-prepped components, and how would that hub model change your equipment purchasing decisions today?

---

## Regulatory Navigation: The Liquor License and Farmers Market Analogy

3. **Trading zone scarcity as leverage**: Farmers markets restrict vendor slots precisely to create a defensible competitive advantage for incumbents — and established vendors often shape the rules that govern new entrants. How could you position yourself as an active participant in shaping the pending city ordinance rather than a passive recipient of it, drawing on the playbook of industries (craft distillers, short-term rental operators) that successfully organized vendor coalitions to influence favorable licensing frameworks?

4. **Licensing as a moat, not just a cost**: In the legal cannabis industry, early licensees used regulatory complexity as a competitive barrier once they were inside it. What relationships, permits, or designated vending spots could you establish now — before the new ordinance passes — that would be difficult for later entrants to replicate?

---

## Capital Structure: The Food-as-Franchise and Music-Touring Model

5. **Asset-light touring economics**: Touring musicians treat their van and equipment as a revenue-generating vehicle, not a prestige asset — they lease or share gear when possible to keep fixed costs low and cash available for the next market. How does the lease-vs.-buy decision on your truck and kitchen equipment look when you model it the way a touring act would: as a ratio of fixed overhead to revenue-generating days, rather than as a balance-sheet ownership question?

---

## Brand Identity: The Regional Cuisine Export Analogy

6. **The "ambassador dish" strategy**: When regional cuisines travel — think Tex-Mex moving into suburban America, or Korean BBQ into the Midwest — the dishes that cross over successfully are rarely the most authentic; they are the ones that translate a genuine flavor profile into a form that lowers the barrier to first taste. Which one item on your Filipino-Mexican fusion menu could serve as the accessible "gateway dish" that converts curious first-timers into repeat customers and catering clients, the way the California roll expanded sushi's American audience?

---

## Resilience and Seasonality: The Agricultural CSA Model

7. **Subscription revenue as a weather hedge**: Community-supported agriculture (CSA) programs solved the seasonal cash-flow problem for small farms by converting unpredictable revenue into upfront subscriptions — customers pay in spring for summer produce, smoothing the farm's income curve. What is the food-truck equivalent of a CSA subscription — a catering retainer, a prepaid event package, or a monthly lunch-spot contract with a local employer — and how large would that recurring revenue base need to be to cover your fixed costs through your slowest months?
