# Brainstorming Questions from The Provocateur: Food Truck Startup and Expansion Strategy

---

## The Customers Are Wrong

1. **What if nobody actually wants fusion?**: Imagine the Filipino-Mexican menu is not a unique selling point but an active liability — customers come once out of curiosity and never return because the flavors don't map onto any existing craving. What does that reveal about the difference between being interesting and being *wanted*, and how would you build a repeat-customer business around a concept nobody has a pre-existing appetite for?

2. **What if you made the truck deliberately hard to find?**: No fixed schedule, no public location announcements — customers have to follow cryptic clues on social media to locate the truck each day. Absurd, yes. But what does this reveal about whether the business's real product is the food, or the *story and hunt* around the food? What would it mean if the scarcity and discovery were actually the thing people were paying for?

---

## The Expansion Logic Is Backwards

3. **What if growth is the failure mode?**: The plan assumes that scaling from one truck to a fleet to a brick-and-mortar is success. Imagine the opposite: every truck you add dilutes the founder's culinary identity, every employee introduces inconsistency, and the brick-and-mortar becomes a mediocre restaurant that kills the brand. What if staying deliberately small — one truck, one founder, maximum control — is the actual competitive advantage, and the expansion roadmap is the trap? *The real insight: which parts of the growth plan exist because they're genuinely better for the business, versus because "growth" is the culturally expected answer?*

4. **What if the city ordinance passing is the best thing that could happen?**: Everyone treats the pending restrictions as a threat. Now flip it entirely: restrictive vending hours and higher fees wipe out half the competition, leaving only operators with real capital and community relationships — exactly the profile this founder is trying to build. What strategy becomes available if you assume the ordinance passes and treat it as a moat rather than a barrier?

---

## The Capital Structure Is Upside Down

5. **What if you ran the business as if the SBA loan will never arrive?**: Not as a contingency plan, but as the permanent operating assumption. Design every decision — menu size, staffing model, equipment choices, catering pricing — around the $60K ceiling only. What does that force you to figure out that the loan money would let you avoid figuring out? *The insight: which parts of the business plan are actually just "we'll solve that with capital" deferring a hard problem that capital won't actually solve?*

---

## The Competition Is the Wrong Enemy

6. **What if the four similar fusion trucks in the city are your best allies, not your competitors?**: Imagine a scenario where you formally co-operate with them — shared commissary costs, joint event bookings, a rotating "fusion district" pop-up that draws food media and drives tourism. Absurd? Maybe. But what does it reveal about whether food truck success is actually zero-sum in a market this size, and whether the competitive instinct is costing more than it's earning?

---

## The Founder Is the Hidden Constraint

7. **What if the culinary background is the obstacle, not the asset?**: A chef thinks about food. A food truck operator thinks about throughput, positioning, weather risk, and permit renewals. Imagine a version of this business run by someone with zero culinary skill but perfect operational instincts — what would they do differently? Now: which of those differences reveal blind spots in the current plan that are being obscured by confidence in the food quality? *The real insight: the business is not a restaurant; it's a logistics operation that happens to serve food, and it should be designed that way first.*
