# Brainstorming Questions from The Systems Thinker: Food Truck Startup and Expansion Strategy

---

## Reputation and Demand Loops

1. **The identity lock-in risk**: Filipino-Mexican fusion is specific enough to attract a devoted niche but narrow enough to limit walk-up curiosity — if early crowds skew heavily toward one segment (say, the Filipino diaspora community), how does that shape which locations and events you pursue, and does that self-selection progressively narrow the customer base rather than broaden it?

2. **The visibility–reputation cycle**: Early press coverage, social media moments, and word-of-mouth all feed each other — a single viral post can fill a calendar for months — but what's the failure mode if the first wave of attention comes before operations are tight enough to deliver on the promise, and how long does a bad-experience reputation linger versus a good one?

3. **Commissary kitchen as a shared resource**: The commissary kitchen is a shared asset with other food truck operators. If your prep schedule conflicts with peak demand from other tenants, or if the kitchen raises rates as demand grows, what's your contingency — and does dependence on a third-party facility create a ceiling on how fast you can scale?

---

## Capital Structure and Growth Sequencing

4. **The SBA loan decision and its downstream effects**: Taking the $40K SBA microloan accelerates your timeline but adds a fixed monthly obligation from day one. If early revenue is soft — common in the first 90 days — does the debt service pressure force operating decisions (accepting low-margin catering gigs, skipping a slow market for a busy one) that compromise the brand before it has traction?

5. **Equipment ownership versus leasing and the capacity trap**: Leasing limits upfront risk but adds a fixed cost that doesn't flex with revenue. Owning outright preserves margin but concentrates capital in a depreciating asset. Which choice actually gives you more flexibility to add a second truck — and does the answer change depending on whether truck #2 comes from cash flow or another loan?

6. **Revenue stream sequencing**: Daily vending, catering, and events each have different margin profiles, booking cycles, and operational demands. If you build the business around high-margin catering contracts early, does daily vending become secondary infrastructure (preserving commissary relationships and staff hours) or does catering crowd out the street presence that builds the brand equity that makes the catering desirable in the first place?

---

## Regulatory Environment and Political Dynamics

7. **The pending ordinance as a system shock**: The proposed downtown vending restrictions and new licensing fees aren't just a compliance cost — they're a selective pressure that will thin the weakest operators from the market. If the ordinance passes, which trucks are most likely to exit, and does that create an opportunity (less competition, more prime spots) or a threat (smaller market, fewer anchor events)?

8. **Engagement with the food truck association**: Joining the local food truck association and engaging city council is usually framed as relationship-building, but it's also a coordination problem — individual operators have incentives to free-ride on advocacy others do. What does it actually cost you to be an active participant in the regulatory fight, and what's your best outcome if you're a visible voice in that process versus a quiet observer?

9. **Regulation as a moat**: Stricter permitting and higher fees raise barriers to entry for future competitors. Is there a version of the regulatory outcome that is actually better for established operators with capital than for new entrants — and if so, at what point do your interests in the regulatory debate diverge from the broader food truck community's?

---

## Operational Constraints and Scaling Dynamics

10. **The solo-founder bottleneck**: Every operation depends on a single person with culinary expertise and business judgment. If you're the one cooking, you can't also be cultivating catering relationships, scouting locations, and managing the books. What's the first function you hire or contract out, and does that decision determine whether you're building a business or a job?

11. **Seasonal oscillation and cash buffer sizing**: Revenue dips in winter are predictable, but the timing of the dip relative to fixed obligations (loan payments, commissary fees, equipment lease) is what actually determines whether the business survives the first year. What cash reserve level would let you ride out two bad months without making decisions that damage the business — and is that reserve achievable from $60K starting capital after equipment and launch costs?

---

## Expansion Path and Success Criteria

12. **The brick-and-mortar transition risk**: A fixed location solves some food truck problems (weather, permit zones, vending hours) while creating new ones (rent, longer operating hours, table service complexity, higher staff count). Operators who expand to brick-and-mortar often find the truck was profitable and the restaurant is not — what would you need to see in the truck's performance data before committing to a lease, and what does "the truck is doing well" actually mean in that decision?

13. **Fleet expansion versus brand depth**: A second truck doubles revenue potential but also doubles operational complexity, staffing dependencies, and the number of things that can go wrong on any given day. Is the path to $1M revenue through more trucks or through deeper revenue per truck (catering, merchandise, a CPG product, a branded sauce)? The choice shapes what kind of organization you're building — and reversing it mid-course is costly.
