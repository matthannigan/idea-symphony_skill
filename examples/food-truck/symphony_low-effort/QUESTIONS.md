# Brainstorming Questions: Food Truck Startup and Expansion Strategy

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Vision, Purpose, and the Expansion Premise

**Cluster focus**: Whether the three-year path from single truck to fleet or brick-and-mortar is the right question at all, and what assumptions about cash generation, founder role, and "growth" are baked into it.

1. **The 3-year horizon and why expand at all**: The goal names a 3-year path to fleet or brick-and-mortar — what assumptions about cash generation rate, personal risk tolerance, and market timing are baked into that number, what changes if the honest answer is 5 years or 18 months, and what evidence would need to be true for a single-truck operation (optimized and stabilized) to produce better risk-adjusted returns than any fleet or storefront ever would?
2. **The real goal: livelihood or business**: Is the aim to build a business that eventually runs without the founder, or to build a livelihood that keeps a culinary artist doing the work they love — and how differently does the expansion path look if those two answers are made explicit from the start rather than conflated under the word "growth"?
3. **Fleet and brick-and-mortar decision criteria, staged**: What specific financial thresholds (monthly net revenue, operating margin, cash reserves) should trigger the decision to acquire a second truck (est. $50–80K additional capital) versus invest that capital in catering infrastructure, a commissary ownership stake, or a brick-and-mortar down payment — and what would you need to see in the truck's performance data before signing a lease, given the brick-and-mortar transition risk that many operators discover the truck was profitable and the restaurant is not?
4. **The moment the fleet makes sense**: Picture the day the decision to add a second truck is made — not because the spreadsheet finally cleared a threshold, but because something felt undeniably right. What would you be seeing, feeling, and celebrating, and what would have had to be true about the first truck for that confidence to be earned?
5. **What if growth is the failure mode?**: The plan assumes that scaling from one truck to a fleet to a brick-and-mortar is success. Imagine the opposite: every truck you add dilutes the founder's culinary identity, every employee introduces inconsistency, and the brick-and-mortar becomes a mediocre restaurant that kills the brand. What if staying deliberately small — one truck, one founder, maximum control — is the actual competitive advantage, and the expansion roadmap is the trap? *The real insight: which parts of the growth plan exist because they're genuinely better for the business, versus because "growth" is the culturally expected answer?*
6. **Proof-of-concept before scaling**: Craft breweries typically treat their taproom as a low-capital laboratory — refining recipes, building brand loyalty, and testing pricing before committing to wider distribution. How might you design your first truck's first 12 months as a deliberate proof-of-concept phase, identifying which menu items, locations, and revenue streams have earned the right to anchor the expanded fleet?

## Topic Cluster 02: Concept Differentiation and Market Fit

**Cluster focus**: Whether Filipino-Mexican fusion earns durable customer preference in a city that already has four similar concepts, and what assumptions about customer knowledge, loyalty, and novelty are load-bearing.

1. **Durable differentiation and identity lock-in risk**: In a Tuesday-at-lunch scenario (not in marketing language), what is the one thing this truck does that the four existing fusion trucks cannot easily copy in six months, how confident are we that the target market — not just adventurous early adopters — can understand and trust lumpia tacos and adobo burritos quickly enough to drive repeat visits at break-even volume, what data points (price point, location patterns, social following, event presence) best reveal whether the fusion segment is oversaturated, and if early crowds skew heavily toward one narrow segment (say, the Filipino diaspora community), does that self-selection create an identity lock-in risk that progressively narrows the customer base rather than broadening it — including the plan for holding volume without eroding margins if the existing four respond with price cuts, menu mimicry, or aggressive positioning at the same vending zones?
2. **Fusion as an asset — the moment of conversion**: Filipino-Mexican fusion is an unusually compelling pairing with real cultural and flavor logic behind it. When have you seen a dish or concept surprise people — convert a skeptic, spark a conversation, create a moment of genuine delight — and what was it about that experience that made the connection land?
3. **If the fusion concept is ahead of the market**: If local appetite for Filipino-Mexican fusion turns out to be thinner than expected in year one, what does the truck do — pivot, educate, or wait — and has that contingency been named and budgeted for rather than left as a vague fallback?
4. **Community footholds already in place**: What relationships, networks, or goodwill do you already have in this city — with neighbors, food communities, potential regulars, or local institutions — that could serve as early anchors for the business even before the truck hits the street?
5. **What if nobody actually wants fusion?**: Imagine the Filipino-Mexican menu is not a unique selling point but an active liability — customers come once out of curiosity and never return because the flavors don't map onto any existing craving. What does that reveal about the difference between being interesting and being *wanted*, and how would you build a repeat-customer business around a concept nobody has a pre-existing appetite for?
6. **The dish that defines them**: Of all the menu items — lumpia tacos, adobo burritos, ube horchata — which one is most likely to become the thing customers describe to friends in a single excited sentence, and what is it about that dish's story (its origin, its surprise, its sensory experience) that makes it stick?
7. **The truck as organism, not vehicle**: What if the food truck weren't a fixed culinary offering that moves through space, but a living concept that continuously reshapes its identity based on where it lands — not just adapting the menu, but becoming a different cultural institution at each stop, something that has no current name?

## Topic Cluster 03: Capital Structure and the SBA Decision

**Cluster focus**: Whether to take the $40K SBA microloan, what obligations it actually imposes, and what "bootstrapping on $60K" means in practice rather than in slogan.

1. **SBA debt service, strings, and what the loan really buys**: What does the debt service on a $40K SBA microloan at typical rates (~8–13%) cost per month, and at what average daily revenue does the truck break even before loan repayment versus after — but also: if the loan were declined tomorrow, which specific activities would be deferred, scaled back, or eliminated (what does that list reveal about what the loan is really for), what reporting, use-restriction, and lender-relationship obligations beyond interest rate interact with the operational flexibility a solo founder in a regulation-shifting environment will actually need, and is there a realistic threshold where soft revenue in months 3–6 turns debt service pressure into operating decisions (low-margin catering gigs, skipped slow markets) that accelerate failure or compromise the brand before traction?
2. **Working capital reserve and minimum self-funding runway**: Beyond the truck and equipment, how much of the $60K–$100K starting capital needs to remain liquid as a cash operating buffer — covering commissary fees, food inventory, fuel, permits, and payroll during the weeks before revenue stabilizes — and what is the realistic minimum runway before the business must be self-funding?
3. **What if you ran the business as if the SBA loan will never arrive?**: Not as a contingency plan, but as the permanent operating assumption. Design every decision — menu size, staffing model, equipment choices, catering pricing — around the $60K ceiling only. What does that force you to figure out that the loan money would let you avoid figuring out? *The insight: which parts of the business plan are actually just "we'll solve that with capital" deferring a hard problem that capital won't actually solve?*
4. **Asset-light touring economics**: Touring musicians treat their van and equipment as a revenue-generating vehicle, not a prestige asset — they lease or share gear when possible to keep fixed costs low and cash available for the next market. How does the lease-vs.-buy decision on your truck and kitchen equipment look when you model it the way a touring act would: as a ratio of fixed overhead to revenue-generating days, rather than as a balance-sheet ownership question?

## Topic Cluster 04: Unit Economics and Pricing

**Cluster focus**: Per-item food cost, daily break-even math, and the price-point decisions that determine both margin and who the truck is actually for.

1. **Food cost ratios by SKU and margin weight**: Breaking this down by item — what food cost percentage is realistically achievable for Filipino-Mexican fusion given that ingredients like ube, adobo proteins, and lumpia wrappers may carry higher unit costs than standard taco components, what price point per item sustains a 28–32% food cost ratio while remaining competitive in a mid-size city market, and which items (lumpia tacos, adobo burritos, ube horchata) must carry the margin weight to let the business survive a slow week?
2. **Daily break-even transaction volume**: At a $12–15 average ticket and a 28–32% food cost ratio, what daily gross margin is generated per operating hour, and how many covers per service period does the truck need to clear fixed daily costs (commissary kitchen rental, truck depreciation or lease, fuel, permit amortization, labor) before generating owner income — and at what point does a day become net-positive versus break-even?
3. **Price elasticity and economic access**: How sensitive is the target customer to a $1–2 price increase per item, what does comparable food-truck-market evidence say about the price range at which Filipino-Mexican fusion items optimize for both volume and margin — and if break-even math pushes prices to $12–16 per item, what does that mean for the segment of the city that eats lunch on $8, and how does a premium price signal interact with aspirations to serve a *community* food experience rather than a specialty-market one?

## Topic Cluster 05: Equipment, Commissary, and Seasonal Cash Flow

**Cluster focus**: Three fixed-cost structures that together determine first-year survivability — the truck itself, the commissary, and the winter trough.

1. **Lease vs. purchase and the capacity trap**: Over a 3-year horizon, what does the full cost-of-ownership calculation look like for a used food truck ($30–60K purchase) versus leasing ($1,500–3,000/month) — purchase price plus maintenance and depreciation versus lease payments plus exit flexibility — at what revenue threshold does ownership become the rational choice, what happens to unit economics under a 30% revenue shortfall on each path (because the right answer may depend on which failure mode is more survivable), and which choice actually gives more flexibility to add a second truck, given that leasing adds a fixed cost that doesn't flex with revenue while owning concentrates capital in a depreciating asset — a classic capacity trap that changes depending on whether truck #2 comes from cash flow or another loan?
2. **Commissary as fixed cost, shared-resource ceiling, and community ecosystem**: Commissary kitchen fees are a recurring cost tied to regulatory compliance rather than production volume — how do they scale (or not) with catering and event work, does scheduling conflict with other tenants or rate increases as demand grows create a ceiling on how fast you can scale, and who are the people in the commissary ecosystem (it sits in a specific neighborhood and employs people) — is there a relationship worth cultivating there rather than just a cost to manage?
3. **Seasonal 12-month cash flow and reserve sizing**: What does a realistic 12-month revenue curve look like in a mid-size city with cold winters if peak summer months generate 2–3× low-season volumes, which fixed obligations (loan payments, commissary minimums, insurance, equipment lease) persist through the trough regardless of revenue, and how many months of operating reserves let the business survive the two or three lowest-revenue months without drawing on the SBA loan principal — and is that reserve achievable from $60K after equipment and launch costs?

## Topic Cluster 06: Revenue Mix and Channel Strategy

**Cluster focus**: How to sequence daily vending, catering, and events so that diversification strengthens rather than dilutes the core operation, and whose celebrations the business is equipped to serve.

1. **Revenue-channel contribution margin and sequencing**: Daily vending, catering, and events each have different margin profiles, booking cycles, and operational demands — what is the effective margin per dollar of revenue for a catering booking vs. a peak street-vending shift vs. a well-attended festival booth (after booth fees, event insurance riders, and long-hour staffing), what is the honest month-by-month revenue picture in year one including months where two of three streams are silent, how many festival bookings per quarter stabilize cash flow through slow street-vending months, and does building around high-margin catering early make daily vending secondary infrastructure — or does catering crowd out the street presence that builds the brand equity that makes the catering desirable in the first place?
2. **The distraction question**: Revenue diversification sounds prudent, but each channel requires its own sales effort, logistics, and relationship-building — does pursuing all three in the first year dilute the core operation before it has a stable identity and customer base?
3. **Revenue mix as resilience**: Catering and events can smooth the seasonal and regulatory volatility that daily vending can't control. What combination of revenue streams would let you lean into what you do best — the food, the experience, the story — while giving the business enough stability to invest in the next step?
4. **Whose celebrations get served**: From the perspective of a Filipino community organization planning a cultural event, or a family celebrating a quinceañera, does the fusion framing serve their needs or create an awkward mismatch — and since high-margin private events (corporate lunches, wedding receptions, festival organizers) tend to skew toward clients with existing social capital, what would it look like to deliberately hold capacity for community events with smaller budgets, and what would that cost in revenue versus what it might build in loyalty and rootedness?

## Topic Cluster 07: Regulatory Exposure and Political Engagement

**Cluster focus**: The pending city ordinance as both a revenue risk and a political process — who loses, who engages, and what a first-year operator's realistic leverage looks like.

1. **Ordinance impact modeling and zone dependency**: If the worst-case ordinance passes on day 90 — shorter downtown operating windows, annual licensing fees, curtailed zones — what is the quantified revenue hit expressed as a percentage of projected weekly revenue (including an estimated annual impact from losing, say, two peak lunch-hour slots per week in the downtown zone), how geographically and temporally concentrated is the model in the specific high-volume zones that may be curtailed, and how does that revenue loss compare to the cost of building catering and events as an offsetting channel?
2. **Ordinance as selective pressure**: The proposed restrictions aren't just a compliance cost — they're a selective pressure that will thin the weakest operators from the market. If the ordinance passes, which trucks are most likely to exit, and does that create an opportunity (less competition, more prime spots) or a threat (smaller market, fewer anchor events) for a well-capitalized new entrant?
3. **Whose downtown is it**: The people most affected by where the truck *can't* go aren't just the owner — they're the workers in downtown buildings, the residents of nearby neighborhoods, and the communities who depend on accessible lunch options without a car. Who loses if vending hours shrink, and have their voices entered the regulatory conversation at all?
4. **Engagement, coordination, and regulation-as-moat**: What realistic political capital does a solo founder with no prior business experience have in year one, what does it actually cost to be a visible voice in the regulatory fight versus quietly free-riding on advocacy others do (joining the association being a coordination problem as much as a relationship), might that time produce more durable protection invested in customer acquisition instead — and under what conditions does a stricter ordinance function as a moat for established operators with capital, such that your interests in the regulatory debate diverge from the broader food truck community's?
5. **Regulatory relationship as a resource**: Imagine a version of this story where your early engagement with the process becomes an asset — where you're known as a constructive voice, where your relationships with city officials and the food truck association open doors. What would that version look like, and what would you have done early on to make it possible?
6. **Trading zone scarcity as leverage**: Farmers markets restrict vendor slots precisely to create a defensible competitive advantage for incumbents — and established vendors often shape the rules that govern new entrants. How could you position yourself as an active participant in shaping the pending city ordinance rather than a passive recipient of it, drawing on the playbook of industries (craft distillers, short-term rental operators) that successfully organized vendor coalitions to influence favorable licensing frameworks?
7. **The city hall moment**: Walk through the scene where the founder attends a city council meeting about the proposed ordinance restricting downtown vending hours — who else is in that room, what does the founder say or choose not to say, and how does that single evening shape the business's relationship with local government for years to come?
8. **What if the ordinance debate is the wrong fight?**: Rather than engaging with city council on food truck licensing terms, what would it look like to make the regulatory category itself obsolete — to invent a model of mobile food that city governments have no existing framework to restrict because it doesn't map onto any prior concept?

## Topic Cluster 08: Customer Experience and Community Rootedness

**Cluster focus**: The human journey from first approach to repeat evangelism, whose experience gets heard versus stays silent, and whether the truck becomes something its neighborhood couldn't imagine losing.

1. **First 90 seconds and the visibility–reputation cycle**: Before anyone orders, they walk up, read the menu board, maybe wait in line — what moments of friction, confusion, or hesitation happen in those 90 seconds, how does the framing of dish names (lumpia tacos, adobo burritos) and signage either welcome or alienate customers encountering Filipino cuisine for the first time, what makes a repeat customer actually come back and what makes them *tell someone else* (different emotional moments), and how does the visibility–reputation cycle — where early press and viral posts fill a calendar for months — punish a launch whose operations aren't tight enough to deliver on the promise, given that bad-experience reputation lingers longer than good?
2. **The fusion-remix experience for the cultural insider**: People who love Mexican food may hesitate at lumpia; people who love Filipino food may feel the fusion "dilutes" it. What does it feel like to be a Filipino-American customer encountering your cultural food remixed — and how might that experience differ radically from the non-Filipino customer's delight at novelty?
3. **Community anchor and the founder's visible story**: Many food trucks build followings by being *in* a neighborhood rather than just passing through — what would it take for this truck to feel like *theirs* to a resident, how much does the solo founder's personal and cultural story matter to the experience, and what happens to the brand's sense of authenticity and connection if/when the founder is less present as the fleet expands?
4. **Who gives feedback and who doesn't**: Customers who love the food will tell you. Customers who had a confusing experience, felt the price wasn't worth it, or didn't understand the menu will mostly just not return. What intentional feedback mechanisms would surface the voices that otherwise go silent — especially from first-timers or lower-frequency visitors who represent the gap between the business's potential audience and its current regulars?
5. **The line that means something**: Imagine it's two years from now and there's a consistent Saturday lunch line at your truck — not just hungry customers, but people who feel like regulars, who bring friends, who tell a story about why they come back. What would those customers say when asked why this place matters to them, and what would you have built to make that true?
6. **The smallest true test**: Before the truck launches, what is the leanest experiment you could run — a pop-up, a catered gathering, a farmers market slot — that would tell you something real about how customers respond to the food and the concept, and give you a genuine win to build from?
7. **The customer as co-creator of the business**: What if the path from single truck to multi-revenue operation wasn't designed by the founder at all, but emerged entirely from what customers collectively decided to fund, participate in, or request — and what kind of relationship structure would that require that doesn't currently exist in food service?

## Topic Cluster 09: Solo-Founder Capacity and Staffing Sequencing

**Cluster focus**: The solo founder as structural bottleneck — the first hire, the network that accelerates the path, and the strengths already present that operational design should protect.

1. **The solo-founder bottleneck**: A food truck run by a solo founder is structurally a business that stops generating revenue whenever the founder is sick, injured, or burned out — and the founder who is cooking cannot simultaneously be cultivating catering relationships, scouting locations, and managing the books. How long can this operation sustain a one-person model before physical and cognitive load compress quality and strategic judgment, what is the first function to hire or contract out, and does that decision determine whether you're building a business or a job?
2. **Operations that scale your strengths**: Given that your culinary instinct and product quality are your clearest competitive advantages, what operational structures — staffing, prep systems, menu discipline — would protect and amplify those strengths as you grow from one truck to two or three, rather than diluting them?
3. **Who already has your back**: You're a solo founder, but you don't have to navigate this alone. Who in your existing network — a mentor, a fellow food entrepreneur, a financial advisor, someone who's run a small business before — already has the knowledge or relationships that would accelerate your path, and what would it look like to bring them into this more deliberately?
4. **Culinary-to-business crossover**: Think about a time when your culinary instincts — knowing what a dish needed, reading a kitchen's rhythm, improvising under pressure — translated directly into a smart business decision. What was happening, and what does that tell you about the strengths you're already bringing to this venture?
5. **The morning ritual**: Imagine it's six months in — what does a typical Tuesday look like for this solo founder, from commissary kitchen prep at 5 a.m. through the final cash count at night, and which moments in that day feel exhilarating versus quietly draining?
