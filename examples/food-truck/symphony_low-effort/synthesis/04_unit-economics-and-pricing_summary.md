# Summary: Unit Economics and Pricing

## Executive Summary

The cluster's organizing tension is that the fusion menu's specialty ingredients push realistic food cost above what simple taco concepts face, while the truck's community-brand positioning creates pressure to keep prices accessible. A feasibility-oriented view holds that the math is solvable: designate ube horchata and one high-volume hand-held as margin anchors, run a tighter 28% internal ceiling rather than 30%, add a $9–10 rice bowl as the accessible entry point, and use catering revenue to partially subsidize street-vending prices. A risk-oriented counterpoint is that this model assumes stable specialty-ingredient supply chains, commissary cost estimates that hold, and launch-phase food cost at steady-state levels—none of which is safe to assume. The feasibility path is coherent in structure. The risk concern is about the inputs, not the structure.

Both lenses converge strongly on two points: the break-even model must be built from actual supplier quotes and real commissary time-clock data before opening, not after, and premium pricing without a deliberate access mechanism will create a reputational problem for a brand that claims community identity. The divergence is sharpest on timing and sequencing. A feasibility-oriented view supports opening at $11–13 and testing elasticity through observed repurchase behavior in the first 30 days. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues for launching with a lean, moderate-priced menu first to build repeat customers, then adding premium items once a baseline traffic pattern is established. These are compatible strategies only if the founder decides upfront which goal year one is actually optimizing for—cash survival or customer-base building—because the menu structure and price ladder differ meaningfully depending on that answer.

A unique risk-side contribution that neither lens fully shared: specialty-ingredient supply fragility. Ube is import-dependent and seasonally variable; building the margin engine around it without a second-source supplier or shelf-stable extract identified in advance is a structural single point of failure, not just a cost-modeling concern. The feasibility lens noted higher raw costs for specialty ingredients but did not flag supply chain continuity as a distinct risk category.

## Key Themes

### Specialty Ingredients Require a Different Cost Model
Both lenses agreed that Filipino-Mexican fusion does not share the bulk-purchase economics of a standard taco truck. Expect 8–12% higher raw food cost per item than commodity ingredients, and model the launch phase at 38–42% food cost (not the 28–32% steady-state target) to avoid a cash surprise in the first three months. The risk-oriented view adds that fusion ingredients may never fully reach bulk pricing at single-truck volumes. The food cost gap versus commodity concepts may be a permanent structural feature rather than a ramp-up artifact.

### Break-Even Math Requires Explicit, Tracked Thresholds
Both lenses converged on the need for a daily P&L model with a named cover threshold ("net-positive day = 35 covers") tracked weekly from day one. The feasibility view provides the clearest mechanics: at a $13 average ticket and 30% food cost, each cover generates ~$9.10 gross margin, and daily fixed costs of $145–195 imply a 16–22 cover floor just to reach zero before owner draw. The risk view adds that the fixed cost list commonly undercounts. Credit card processing (2.5–3% of gross), commissary prep overruns on a complex fusion menu, and spoilage on an unproven menu are all real and frequently omitted. Contribution margin per service day is a more useful diagnostic than a binary break-even/loss framing.

### Premium Pricing and Community Identity Are in Active Tension
This is the cluster's sharpest divergence. The feasibility lens frames the $8 lunch segment as not the primary addressable market at launch and argues that a $9 rice bowl plus strategic location near mixed-income lunch destinations is sufficient to maintain community credibility. The risk-oriented counterpoint is more pointed: a brand explicitly positioned around Filipino-American culture that prices out working-class Filipino-American customers risks a reputational backlash that good marketing cannot fully defuse. Whether a structural access mechanism (rice bowl, EBT/SNAP, loyalty discount) is sufficient community signal or reads as a token gesture at a premium price ladder is not resolvable through unit economics alone.

### Revenue Segmentation Is the Feasibility Lever
A unique feasibility-oriented contribution: catering and corporate event pricing ($16–18 per head) can do structural work in the business model, not just supplemental work. Catering margin partially subsidizes street-vending prices at $12–13 rather than forcing the street menu to carry full margin recovery. This framing shifts the question from "can we make the street menu pencil out at accessible prices?" to "can we build a mixed-revenue model where catering margin buys down street pricing?" The risk lens did not address this mechanism.

### Supply Chain Fragility Is a Distinct Risk Category
A unique risk-oriented contribution: ube's import dependency and seasonal variability make it a fragile anchor for the truck's margin engine. This is separate from the food cost modeling question and represents a business continuity risk. Identifying a second-source supplier or shelf-stable extract before launch is a concrete mitigation for the pre-opening checklist, not a contingency to address when supply disruption actually occurs.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Build a per-SKU cost model from actual supplier quotes (not category benchmarks) before finalizing the menu, including ube, lumpia wrappers, and adobo proteins at realistic single-truck order volumes. [convergent]
- Set an internal food cost ceiling at 28% (not 30%), and plan cash reserves assuming 38–42% actual food cost for the first 90 days. [convergent]
- Identify a second-source ube supplier or shelf-stable ube extract before opening; document it in the pre-launch checklist. [unique: risk]
- Time-clock actual commissary prep sessions for the full fusion menu before finalizing daily fixed cost estimates; fusion prep runs materially longer than simple taco menus. [unique: risk]
- Define the "net-positive day" cover threshold (e.g., 35 covers = $455 gross at $13 average ticket) and track it visibly from day one. [convergent]

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Track weekly actual-vs.-target food cost from receipts for the first 60 days; use this data to confirm or revise the margin-anchor item selection. [convergent]
- After 30 days of operation, review repurchase rates and basket size: if customers are returning weekly and adding drinks, test a $1 price increase on one core item; if repeat visits are low, hold prices and investigate other variables. [unique: feasibility]
- Add a $9–10 rice bowl as a permanent menu item positioned explicitly as the accessible entry point (not a discount or promotion, but a structurally lower-cost item that holds margin in dollar terms through volume). [trade-off: feasibility case for this as sufficient community access signal; risk-oriented counterpoint that it may not defuse premium-pricing reputational risk if the overall menu reads as not-for-us to working-class Filipino-American customers]
- Negotiate commissary rental on a monthly rather than daily rate as soon as volume patterns are established; a $200/month reduction shifts break-even by ~20–25 covers per month. [unique: feasibility]

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Build catering and corporate event revenue explicitly into the business model as a margin subsidy for street pricing. Target $16–18 per head at events, allowing street-vending prices to hold at $12–13 without requiring full margin recovery from street sales. [unique: feasibility]
- Revisit the community-access question after 12 months with actual customer data: who is buying, at what frequency, and from what locations. Assess whether the current access mechanisms (rice bowl, loyalty structure, event pricing) are achieving the community-embedding goal or serving a different customer segment than intended. [trade-off]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- Food cost for fusion specialty ingredients will run structurally higher than commodity taco concepts; model this explicitly by SKU, not by category average.
- Launch-phase food cost will run 38–42% for the first 90 days regardless of steady-state targets; cash reserves must absorb this without touching SBA loan funds.
- A named, tracked daily cover threshold is essential from day one; operating without it produces the common outcome of feeling busy without knowing profitability.
- A deliberate price-accessible menu item is necessary to maintain community-brand credibility at premium price points.
- The break-even model commonly undercounts real fixed costs; credit card processing fees, commissary prep overruns, and spoilage must be itemized, not treated as rounding error.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility case for opening at $11–13 and using observed repurchase behavior to test elasticity within the first 30 days, versus a risk-oriented counterpoint that launching at a moderate price with a lean menu first (then adding premium items after a repeat-customer base is established) reduces the risk of being stranded in a price bracket that does not recover costs once traffic patterns clarify.
- A feasibility case that a $9 rice bowl plus strategic location is sufficient community-access signal even at a $12–14 overall menu, versus a risk-oriented counterpoint that this reads as a token gesture to a brand audience that may judge the overall price ladder as culturally extractive rather than community-embedded.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- Ube supply chain fragility as a distinct business continuity risk, not just a food cost variable — second-source supplier identification is a pre-launch task, not a contingency. [risk-oriented]
- Revenue segmentation: catering margin as a structural subsidy for street pricing, not merely supplemental revenue. [feasibility-oriented]
- Contribution margin per service day as the correct diagnostic for distinguishing a fixed-cost problem from a sales-volume problem. [risk-oriented]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- *Relational/social*: Neither lens addressed the supplier relationship dimension. A solo founder with no prior business ownership may lack the vendor relationships or negotiating leverage to achieve even the "realistic" specialty-ingredient pricing both lenses use as inputs. Food cost targets are also a function of who you know, not just market prices.
- *Equity/access/distributional*: The price-elasticity discussion stayed at the level of customer segments and brand risk. Neither lens addressed whether EBT/SNAP mobile vending is operationally feasible under the city's current permit structure, or what the actual income distribution looks like in the candidate vending zones. Both determine whether the $9 rice bowl actually reaches the customers the access argument assumes it will.
- *Lifecycle/temporal-stage*: Both lenses implicitly modeled the truck as if it will operate in a stable environment. Neither addressed how pending downtown vending ordinance changes could shift fixed costs (new licensing fees) or accessible locations mid-year-one. This timing risk is directly relevant to whether the break-even model remains valid six months after it is built.
