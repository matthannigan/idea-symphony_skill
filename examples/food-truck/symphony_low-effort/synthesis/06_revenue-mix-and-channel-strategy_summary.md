# Summary: Revenue Mix and Channel Strategy

## Executive Summary

The central tension in this cluster is an inversion: catering is the channel most likely to smooth cash flow and build resilience, yet the brand recognition that makes catering bookings possible is built through consistent street presence — the very thing catering undermines when it crowds out key lunch days in months 4–8. Both lenses recognized this dynamic, but drew opposite conclusions about how to manage it. A feasibility-oriented view recommends a practical middle path: accept catering only on days when street vending wasn't planned anyway, protect the regular street schedule for the first six months, and treat each channel as a stage gate that the prior channel's profitability unlocks. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues the stage-gate framing still overstates what a solo founder can manage. Year one should be treated as a single-channel year with only inbound catering accepted, building the pipeline deliberately in year two once operations are stable.

Both views converged on the honest shape of year-one revenue: months 1–4 are likely near-single-stream street vending, catering pipelines take 3–6 months to build from cold, and the "diversification" story in early projections is probably wrong. They also agreed that festival economics deserve hard scrutiny. Booth fees, insurance riders, staffing, and spoilage can render festival shifts break-even or negative, and a go/no-go scorecard with a minimum net-margin threshold is the right discipline before accepting bookings. On the community events question, both lenses agreed the fusion framing should be stress-tested directly with Filipino and Latino organizations before pursuing cultural event catering, and that any community pricing tier must be modeled explicitly rather than treated as a values intention that won't affect the numbers.

Where they genuinely diverged is on the distraction question. The feasibility-oriented view argues a "minimum viable catering" structure (fixed menu, groups of 25–75, $600 minimum spend) captures real revenue without requiring a second operational playbook. Q1 street-first with Q2 catering onboarding is operationally achievable. The risk-oriented view treats any active catering outreach in year one as execution risk that multiplies the already high cognitive load on a solo founder learning commercial kitchen management, permits, and cash flow simultaneously.

## Key Themes

### The Catering Margin Illusion
Both lenses agreed that catering looks high-margin until fully loaded. A $3,000 booking can net less per hour than a busy lunch shift once commissary rental, helpers, event insurance, unpaid proposal time, and follow-up are included. The practical response both views endorsed: build a rate card that enforces a minimum net-margin threshold before accepting any booking. Measure actual realized margin per event before scaling the catering calendar.

### Year-One Revenue Has Four Quiet Months
Both lenses converged on the same structural warning: a year-one model showing three channels contributing every month is probably wrong. With catering pipelines taking 3–6 months to build and festival organizers booking 6–12 months in advance, months 1–4 are likely almost entirely street vending. Both views recommended explicitly stress-testing a scenario where catering doesn't generate meaningful revenue until month 7. This changes the loan decision and burn-rate math. The conservative revenue figure (one estimate: ~$55K rather than $100K if two channels stall simultaneously) should be the number against which fixed costs are sized.

### Street Presence as Brand Infrastructure
Both lenses identified a structural dependency: catering desirability for a fusion concept rests on brand recognition the client base has to build first. A feasibility-oriented view treats this as a scheduling problem solvable by protecting the regular street calendar. A risk-oriented counterpoint treats it as a reason to hold a hard monthly cap on catering days in year one (no more than four) regardless of inbound demand.

### Channel Proliferation Multiplies Execution Risk
Both lenses agreed that three channels in year one is effectively three businesses, each with its own sales motion, relationship-building cycle, and logistics demands. They diverged on the remedy: a feasibility-oriented view proposes dominating one channel per quarter (Q1 street, Q2 first catering bookings, Q3 selective fall events) as a practical sequencing framework; a risk-oriented view holds that any active multi-channel pursuit before operations are stable is the wrong call for a solo founder with no prior business ownership.

### Community Event Catering Requires Explicit Financial Modeling
Both lenses treated the community-capacity question as a real budget decision, not a values aspiration. Holding two Saturdays per month for community events at below-market rates represents an estimated $10,000–$15,000 in foregone income annually on a $100K capital base. Both views endorsed requiring the same deposit and lead-time discipline from community clients as from corporate ones and evaluating the return on community bookings before committing to a permanent pricing tier. A risk-oriented flag added that the fusion framing itself may be a poor fit for cultural heritage events where authenticity, not novelty, is the organizing value.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Build an explicit month-by-month cash projection with street vending as the floor and catering/festivals as upside; stress-test a scenario where catering generates no meaningful revenue until month 7 — size fixed costs (commissary contract, loan payment, insurance) against the conservative number. [convergent]
- Establish a festival go/no-go scorecard (expected covers, booth cost, staffing requirement, net margin threshold) before accepting any event booking; treat year-one festivals as a marketing budget line rather than revenue, and accept only the highest-traffic events. [convergent]
- Build an email/text opt-in list from opening day; a simple "text to get location updates" program costs almost nothing and creates a direct channel that survives permit changes, algorithm shifts, and ordinance restrictions. [unique: feasibility]

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Introduce catering only on days when street vending was not planned (weekday evenings, Sundays) for the first six months; define a minimum viable catering offering — fixed menu, 25–75 guests, $600 minimum spend, no dietary customization — before accepting bookings. [trade-off: a feasibility case for capturing inbound catering revenue via a constrained offering; a risk-oriented counterpoint that any active catering outreach before month 6 multiplies solo-founder execution risk and should be deferred]
- Cap catering days at no more than four per month in year one regardless of inbound demand, to protect the street presence that makes catering desirable. [convergent]
- Before pursuing Filipino or Latino cultural event catering, do direct outreach to community organizations to ask explicitly whether the fusion format fits their events; do not assume the street brand translates to a heritage celebration context. [convergent]
- For any community-rate bookings, model the discount explicitly in the financial plan, require the same 3–4 week lead time and 25% deposit as corporate clients, and evaluate at year-end whether those bookings produced measurable downstream value before committing to a permanent community pricing tier. [convergent]

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Treat each channel as a stage gate financed by the prior channel's profitability: street vending profitability unlocks catering investment (rate card, catering kit), catering profitability unlocks festival applications (booth equipment, staffing capacity). [unique: feasibility]
- If community event catering is pursued at scale, consider a deliberate cross-subsidy structure — a small surcharge on corporate bookings that funds a "community rate" reserve — rather than an informal discount that quietly erodes margins. [unique: risk]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- Catering margin must be fully loaded (commissary, helpers, insurance, unpaid proposal time) before comparing to street vending margin. The apparent margin advantage often shrinks significantly.
- Year-one revenue projections should be built around a conservative scenario where two of three channels are silent for extended periods. Month-by-month modeling with explicit stress testing is not optional.
- Festival bookings require a numerical go/no-go gate. "Brand exposure" is a real value but must be tracked separately from revenue and should not rationalize economically negative events.
- The fusion framing should be tested directly with Filipino and Latino community organizations before cultural event catering is pursued.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility case for a minimum viable catering offering in Q2 (constrained menu, size limits, no customization) as a way to capture revenue without building a second operational playbook; a risk-oriented counterpoint that any active catering outreach before operations are stable adds execution risk a solo founder cannot absorb, and inbound-only acceptance is the safer boundary.
- A feasibility case for a quarter-by-quarter channel introduction (Q1 street, Q2 catering, Q3 events) as manageable sequencing; a risk-oriented counterpoint that multi-channel development before the core operation has a proven identity is structurally the same mistake whether it happens sequentially or simultaneously — the cognitive load accumulates regardless.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- Catering bookings in months 4–8 may cannibalize the street presence window that makes catering desirable. A hard monthly cap of four catering days in year one protects that window. [risk-oriented]
- Building a direct-contact customer list from day one (email/text opt-in) creates a channel-agnostic resilience asset that survives permit changes, ordinance restrictions, and slow festival seasons at near-zero cost. [feasibility-oriented]
- The "existing social capital" critique of high-margin clients can become a values-based reason to avoid the revenue that would fund community service. Aggressively pursuing corporate and event catering to cross-subsidize community bookings may be the most mission-aligned structure. [risk-oriented]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- **Equity/access/distributional**: Neither lens examined who on the team absorbs the physical and emotional cost of high-volume festival shifts and late catering events. Solo-founder burnout is a distributional question, not just an operational one. A lived-experience lens would ask what the working conditions of the business look like at month 8, not just the margin profile.
- **Relational/social**: Both lenses treated community relationships instrumentally (referral value, retention rate). Neither examined what it means reputationally to enter a Filipino or Latino community event space as a fusion brand. The social dynamics of being perceived as an outsider to one's own heritage, or of being celebrated for it, are not captured in a go/no-go scorecard.
- **Lifecycle/temporal-stage**: Neither lens fully grappled with the asymmetry between channel-building timelines and capital runway. The business may exhaust its stress-test buffer before catering pipelines mature, meaning the sequencing framework presupposes a solvency condition it cannot guarantee.
