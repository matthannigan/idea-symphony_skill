# Summary: Equipment, Commissary, and Seasonal Cash Flow

## Executive Summary

The lease-vs.-purchase question has a different right answer depending on the failure mode you're most worried about. A feasibility-oriented view argues for leasing during the first 12–18 months to preserve cash, buy time to validate the concept, and strengthen the loan application when you do purchase. A risk-oriented counterpoint flags that a $2,000/month lease payment becomes a proportionally larger fixed drain precisely during a trough month at 30–45% of peak revenue. Ownership, once the loan is retired, eliminates that recurring obligation entirely. Both lenses converge on uncomfortable arithmetic: either path may foreclose truck two within three years, and neither path should be chosen without modeling what a lender will actually require for that second asset.

Both lenses agreed that commissary scheduling conflicts are a genuine ceiling, not a hypothetical. Prime prep windows in a high-demand market fill up, and a catering business that can't book Friday morning prep slots is capped by the commissary schedule, not by client demand. They also converged on treating the commissary as more than a compliance cost. The operator network inside a shared kitchen is a low-cost intelligence channel for event leads, payment-history intel, and vending-spot availability.

On cash reserves, both perspectives arrived at the same warning from different directions. The apparent $60K starting pool shrinks dramatically after launch costs, and the SBA microloan shifts from optional to structural once you run the real numbers. A risk-oriented view stresses that cold-climate trough seasons can run five months, not two or three, and that behavioral surplus spending in summer can silently drain the winter reserve. A feasibility-oriented view frames the SBA loan not as debt for the truck but as the mechanism that maintains a survivable buffer (roughly $20K) after launch costs are paid. The two stances converge on a single imperative: model the fixed-cost floor in absolute dollars before committing to any launch cost.

## Key Themes

### Fixed-Cost Stack vs. Trough Revenue

Both lenses identified the same structural risk. Loan payments, commissary minimums, insurance, and equipment costs land monthly regardless of whether revenue is $5,000 or $20,000. A feasibility-oriented view puts the monthly fixed floor at $3,400–6,600 and recommends calculating it from actual contract terms before setting a reserve target. A risk-oriented view adds that if the fixed floor is $4,500 and trough revenue is $5,000, the margin before reserve drawdown is $500. A single slow week wipes it out. Both lenses treat knowing this number as a prerequisite, not a refinement.

### The Commissary Scheduling Ceiling

Convergent across both lenses: commissary utilization rates and scheduling terms must be investigated before signing, not after. A feasibility-oriented view recommends negotiating first-right-of-refusal on additional time blocks and building a 15% annual cost escalator into the 3-year model. A risk-oriented counterpoint adds that even a negotiated rate-cap clause leaves you exposed after year one or two. Identifying a backup commissary in advance is the only way to negotiate from a non-compliance-trapped position.

### The Commissary as Community Asset

Both lenses independently surfaced this point: other operators in the commissary are a direct intelligence network. This is the clearest convergent theme in the cluster. It surfaces event leads, vending spot availability, and catering client reputation data faster than any marketing spend. The practical implication is showing up in person for prep time, not just using the kitchen and leaving.

### Lease-vs.-Purchase and the Path to Truck Two

A feasibility-oriented case for leasing first: it validates the concept on real revenue data before a purchase decision. The break-even on ownership doesn't arrive until roughly month 24 anyway. A risk-oriented counterpoint: a lease obligation reduces debt service capacity for a second truck loan, and if the first truck is purchased and still depreciating, lenders may require more operating history than a three-year plan assumes. Both lenses agree the path to truck two must be modeled explicitly in year one rather than assumed to emerge organically.

### Behavioral Reserve Risk in Peak Months

Unique to the risk-oriented lens: strong summer months create the subjective sense that the business is working, which can trigger spending on marketing, menu expansion, or fleet research at precisely the moment reserves should be accumulating for winter. The mitigation is specific and actionable. Automate a transfer of at least 20% of weekly net revenue into a segregated winter reserve account. The feasibility lens addressed seasonal smoothing through catering and event revenue rather than behavioral controls, treating winter gap-filling as a sales activity to pursue in summer, not a savings discipline problem.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0–3 months)

- Before signing any truck purchase or lease, build a launch cost itemization (truck, permits, initial inventory, commissary deposits, marketing) and treat the gap between $60K and that total as the real operating reserve number — not $60K minus the truck price. [convergent]
- Map every fixed monthly obligation (loan/lease, commissary, insurance, fuel baseline, any employees) to a dollar amount; sum them; treat that total as the minimum monthly revenue floor below which reserves are being consumed. [convergent]
- Before signing a commissary agreement, request current utilization rates by day-of-week and time block, and negotiate either preferred scheduling rights or a rate-lock clause for at least 12–24 months. [convergent]
- Open a segregated winter reserve account and automate a transfer of at least 20% of weekly net revenue into it from the first service day; require deliberate action to access it. [unique: risk]

### Near-term (3–12 months)

- During summer peak months, actively pitch corporate lunch programs, holiday party catering slots, and Q4/Q1 indoor event bookings — catering clients book 2–3 months out, so the sales effort must happen when cash flow feels least urgent. [unique: feasibility]
- Identify a backup commissary before you need one; never be in a position where losing the primary commissary means losing your health permit compliance with no alternative. [unique: risk]
- If leasing the truck, revisit the purchase decision at month 12–18 using actual revenue data to strengthen the loan application and validate the concept before concentrating capital. [trade-off: a feasibility case for deferring purchase until revenue is proven; a risk-oriented counterpoint that a 24-month lease obligation at $2,000/month costs $48K with no asset and no exit, so the deferral window should be bounded, not open-ended]

### Long-term (1–3 years)

- Before committing to a second full truck, test fleet expansion with a permitted catering trailer or event cart ($8–15K) that generates demand data without requiring a commissary slot or full vending permit. [unique: feasibility]
- Model the path to truck two in year one's business plan, working backward from lender requirements for revenue history and debt service coverage — do not assume scale becomes available organically. [convergent]
- At the 3-year mark, investigate commissary co-ops or restaurant subleasing to reduce per-truck commissary cost; build the relationships now that make those options available later. [unique: feasibility]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- The monthly fixed-cost floor must be calculated in absolute dollars from actual contracts; the reserve target follows from that number, not from a generic rule of thumb.
- Commissary scheduling conflicts are a real ceiling in active markets — utilization data and scheduling rights must be negotiated before signing, not assumed to be available.
- The commissary operator network is a genuine competitive asset; show up in person for prep time and treat those relationships as market research.
- The SBA microloan is structurally necessary for most founder profiles at this capital level — it funds the survival margin, not the truck.
- The path to truck two must be modeled explicitly, not assumed to emerge from success.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility case for leasing the first truck to preserve capital and exit flexibility during concept validation; a risk-oriented counterpoint that a $2,000/month lease payment becomes a proportionally larger drain during the trough months it was meant to survive, and that ownership eliminates that monthly obligation once the loan is retired. Neither path is clearly dominant without knowing the specific trough duration and fixed-cost floor.
- A feasibility case for a 5-month cold-climate trough being manageable through proactive catering sales in summer; a risk-oriented counterpoint that four to five slow months (not two to three) is the realistic northern-market scenario, and that the reserve must be sized for the conservative case or it will be exhausted before spring.
- A feasibility case that the SBA loan funds the reserve buffer and makes the business survivable; a risk-oriented counterpoint that drawing on SBA principal for operating losses may violate loan covenants and signals distress to future lenders — the backstop should be a separate personal line of credit, not the SBA principal itself.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- Behavioral surplus risk: peak-season cash flow creates a subjective sense of stability that drives spending on expansion precisely when reserves should be accumulating; an automated, segregated transfer mechanism is the structural fix. [unique: risk]
- Trailer/cart as minimum viable fleet test: a $8–15K permitted trailer generates fleet-expansion demand data without a commissary slot, full permit, or second operator. [unique: feasibility]
- Resale risk on a custom-built truck: a niche fusion concept with a custom build-out may recover only 40–60 cents on the dollar in a forced sale; favor standard equipment configurations. [unique: risk]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- *Equity/access/distributional*: neither lens addressed whether the Filipino-Mexican fusion positioning creates any particular labor market considerations — whether family or community labor is part of the model, and what that means for the cost floor and reserve math if that labor source is unavailable or needs to be compensated.
- *Relational/social*: neither lens addressed the founder's personal financial exposure during the trough — whether a partner income, personal savings runway, or community-supported pre-sale arrangement (e.g., catering deposits or CSA-style meal bundles) provides a backstop that the business plan can rely on without formalizing as a loan.
