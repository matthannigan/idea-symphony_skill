# Brainstorming Responses: Safety, Legal Compliance & Institutional Protection

---

## Question 1: What should our minimum disqualifying criteria be for volunteer background checks, and how should we handle borderline cases like old non-violent offenses or records that were later expunged?

* **Adopt MENTOR/AIM's three-tier disqualification framework as your baseline.** Any offense involving violence, sexual misconduct, or crimes against minors is a permanent automatic bar — no exceptions, no waivers. Drug or theft offenses within the past 7 years are a presumptive bar reviewable by a committee. Older non-violent offenses go to a case-by-case review using a written rubric: time elapsed, demonstrated rehabilitation evidence, nature of the victim relationship. This tiered approach is defensible because it distinguishes genuine risk from bureaucratic over-exclusion, and it's auditable — every decision is documented with reasoning.

* **Require fingerprint-based checks through your state's criminal justice repository, not just a commercial database search.** Commercial checks like Checkr miss arrests that didn't result in convictions, out-of-state records that weren't reported to the national index, and sex offender registry entries in states without data-sharing agreements. Budget roughly $35-60 per volunteer for the state-FBI channel. Because you're serving juveniles referred through juvenile justice, funders and liability insurers will expect the more rigorous standard — this is where your $15K seed money should go before you spend on anything else.

* **Create a volunteer review panel for borderline cases — three people, written decision, 30-day maximum turnaround.** Include one person with social work or legal background, one congregant elder, and your program director. Give them a written scoring rubric: offense type (0-3 points), years since offense (0-3 points), demonstrated change evidence — letters, sustained employment, community ties (0-3 points). Anyone scoring 7+ passes. Below 5 is a no. The middle band goes to a second conversation with the applicant. This matters because blanket exclusions of people with old records unnecessarily narrow your pool and can feel hypocritical in a community where many families have justice-involved members.

* **Expunged records legally cannot be considered in most states — honor that, document that you honor it.** Train your screening staff to stop asking about expunged records on the application form. If a background check vendor returns expunged data (which some do, illegally), have a written protocol to set that data aside and not use it. This protects you legally and builds trust in communities where over-policing has created justified skepticism about background check processes. Your policy document should explicitly state "We do not consider records that have been legally expunged."

* **Revisit screens every two years for active mentors, not just at onboarding.** Someone who passes a clean check on Day 1 could acquire a DUI or domestic violence charge in Year 2. A biennial re-screen — or a continuous monitoring subscription at ~$5/month per person — catches this. Because your mentors are in sustained one-on-one relationships with minors, you have more exposure than a one-time volunteer event. Frame this to mentors as a program standard, not a sign of distrust.

## Question 2: Should mentor-mentee meetings always be held in public spaces, or are there specific circumstances where private or home settings are appropriate and defensible?

* **Default to observable public spaces for all meetings, defined precisely in your policy.** "Public" means a place where bystanders are present and at least one other adult can see the pair — a coffee shop, library study room with a window, park during daylight. "Observable" is the operative word, not "public" per se. A private room in your church with a glass door is observable and appropriate; a closed basement room is not. Write this definition into your policy and train mentors to assess locations before sitting down. This matters because ambiguous policies leave well-meaning mentors making ad hoc calls that create liability exposure.

* **Permit home visits under a specific two-adult rule, not a blanket prohibition.** Some teens live in homes where the parent or guardian is present and the home visit is the most relationship-building option — meeting someone on their own turf, for dinner, for a family event. Allow this only when: (a) a parent/guardian is present in the home for the duration, (b) the mentor notifies the program coordinator with address and time beforehand, and (c) the visit is logged within 24 hours. This keeps home settings defensible because there is always a second adult present and a documentation trail — two factors that deter misconduct and establish accountability.

* **Use check-in technology to create an audit trail for all meetings.** Have mentors text a program-designated number with "Start: [location] with [mentee first name]" when they arrive and "End: [duration]" when they leave. A volunteer coordinator reviews logs weekly. This adds almost zero burden — one text per meeting — but creates a record that shows where meetings happen, for how long, and establishes a habit of accountability. If a parent later raises a concern about a meeting location, you have documentation. If a mentor is meeting somewhere off-policy, the pattern shows up in the logs.

* **Treat vehicle travel as a special case requiring prior approval and specific safeguards.** Mentors driving mentees to events — games, college visits, job shadows — is high-value mentoring activity. But an enclosed vehicle alone with a minor is also the highest-risk configuration. Require: route shared with a parent and coordinator before departure, front seat only (never alone in back), no unplanned detours, and confirmation text when the mentee is dropped home. If possible, have a second adult (another mentor, a sibling) ride along for any trip over 30 minutes. Your liability policy almost certainly has language about transporting minors — read it carefully and align your transport policy to whatever coverage it requires.

* **Sunset ambiguous location policies with a 6-month annual review.** What counts as an "appropriate public space" shifts — a favorite diner closes, a library branch cuts hours, a park becomes unsafe seasonally. Convene your safety committee each spring to audit where meetings are actually happening (from your logs), flag any patterns that look off-policy, and update the approved location examples in your policy. This keeps the policy grounded in real geography rather than becoming a document no one applies.

## Question 3: What level of liability insurance is appropriate for a program of our size, and should we carry a separate sexual misconduct policy in addition to general liability?

* **Yes, carry a separate sexual misconduct and abuse (SMA) policy — this is non-negotiable for one-on-one youth work.** General liability policies routinely exclude abuse and molestation claims, often in small-print exclusions that program directors miss until a claim is filed. A standalone SMA policy costs roughly $800-1,500/year at your scale (40 volunteers, under 40 youth). Shop through nonprofit specialty brokers like Philadelphia Contributionship, Nonprofits Insurance Alliance, or your state's nonprofit association insurance pool. Frame this to your board as the cost of the program being operable — without it, a single claim could end the organization, not just the program.

* **Target $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate on general liability as your floor.** At your size — 40 volunteers, community-based, no owned vehicles — this is the standard nonprofit expectation and what most grant funders require to list you as an additional insured. If your church's existing general liability policy covers programs, verify in writing that it covers volunteer activities with minors that are not church-sponsored worship activities — many policies have carve-outs for affiliated nonprofits running separate programs. Get this in writing from your broker before launch.

* **Require your volunteers to carry personal auto insurance with minimum $100K/$300K limits if they transport mentees.** Your organizational policy won't cover a volunteer's personal vehicle in most configurations. Collect proof of insurance annually from any volunteer who transports youth. If a volunteer can't meet this threshold, they don't drive mentees — period. This transfers some liability back to the volunteer's own insurer and establishes that you verified coverage, which matters in any subsequent litigation.

* **Budget for a one-time legal review of your policy documents by a nonprofit attorney.** A $500-800 attorney review of your volunteer agreement, parental consent form, and program policies is likely the best risk-management dollar you'll spend. They'll flag gaps your insurance won't cover — like indemnification language, or whether your policies create a duty of care standard you can't actually meet. Many community foundations that fund programs like yours can connect you to pro bono legal counsel, or your local bar association has nonprofit law sections that offer discounted reviews.

* **Re-evaluate coverage annually as the program grows.** At 40 volunteers and under 40 mentees, you're a small program. If you double in Year 2, your SMA premium and aggregate limits should increase proportionally. Build an annual insurance review into your board calendar — October, before most policy renewal periods — with a checklist: current volunteer count, incident report review, and a 15-minute call with your broker. The worst outcome is discovering your coverage lapsed or underrepresented your program size after a claim is filed.

## Question 4: How do we obtain informed consent from teens referred through juvenile justice, given the power dynamics and the privacy stakes of their records?

* **Separate consent for program participation from consent to share juvenile justice information — these are two distinct decisions.** A teen (and their guardian) should be able to say yes to mentorship without authorizing the program to access their court records. Create two separate forms: one for program enrollment (required) and one for limited record access if you need it at all (optional). Most mentorship programs don't actually need to read court files — they need to know the teen's availability, any active probation conditions that affect scheduling, and whether there are victims who require geographic separation. Get only what you need.

* **Hold a consent conversation with the teen alone, separate from the referring probation officer.** When a juvenile justice officer is in the room during a consent conversation, teens read coercion into the situation even when none is intended. "Your PO brought you here and is watching you sign this" does not produce informed consent. Instead, have a program staff member meet one-on-one with the teen after an initial introduction to explain what the program does, what is confidential, what is not (mandatory reporting), and what happens if they want to leave. Then, and only then, present the enrollment form. This takes 20 extra minutes and meaningfully changes the quality of consent you receive.

* **Use plain-language forms written at a 6th-grade reading level, and read them aloud for teens who struggle with literacy.** Consent forms written in legal language are not actually consent — they're a signature on a document the person didn't understand. Rewrite your forms using the Health Literacy Measures criteria: short sentences, active voice, no jargon. For "confidentiality," say "what we keep private and what we have to tell others." For "mandatory reporting," say "if you tell us you are being hurt or plan to hurt yourself or someone else, we are required by law to report that." Field-test the forms with two or three teens before launch.

* **Engage the teen's guardian or parent in a separate conversation from the teen's.** Parents of justice-involved youth may have their own complicated relationship with systems — schools, courts, social services — that makes them suspicious of consent forms from yet another institution. Call them, don't just send paperwork. Explain specifically what data you collect, who sees it, how long you keep it, and how they can pull their child from the program at any time with no consequences. A 15-minute phone call before sending forms increases form return rates and converts skeptical guardians into program allies.

* **Build in a 72-hour cooling-off period before enrollment is finalized.** Don't accept signatures in the same meeting where you introduce the program. Give teens and families the forms to take home, sit with, and return — or not. This eliminates any implicit pressure of in-room signature-gathering and is consistent with how more formal research consent processes handle vulnerable populations. It also self-selects for teens who are actually willing participants, rather than those who signed because a PO drove them to your office.

## Question 5: Beyond consent, what data retention policies, access controls, and breach protocols should govern the storage of juvenile justice referral information and incident reports — particularly given FERPA and state juvenile privacy law?

* **Store juvenile justice referral data in a system entirely separate from your general program files, with role-based access limited to two people maximum.** Your program coordinator and executive director — no one else. Not volunteers, not other staff, not board members unless they have direct program oversight roles. Use a dedicated folder in a password-protected, encrypted cloud service (not a shared Google Drive folder with broad org access), and log every access event. The reason for this extreme compartmentalization: juvenile justice records in most states carry statutory penalties for unauthorized disclosure, and FERPA's protections for education records overlap with court-referred youth in ways that create compounding legal exposure.

* **Establish a 12-month active retention limit with a documented destruction process.** Once a mentorship match ends — whether the program completes successfully or the mentee exits — retain records for 12 months (for any pending legal matters or program evaluation), then destroy them using a documented process: shredding physical files, permanent deletion confirmed by a second staff member for digital files. Some states require longer retention for records involving minors; check your state's nonprofit records retention statute. The destruction date and method should be logged. Long-term retention of sensitive juvenile records with no purpose is itself a liability — it creates a larger breach surface with no offsetting benefit.

* **Write a breach response protocol before you ever collect the first record.** The protocol should specify: within 2 hours of discovering a breach, take the compromised system offline and notify the executive director. Within 24 hours, notify the organization's legal counsel and insurance carrier. Within 72 hours, notify affected families and, where required, state authorities. The protocol should live in a printed binder (because a digital breach may compromise your systems) accessible to the two people with record access. This matters because breach notification laws in most states impose timelines — violating them creates independent legal exposure on top of the breach itself.

* **Audit access logs quarterly and treat any unexplained access as a potential incident.** If your records system shows a login at 11pm on a Saturday by an account that normally logs in during business hours, treat that as a potential incident and investigate before the next board meeting. Quarterly audits take about 30 minutes and should be a standing agenda item for your executive director. Document the audit outcome — "reviewed Q1 access log, no anomalies" — in your board minutes. This demonstrates due diligence if you're ever asked to show your data governance practices.

* **Get explicit clarity on whether your program is a FERPA-covered entity or receives FERPA-protected records from schools.** If school counselors are sharing student records to refer teens to your program, those records may be FERPA-protected even in your hands — meaning you need a signed data-sharing agreement with the school district that specifies permitted uses, prohibits re-disclosure, and establishes your data destruction obligations. This is different from records you create yourself. Talk to a nonprofit attorney about whether your referral process triggers FERPA obligations. If it does, the data-sharing agreement with the district becomes a mandatory document before you accept a single school-referred teen.
