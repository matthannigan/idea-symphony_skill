# Brainstorming Responses: Program Design, Sustainability & Community Fit

---

## Question 14: With $15K in seed funding that will eventually run out, what revenue streams — fee-for-service referrals, government contracts, individual donor cultivation, earned income — are realistically available to a faith-based community organization of this size, and which should be pursued first?

* **Start with individual donor cultivation because it builds the relational base you already have.** Your congregation is a warm list of 40+ people who already volunteered. Launch a "Sponsor a Mentor Pair" annual fund ask at $500/pair — 30 pairs funded by congregants covers a coordinator salary. This works because the donor already knows the program personally and the cost-per-relationship is easy to visualize. Start this in month 3, before the seed grant runs out, so you have proof of community investment when you approach foundations for renewal.

* **Pursue a federal Mentoring Children of Prisoners (MCP) or Title IV-E contract once you have 12 months of outcome data.** Government per-youth contracts ($1,500–$3,000/youth/year) require documented service delivery and match a specific population you're already serving through juvenile justice referrals. The catch: you'll need a licensed social worker on staff or subcontracted and quarterly reporting capacity. Budget 6 months to write the proposal and 6 months for award processing — meaning you need bridge funding, so individual donors come first.

* **Build a fee-for-service training income stream by packaging your mentor curriculum.** Once you've run 2 cohorts, you have a tested curriculum other faith communities and nonprofits will pay to license or attend as a workshop ($200/person, 20-person cohort = $4,000/session). This is low-overhead earned income because it uses existing staff time and the church's meeting space. The prerequisite is documenting your training materials during year 1, not retrospectively. Designate one staff hour per week in year 1 to creating a curriculum binder.

* **Apply to your city or county's juvenile justice reinvestment fund before any other government source.** Cities are actively redirecting resources from incarceration toward community-based alternatives, and faith-based orgs with juvenile justice referral relationships are exactly what these funds target. Grant sizes run $25,000–$75,000 for first-time applicants. Unlike HHS contracts, these grants often don't require prior government experience — a letter of support from the juvenile court is typically enough to clear the eligibility bar.

* **Don't pursue earned income or fee-for-service before you're 18 months old.** The organizational bandwidth cost of invoicing, contract management, and compliance will cannibalize program capacity. Sequence matters: individual donors first (months 3–6), foundation grant renewals second (months 6–12), government contracts third (year 2+). Earned income is a year-3 strategy, not a year-1 lifeline — treating it as one leads to distracted leadership and mediocre program execution.

## Question 15: How does the organization navigate its faith identity in ways that welcome teens and families of all backgrounds — including those referred through secular juvenile justice channels — without requiring religious participation as a condition of program access?

* **Write a two-sentence access policy into every referral agreement: participation requires no religious belief, attendance at services, or prayer.** Put it in the MOU with the juvenile justice office before the first referral arrives. This is not a compromise of identity — it's an exercise of it. Most faith traditions have explicit teachings about serving the stranger without condition. Having the policy in writing removes ambiguity for skeptical case managers and protects families who might otherwise self-screen out.

* **Separate the program's operational home from its worship home.** Hold mentor-mentee meetings in a neutral room that doesn't have pews, a pulpit, or religious iconography visible — a classroom, a library, a community center. The church can still provide the space and pay the bills, but the teen who was referred by a probation officer shouldn't feel like they walked into a recruitment pipeline. This costs nothing; it's a room assignment decision.

* **Lead with shared values language rather than theological language in all program-facing materials.** Instead of "rooted in our faith," say "rooted in our commitment to dignity, belonging, and second chances." These values are genuine expressions of the organization's faith without being exclusionary signals. Use the theological language internally, in board meetings and congregant communications. Use the values language in intake forms, brochures, and referral packets.

* **Create a mentor briefing that explicitly addresses how to handle religious conversations.** Mentors will inevitably face moments — holidays, death in the family, a teen asking about the mentor's church attendance. Train mentors to share personally ("this is what faith means to me") without recruiting ("you should come"). Distinguish between authentic relationship and institutional pressure. Role-play this in training because the line is real and mentors need practice navigating it, not just a policy statement.

* **Use advisory board composition as a structural signal.** Seat a school counselor, a juvenile justice liaison, and ideally a parent who is not a congregant on your advisory board. Their presence signals to referring agencies that the program has genuine accountability to the secular community it serves. It also provides early warning if program culture drifts toward implicit religious pressure — because these advisors will hear about it from families before the organization does.

## Question 16: How should the program structure its ongoing relationships with school counselors and juvenile justice partners — what information flows, coordination meetings, and feedback loops maintain the referral pipeline without compromising mentor-mentee confidentiality?

* **Establish a quarterly "referral partner check-in" meeting, 45 minutes, standing agenda.** Invite school counselors and juvenile justice liaisons to a shared meeting where you report aggregate data only: number of active matches, average match duration, percentage of youth who are school-attending. No case-specific information. This serves the referral relationship because partners feel informed and valued without you violating FERPA or HIPAA. Bring a one-page program update to every meeting so partners have something tangible to share with their supervisors.

* **Use a release of information framework where youth and families control what flows to whom.** At intake, give families a tiered consent form: Tier 1 authorizes you to confirm enrollment to the referring agency; Tier 2 authorizes sharing of attendance and milestone data; Tier 3 authorizes case coordination calls. Most families will sign Tier 1; some will sign Tier 2. No tier requires you to share what the teen talks about with their mentor. This gives partners enough information to close their referral loop without you becoming a surveillance arm of the justice system.

* **Create a named liaison relationship with each referring school or agency, not a generic contact.** Assign your program coordinator as the point person for a specific counselor at each school. The counselor knows who to call; your coordinator knows who to loop in for urgent safety concerns. This one-to-one relationship means referrals happen through trust rather than through paperwork — counselors refer to people they know, not to programs they've read about. Budget two lunches per semester with each key liaison to maintain the relationship.

* **Build a "no-news-is-good-news" referral closure protocol.** Juvenile justice partners need to document what happened to each referral. Create a simple template: enrollment confirmed, match active, match closed (reason: youth aging out / geographic move / other). You send this update at 30 days, 6 months, and match close — that's it. Anything more becomes a reporting burden that erodes the relationship; anything less leaves the partner without the documentation they need for their own case files.

* **Be deliberate about what you never share, and say so explicitly in your MOU.** Specify in writing that mentor-mentee conversation content, mental health disclosures, and family conflict details are not relayed to referral partners unless a mandatory report is triggered. This protects the teen's trust in the mentor relationship — the most important asset you have — and it protects you legally. Partners will respect the boundary if you name it up front rather than awkwardly declining case-by-case.
