# Brainstorming Responses: Outcomes, Learning & Continuous Improvement

---

## Question 17: Measuring outcomes without surveillance — How do we measure program outcomes without overburdening teens with surveys or making mentors feel surveilled?

* **Embed check-ins as conversation, not clipboard.** At month 3 and month 9, give mentors a single laminated card with five open prompts — "What's something your mentee is proud of?" "What's one thing they're worried about?" — and ask mentors to jot two to three sentences in a shared Google Form after that session. This captures qualitative signal in under five minutes because mentors are already reflecting; you're just creating a container. The program coordinator reviews responses for patterns across pairs, not to evaluate any individual mentor.

* **Use school-provided proxy data with consent built into intake.** At enrollment, ask parents to authorize the program to receive termly attendance and GPA summaries directly from the school counselor who made the referral. This costs the teen nothing — no surveys, no intrusion — and gives you longitudinal data tied to a real outcome (school engagement). Because school counselors are already partners in the referral pipeline, adding a data-share agreement is a small legal step, not a new relationship.

* **Run a single annual "temperature check" session for mentees.** Host a pizza dinner for all mentees around the program anniversary. End the meal with a ten-minute anonymous paper card: three questions, five-point smiley-face scale, one open line. Physical, collective, light. Teens are more honest in a group ritual than in a formal survey because there's no individual accountability. Staff tally the cards that night and keep the raw cards for future auditors.

* **Track mentor-reported "moments of trust" as a leading indicator.** Define a "moment of trust" as any time a mentee voluntarily shared something personal, asked for advice, or initiated contact outside the scheduled meeting. Train mentors to log one of these moments per quarter — a sentence in their check-in form. This sidesteps surveillance because it's mentor self-report, not external evaluation, and it captures relationship depth far better than attendance rates.

* **Build a simple dashboard visible only to the program director, not to funders.** Aggregate the proxy and check-in data into four metrics — match retention at 6 months, school attendance trend, mentor-reported trust moments, and mentee self-reported wellbeing from the annual dinner — displayed on a private spreadsheet. The discipline of separating internal monitoring from external reporting prevents the program from shaping its data collection around what funders want to see rather than what actually matters.

## Question 18: Defining Meaningful Success Metrics — Beyond attendance rates and match duration, what indicators — school engagement, social connectedness, mentee self-reported wellbeing, mentor growth — actually reflect whether a mentorship relationship is producing the change the program exists to create?

* **Track school re-engagement as a behavior, not a grade.** For this population — 35% chronic absenteeism in local schools — the leading indicator is whether a mentee starts showing up more, not whether their GPA climbs. Work with the referring school counselor to flag if a mentee crosses the threshold from "chronically absent" (missing 10% or more of days) to "regularly attending" within the program year. This is binary, observable, and meaningful because chronic absenteeism predicts dropout more reliably than any single grade.

* **Measure social connectedness through named relationships.** At intake and at month 12, ask the mentee one question: "Name three people outside your family you could call if something went wrong." If that number grows, or if the mentor's name appears, the relationship is doing its job — because social connectedness is literally about expanding a young person's network of reliable adults. This single question costs 30 seconds and is validated by decades of youth development research on protective factors.

* **Make mentor growth a first-class metric, not an afterthought.** Survey mentors at 6 and 12 months with two questions: "Do you feel more confident navigating conversations about difficult topics with young people?" and "Has mentoring changed how you think about this community?" Mentors who report growth stay longer and become recruiters for future cohorts. Treating mentor development as an outcome — not just mentee development — also shifts the program's culture from charity toward mutual transformation.

* **Use a two-item wellbeing screen rather than a full instrument.** The PHQ-2 (two questions about mood and interest) and a single-item life satisfaction question ("On a scale of 1–10, how is life going overall?") take under 90 seconds to administer and are validated for adolescents. Administered at intake, 6 months, and 12 months, they give you a longitudinal arc without the burden of a 20-item tool — and because the questions feel like ordinary conversation, teens are less likely to game them.

* **Define a "relationship milestone" scale specific to your population.** Create a five-point scale — (1) mentee attends sessions, (2) mentee initiates contact, (3) mentee shares a personal goal, (4) mentee asks mentor to attend a school event or activity, (5) mentee credits mentor when talking to family or peers. Each rung reflects deepening trust and integration. This is a process metric, not an outcome metric, but it gives staff an early warning if a match is stalling at level 1 after three months, when there's still time to intervene.

## Question 19: Learning From Early Matches — How should the program systematically capture and act on what it learns from the first cohort of matches — including failed or ended matches — before the model ossifies into habit?

* **Treat the first cohort as a funded pilot, not a permanent program.** Frame Year 1 internally as a "design year" — tell mentors at orientation that you'll ask for their structured feedback at 3, 6, and 12 months, and that their experience will shape the program's next version. This framing creates a culture of reflection from day one because it gives mentors a role beyond "volunteer" — they're co-designers. It also makes it easier to change things without admitting failure.

* **Conduct a debrief for every ended match within 30 days of closure.** Whether a match ends by graduation, by mentee withdrawal, or by mismatch, the program coordinator conducts a 30-minute structured conversation with the mentor (and optionally the mentee, if appropriate). Four questions: What worked? What didn't? Was there a moment the match could have been saved? What would you tell your past self at the start? Aggregate these into a "match closure log" — after 10 closures you'll see patterns the onboarding process couldn't have predicted.

* **Run a mid-year "learning session" with all active mentors.** At month 6, gather all mentors for a two-hour dinner meeting. Use a structured protocol: pairs share one thing going well and one thing they're stuck on, then the group identifies common sticking points. Staff document the themes. This is faster than individual interviews and surfaces problems mentors might not report to a coordinator because they don't want to seem like they're struggling — peer normalization unlocks honesty.

* **Assign one staff person to be the "learning steward" for Year 1.** This doesn't require a new hire — it's a dedicated 20% of one coordinator's role. Their job is to maintain the match closure log, summarize the mid-year session themes, and write a 2-page "what we learned" memo before Year 2 planning begins. Without explicit ownership, learning tasks get crowded out by operational demands; naming the role protects the function.

* **Build a "not-yet list" for features you deliberately deferred.** During program design, you will make compromises — maybe you couldn't build a formal peer support group for mentors, or you couldn't afford a second intake cohort mid-year. Write these down in a living document. In Year 2 planning, revisit the list with data from Year 1: which deferred features now have evidence behind them? This prevents the program from either ignoring good ideas or adding features reactively after a single bad match.

## Question 20: Communicating Impact to Funders and Community — How does the program tell a credible, non-exploitative story about its results to the regional foundation and the congregation, given that the most powerful evidence often involves minors whose privacy must be protected?

* **Build a "composite portrait" instead of individual case studies.** Rather than profiling a real teen — even with permission — create a composite character drawn from the actual patterns across your cohort: "A teen like Marcus — referred by his school counselor after missing 40 days in seventh grade — typically improves attendance by 15% within six months of being matched." This is honest, specific, and protective because no individual is identifiable. It also forces you to describe patterns rather than cherry-pick your best case.

* **Let mentors be your public voice, not mentees.** Mentors can speak freely about their own experience — what they expected, what surprised them, what changed in them — without exposing any youth. A congregation presentation featuring three mentors describing their journeys is more emotionally compelling than statistics and carries zero privacy risk. Recruit two or three mentors willing to speak publicly at the program's six-month mark and coach them to tell their own transformation story, not the youth's.

* **Report to the foundation using a "process + trend" format, not point-in-time snapshots.** Instead of "6 of 10 mentees improved attendance," write "attendance trends for matched youth moved from a median of 72% to 84% over the program year, against a school-wide trend that held flat at 65%." This comparison — your cohort versus the school baseline — tells a credible causal story without cherry-picking, and it protects individuals because it's aggregate and longitudinal.

* **Create a "data wall" for the congregation that shows volume and momentum, not personal details.** A physical or digital display in the church showing: matches active, total hours mentored, months of match retention, and mentor satisfaction rate gives the congregation a visceral sense of scale without any individual data. Update it quarterly. People give to programs they can see running — and this format communicates momentum ("12 active matches, 1,400 hours mentored this year") more powerfully than a formal report.

* **Negotiate privacy terms with the foundation upfront, not at reporting time.** Before you begin collecting any data, send the regional foundation a one-page memo: "Here is what we will track and why; here is what we will not share publicly and why; here is the format of our annual report." Ask for their sign-off. This protects you from pressure 12 months later to produce individual stories, because you've already established the expectation — and most foundations, when asked directly, prefer institutional credibility over feel-good anecdotes.
