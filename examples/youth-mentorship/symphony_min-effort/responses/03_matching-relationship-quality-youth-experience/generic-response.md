# Brainstorming Responses: Matching, Relationship Quality & Youth Experience

---

## Question 10: What Matching Criteria Produce the Strongest Mentor-Mentee Outcomes — Demographic Similarity, Shared Interests, Personality Fit, or Something Less Obvious?

* **Mentor availability and reliability outrank identity similarity.** Research from Big Brothers Big Sisters consistently shows that match length and meeting frequency are stronger outcome predictors than race, gender, or shared hobbies. A mentor who shows up every Tuesday at 4pm for 18 months produces more measurable gains in school attendance and self-efficacy than a demographically identical mentor who meets sporadically. Build matching criteria around confirmed schedule availability — ask volunteers to name their three guaranteed weekly time windows before they're assigned anyone.

* **Shared interest in a specific activity creates a natural relational container.** When both mentor and mentee say they want to do something together (cooking, cars, music production, basketball), the activity absorbs the awkwardness of early meetings and gives the relationship a third thing to focus on. Teens from high-surveillance environments often struggle to make eye contact and hold "how was your week" conversations, but they can talk for 45 minutes while fixing a bike chain. Build a short interest inventory for both parties — 10 categories, pick top 3 — and weight matches toward activity overlap.

* **Mentee input on mentor gender and race is worth collecting, then following when feasible.** Some teens have explicit preferences based on their own history — a teen who has been harmed by men in authority may have strong reasons to prefer a female mentor. Don't override that. Collect preference data during intake and treat it as a soft constraint: match to it when possible, explain honestly when the pool doesn't allow it. This respects the teen's self-knowledge without making demographic match a hard requirement.

* **Trauma exposure level and mentor experience should be calibrated together.** A first-time volunteer mentor paired with a teen who has significant juvenile justice involvement, housing instability, and a history of placement disruptions is likely to struggle and possibly quit. Create a simple tier system: Tier 1 mentors (first-year, no prior youth work) get matched to teens with fewer compounding stressors; Tier 2 mentors (returning volunteers or those with relevant professional backgrounds) take higher-complexity matches. This protects both parties and improves retention.

* **The intake conversation itself is a matching signal.** During the intake interview, note which staff member or volunteer the teen responds to most warmly. A teen who opens up to a particular interviewer — makes jokes, asks questions back — is signaling relational compatibility. Ask that interviewer to flag what they noticed. This soft observational data, held alongside the structured inventory, catches chemistry that no questionnaire captures.

## Question 11: What's the Right Protocol When a Mentor-Mentee Pairing Isn't Working, and How Do We Transition Without Causing Harm to the Teen?

* **Distinguish "rocky adjustment" from "structural mismatch" at the 60-day mark.** Most new matches have at least one difficult month — the teen tests limits, the mentor feels frustrated, communication breaks down. Build a mandatory 60-day check-in with the program coordinator where both parties are asked separately: "What's going well? What's hard?" If both sides name the same surface friction, that's normal; coach through it. If the teen says "they don't get me" and the mentor says "I don't know how to connect," that's a structural mismatch worth acting on before six more months pass.

* **Frame termination to the teen as a program decision, not a personal rejection.** The language matters enormously because many referred teens have histories of adults leaving. Say: "We think we can find you a better fit, and that's on us to get right, not on you." Have a staff member — not the departing mentor — deliver this message in person. Avoid the mentor breaking the news via text or through a parent. Give the teen 2-3 weeks to process before introducing a new mentor name, so there's a clear gap rather than an abrupt handoff that feels like replacement.

* **Give the mentor a structured exit conversation with program staff.** Mentors who exit a failed match without debriefing often carry guilt or frustration into future volunteer roles — or don't return at all. Within a week of the match ending, have the coordinator walk through what happened, what the mentor did well, and what a different approach might look like. This debrief also surfaces program-level patterns: if three mentors with similar profiles have all struggled with similar teen profiles, that's a matching calibration problem, not individual failure.

* **Maintain a small "on-deck" mentor cohort for transition situations.** If every volunteer is already matched, a teen waiting for a new mentor after a termination has no visible option — the gap becomes open-ended and demoralizing. Aim to keep 2-3 trained, background-checked mentors in a ready state at any time, by staggering your volunteer recruitment cycles so not everyone onboards simultaneously. A 3-week maximum gap from termination to new match introduction is a reasonable program standard to build toward.

* **Use the transition period actively, not as dead time.** A teen between matches can do a group session with a staff member, attend a program event, or get paired temporarily with a senior peer mentor. This keeps the program present without forcing a new one-on-one relationship prematurely. It also signals to the teen that the program's commitment to them is institutional, not dependent on one individual mentor.

## Question 12: How Can Teens Exercise Meaningful Choice in Who They Are Matched With and What Activities They Pursue Together?

* **Run a structured "preference interview" as part of intake, not just an intake form.** A checklist handed to a 14-year-old in a waiting room produces different (and usually shallower) data than a 20-minute conversation with a trained staff member who asks follow-up questions. "What kinds of adults do you find it easiest to talk to?" and "What do you do when you actually have free time?" surface more usable matching criteria than "list your hobbies." Budget 20 minutes per teen intake for this conversation; it pays off in match quality and also signals from day one that the teen's voice matters.

* **Show teens 2-3 sanitized mentor profiles and let them react.** A profile doesn't need to be a full biography — a paragraph describing the mentor's work, what they like to do with youth, and why they signed up, plus a photo, is enough. Asking the teen "does any of these feel like someone you could talk to?" gives them an active role without exposing them to the full volunteer pool or creating a false sense of unlimited choice. This also surfaces negative reactions you want to know about: a teen who reacts strongly negatively to one profile is giving you useful data.

* **Build an explicit "activity agenda" into the first three meetings.** Give the mentor a structured question to ask in meeting one: "What's one thing you'd want to try or learn this year if someone would do it with you?" Document the answer. By meeting three, the pair should have at least one agreed-upon recurring activity — not a vague plan, but a named thing with a frequency. This habit of decision-making together about what the relationship looks like is itself an exercise in agency for a teen who is used to having systems make decisions about them.

* **Create a low-stakes escalation path for teens who want a different mentor.** Most teens won't advocate for themselves in a formal complaint process — the power differential is too high. Instead, build a regular check-in question into every quarterly survey: "Is there anything about your mentor or your meetings you'd want to change?" Normalize this question so it doesn't feel like a crisis report. Train staff to follow up on "I don't know" or "it's fine" answers with a gentle probe conversation, because teens from institutional settings often default to compliance when asked directly.

* **Let youth voice shape program-level decisions, not just individual matches.** Convene a quarterly "youth council" — six to eight mentees who meet with the program director for 45 minutes to give input on program rules, events, and policies. This demonstrates that teen perspective has weight beyond their individual match, which builds investment in the program overall. Concrete agenda items work better than open brainstorming: "We're thinking about adding a Saturday group event — what would make you actually show up?"

## Question 13: What Relationship Structures, Early Activities, or Program Norms Would Help a New Mentor Earn Trust Rather Than Demand It?

* **Design the first four meetings around doing, not talking.** Conversation-heavy early meetings put teens in the spotlight in a way that feels evaluative — who is this adult deciding about me? Activity-based meetings (cooking something together, walking to a hardware store for a project, shooting hoops) distribute attention and lower the stakes. A mentor who asks questions while doing something side-by-side feels different from a mentor who sits across a table asking questions. Build this into mentor training explicitly: the first month is not therapy, it's presence.

* **Train mentors to withhold advice for the first 90 days.** Teens who have been bounced through systems are often saturated with adults telling them what to do. A mentor who shows up and immediately opines on the teen's choices — school, friends, phone use — triggers the same defensive posture those systems produced. Instead, coach mentors to ask questions and reflect back: "That sounds like it was frustrating. What did you end up doing?" This isn't passive; it's a deliberate trust-building strategy because it signals that the mentor is there to understand, not to fix.

* **Be explicit with teens about what mentors are and aren't.** During intake orientation, tell the teen directly: "Your mentor is not a parole officer, not a school counselor, and not someone who reports to your parents about what you tell them." (Be accurate about mandatory reporting limits — you can't promise total confidentiality — but be clear about what the mentor's role is not.) Many teens assume every new adult in a program role is another surveilling authority. Naming what the mentor is not creates space for what they could actually be.

* **Use low-stakes reliability rituals to build trust through repetition.** Trust with skeptical youth is built through small, repeated confirmations that this adult does what they say. A mentor who texts "see you Thursday at 3" and shows up on Thursday at 3, and does that for eight Thursdays in a row, has built more trust than a mentor who has one deep emotional conversation. Coach mentors to make micro-commitments and keep them: "I'll send you that playlist by Friday." Then send the playlist by Friday. Train mentors to understand that consistency is the mechanism, not a nice bonus.

* **Normalize the relationship being slow.** Mentor training should include explicit expectation-setting: "Months 1-3 may feel flat, even frustrating. Some teens will test you by canceling, being rude, or refusing to engage. This is normal behavior for youth who have been let down by adults. Your job during this period is to stay calm and keep showing up." Include at least one case study of a slow-start match that became strong by month 6. Without this framing, mentors misread the teen's self-protective behavior as rejection and either disengage or escalate pressure, both of which confirm the teen's worst predictions about how this would go.
