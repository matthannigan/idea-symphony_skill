# Topic Cluster 01: Foundations, Purpose, and Theory of Change

## Questions

1. **What "at-risk" and "meaningful relationship" actually mean — and what each label does to the work**: At its core, "at-risk" functions as a proxy for chronic absence, justice involvement, and family instability, while "meaningful relationship" is an aspirational phrase with no operational definition. What is the necessary and sufficient core each phrase points at, how do we distinguish a meaningful relationship from a compliant one, and how does framing teens as "at-risk" before they ever meet a mentor shape the relationship in ways that may undermine the goal?
2. **The fundamental mechanism by which mentorship changes a teen's trajectory**: Strip away curriculum, training, and metrics — what irreducibly has to happen between two people for a mentoring relationship to improve a teenager's life: consistent presence, a trusted adult role model, skill transfer, network access, or something else entirely? Does the current program design optimize for that mechanism or for proxies of it?
3. **Why one-on-one, one-year, and weekly — and whose comfort each format protects**: The program inherits three structural assumptions (dyadic pairing, year-long minimum, weekly cadence). What would shift in the theory of change if at-risk teens here would thrive in peer-group, near-peer, family-inclusive, or small-cohort mentor configurations — and what hidden message does each chosen format send about whose schedule, dignity, and relational style the program is centered on?
4. **Discovery — peak moments of mentorship in this community and the existing assets the program can activate this week**: Think about the most powerful story of youth mentorship you have witnessed or heard from this neighborhood — a moment when a relationship between an adult and a teenager visibly shifted what was possible. What conditions allowed it to unfold; what does the congregation's 20-year presence and the eagerness of 40 adult volunteers tell us about hidden assets already in place; and what is the smallest meaningful first step the program could take this week that draws on those capacities rather than waiting for everything to be built?
5. **What if chronic absenteeism is the correct response**: Thirty-five percent of these teens are chronically absent from school — but what if they are not failing school, school is failing them, and the mentorship program inadvertently reinforces the same institutional logic? What would a mentorship model look like if it started from the premise that the teen's disengagement is accurate information, not a problem to correct?
6. **What if mentorship weren't a one-way transfer**: The traditional model assumes adults hold wisdom teens lack — but what if we completely reinvented this as a mutual becoming, where both mentor and mentee are explicitly transformed by the relationship? What structures, rituals, or agreements would make reciprocal growth the stated purpose, not a side effect?
7. **Radical prioritization**: With only $15K in seed funding and no room for sprawling program infrastructure, what if this constraint forces the organization to identify the single highest-impact element of mentorship — and what would a program look like if it was designed around only that element?
