# Topic Cluster 05: Safety Protocols, Meeting Logistics, and Felt Safety

## Questions

29. **Public spaces, private settings, and when each is defensible** [User Q]: One-on-one meetings are simultaneously the most relationship-building setting and the setting where abuse most commonly occurs. Should mentor-mentee meetings always be held in public spaces, or are there specific circumstances where private or home settings are appropriate and defensible — and what observable, interruptible structural controls (location requirements, visibility standards, third-party check-ins) are embedded in the model rather than left to individual mentor judgment?
30. **The two-deep / observable-and-interruptible standard and the policy that operationalizes it**: One-on-one adult-minor programming creates the highest category of sexual misconduct liability exposure for nonprofits; MENTOR (the national mentoring organization) and many state youth-serving statutes effectively require observable, interruptible meetings. Is the two-deep standard a hard legal requirement under state law or a best-practice standard derived from insurance underwriting — and either way, how does the meeting-location policy operationalize it, and what does non-compliance trigger under the D&O or general liability policy?
31. **Felt safety versus documentable safety, and where teen dignity lives inside the scaffolding**: Programs often think about safety as rules and compliance — background checks, signed forms, supervised locations. Seen from the teen's side, this scaffolding can feel like a message that they are a liability to be managed, not a person to be trusted. What would it look like if teens genuinely *felt* safe — safety as an experience rather than a protocol — what specific relational, environmental, and structural conditions create that felt sense for a 15-year-old who has reason to distrust adults, and what choices does the teen actually get to make in this relationship?
32. **What would it mean to let teens design the safety architecture**: Every protection framework in this program — background checks, meeting-location rules, escalation protocols — is designed by adults for teens. What if we completely reimagined this and involved teens themselves as co-designers of the safety system, drawing on their own lived knowledge of which situations feel dangerous and which feel safe? How might a teen-authored safety framework differ from an expert-authored one, and where might it be more protective?
33. **What if legal compliance were the floor, not the ceiling, of safety**: Background checks, mandatory reporter training, and liability coverage are all designed to prevent known failures. But the deepest harms in youth-serving programs often come from dynamics no checklist captures. What would a program look like that treated relational safety — attunement, emotional honesty, power awareness — as its primary protection mechanism, with legal compliance as merely the minimum required baseline?
34. **Boundary protocols as interface contracts**: Software interfaces define what each module is responsible for and, just as importantly, what it explicitly is not responsible for — clear contracts prevent one module's failure from cascading into another's. Mentor safety protocols often focus on what mentors should not do (be alone with a youth in a private space, engage in physical contact beyond defined norms) but rarely define with equal clarity what mentors are positively responsible for and where their responsibility ends. What would a boundary protocol look like that is framed as an "interface contract" — specifying both what the mentor owns and the handoff point to program staff — so that mentors feel guided rather than policed, and so that boundary violations are legible as protocol failures rather than purely moral failures?
