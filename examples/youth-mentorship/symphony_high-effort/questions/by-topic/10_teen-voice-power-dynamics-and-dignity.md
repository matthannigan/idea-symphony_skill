# Topic Cluster 10: Teen Voice, Power Dynamics, and Dignity

## Questions

59. **Power asymmetry, real recourse, worldview conflict, and what the referral pipeline communicates**: The one-on-one model places a single adult in a sustained, close relationship with a teenager who likely has complex trauma, institutional distrust, and limited power to exit safely; meanwhile the school-counselor and juvenile-justice entry pathways send their own implicit message about who the program thinks the teen is, and a mentor's values, background, or life experience may be genuinely at odds with a teen's family structure, identity, or circumstances. What structures give teens real recourse when a relationship feels wrong without requiring them to navigate adult bureaucracy, what mechanisms allow teens to name what they actually need rather than performing compliance, what mechanisms let worldview conflict surface safely rather than be suppressed, and what would it look like to design an entry point where teens could refer themselves?
60. **Stigma, social identity, and the cost of being seen in a faith-organization mentorship**: For teens navigating social identity in a high-poverty urban neighborhood where 35% of peers are chronically absent, what does it mean socially to be seen participating in a faith-organization mentorship program — and how might peer perception (being labeled "at-risk," being mentored by a church adult) affect whether teens engage authentically versus perform compliance?
61. **The emotional cues teens read in the first twenty minutes**: Before a word of conversation happens, teens read the room — the faces of the volunteers, the space where meetings happen, how they were told what this program is. Teens from this community will know within the first 20 minutes whether this feels real or performative. What sensory and relational cues is the program designing — consciously or not — and which will make a kid lean in versus shut down?
62. **Coerced participation and the program's response when motivation is not intrinsic**: Teens referred through juvenile justice may not be participating entirely voluntarily. How does the program distinguish a teen who genuinely wants a mentor from one complying under pressure, and what happens to match quality and teen safety when motivation is coerced rather than intrinsic?
63. **What if the match is backward**: Imagine we reversed the pairing — teens select and vet adult mentors, not the other way around. Adults audition. Teens reject candidates. The teen holds the relationship initiation. What does this reveal about whose comfort the current model is actually designed to protect, and what would the program look like if teen agency were architecturally central rather than a stated aspiration?
64. **What if we deliberately made mentors easier to remove**: Imagine a program where the default assumption is that any mentor could be removed at any time, for any reason stated by the teen, with no explanation required and no consequence to the teen. No appeals, no mediation, no "working it out." What would this reveal about how current match-termination protocols are actually protecting program continuity and adult dignity rather than teen safety — and what would a genuinely teen-protective exit architecture look like?
65. **The walk through the door**: Imagine a 15-year-old referred by a juvenile justice counselor arriving for her very first meeting with her assigned mentor — what does that moment look and feel like, and how does your program's physical environment, intake ritual, and tone of welcome either open her up or shut her down before a word is exchanged?
