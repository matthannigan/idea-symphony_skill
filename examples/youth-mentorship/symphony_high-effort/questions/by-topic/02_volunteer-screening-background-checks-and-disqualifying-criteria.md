# Topic Cluster 02: Volunteer Screening, Background Checks, and Disqualifying Criteria

## Questions

8. **Minimum disqualifying criteria and the borderline case** [User Q]: Background check results sit on a spectrum from absolute bars (crimes against minors) to judgment calls (old non-violent offenses, expunged records). What should the minimum disqualifying criteria be, what decision framework classifies each finding, who in the organization holds adjudicating authority for borderline cases, and how do we validate criteria from evidence rather than inheriting risk-aversion logic?
9. **Background check statutory floor, FCRA pre-adverse-action, and the screening workflow**: Several states impose a mandatory screening floor for youth-serving organizations (e.g., fingerprint-based FBI checks, Pennsylvania's Act 153 requirements, California's Child Abuse and Neglect Reporting Act ecosystem) and FCRA (15 U.S.C. § 1681) requires pre-adverse-action notice, waiting period, and final adverse-action notice with dispute rights when a third-party consumer reporting agency is used. Does the policy match or exceed the state floor, and does the screening workflow build in the FCRA steps without which the organization carries direct liability?
10. **Documented history misses, congregation familiarity bends — and what counter-balances both**: Documented criminal history catches only those who have been caught, and many people who harm minors have no prior record; meanwhile, social loyalty in a faith community where staff and volunteers worship together can quietly bend screening decisions toward leniency for known members. What structural safeguards (independent or rotating adjudication committees, blinded review, ongoing peer-observation channels) compensate for both the evidentiary limits of background checks and the social-loyalty drift that congregation familiarity creates?
11. **Recruiting for relational capacity, not just willingness — and the 40-volunteer ceiling versus deep-design canvas**: With ~40 interested adults sourced primarily from a single congregation, the program is volunteer-constrained from day one — yet a small, deeply known pool also enables every match to be deliberately designed rather than logistically assigned. What screening instruments or structured interview components most reliably differentiate applicants with genuine relational capacity from those motivated by service hours, religious obligation, or savior energy, and would the program rather have 10 deeply committed mentors than 40 moderately interested ones?
