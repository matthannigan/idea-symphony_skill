# Topic Cluster 11: Outcome Measurement, Evidence, and Program Learning

## Questions

66. **Outcome measurement without overburdening teens or making mentors feel surveilled** [User Q]: The program's real goal is meaningful relationship — but standard metrics (attendance, GPA, recidivism) are lagging proxies that can feel transactional and surveillance-like to teens who have already been assessed and processed by schools and juvenile justice. From the teen's side, being asked to fill out surveys can feel like being assessed rather than known, subtly reframing the relationship as evaluation. What measurement approach honors the depth of the work, remains light enough not to burden mentors or teens, captures leading indicators detectable within weeks, preserves a teen's sense of being valued as a person rather than a data point, and still gives the foundation evidence worth funding?
67. **Instrument selection, weighting, and the pull toward easy proxies that distort mentor effort**: Outcome metrics for mentorship often default to proxies like attendance at meetings or mentor self-reported satisfaction because genuine youth outcomes (school performance, recidivism, emotional resilience) are hard to attribute and slow to materialize — and what gets measured ends up shaping how mentors orient effort, sometimes consciously, sometimes not. What are the observable, measurable proxies for relationship quality over a one-year arc, how should each metric be weighted to avoid over-indexing on easy-to-collect but low-validity indicators, and what risk exists that mentors steer effort toward what is easy to count rather than what matters most for the teen?
68. **Baseline data, comparison strategy, and alignment with evidence-registry trajectories**: Without a control group, attributing program impact to outcomes like chronic absenteeism (currently 35%) is methodologically difficult. What baseline data should the program collect at intake, and what comparison strategy (matched historical cohort, waitlist control, school-wide trend line) gives the most defensible signal — and should measurement design align with What Works Clearinghouse / SAMHSA evidence-registry trajectories that government and larger foundation funders will increasingly require?
69. **Match dissolution feedback loop and selection bias in the public success story**: Match terminations carry concentrated diagnostic information about program design failures, and faith-based organizations often share program narratives publicly through congregational communications, social media, and foundation reports. What structured debrief protocol should the organization apply to dissolved matches so findings are systematically fed back into screening, training, and matching logic rather than treated as isolated incidents — and what safeguards ensure that youth whose stories are shared have genuinely consented (not just their guardians) and that program communications do not create reputational pressure to present a rosier picture than the data supports?
70. **What if we measured outcomes by what mentors learned**: Every outcome measurement framework in this program will focus on teens: attendance, behavior, graduation rates. But here's something absurd — what if the program only measured what mentors learned and how mentors changed? What would it mean if the 40 congregation adults became significantly more capable of sitting with discomfort, navigating unfamiliar institutional systems, and tolerating ambiguity as a result of being mentored by teens? What does inverting the measurement direction reveal about who the current outcome framework implicitly assumes is the developed person and who is the developing one?
71. **What if a hundred successful matches changed the neighborhood's social physics**: Most mentorship programs measure outcomes teen by teen. Imagine instead that the real output is something that emerges at the community level — a new kind of social fabric, or a shift in how adults and young people relate to each other across the neighborhood, not just within program pairs. What outcomes would you measure if the unit of transformation were the community rather than the individual? How would you design the program differently if that were the goal?
72. **The measure that matters in year three**: Fast-forward two years past the formal match closure — your foundation funders want outcome data, but the most meaningful change in a mentee's life may not show up in attendance rates or GPA for years — and consider what story you want to be able to tell then: whose voice carries that story, how was the data gathered in a way that didn't reduce the teen to a metric during the program, and what would count as evidence that the relationship was worth building?
73. **Outcomes as lagging indicators and the need for leading proxies**: In financial markets, GDP is a lagging indicator — it confirms what already happened — while purchasing managers' indexes and consumer confidence surveys are leading indicators that predict direction. Mentorship outcomes like school attendance improvement, reduced juvenile justice recidivism, or academic advancement are lagging indicators that take one to three years to materialize. What leading indicators — detectable within weeks or months of a match — can predict whether a mentorship relationship is on track to produce those long-term outcomes? Possibilities might include: frequency of mentor-initiated contact, teen's self-reported trust score, counselor observation of behavioral change. How should the measurement system be designed to act on leading signals rather than waiting for lagging outcomes?
74. **Cross-system data as triangulation**: Surveyors determine a precise location by taking bearings from multiple known points — no single bearing is sufficient, but three independent bearings triangulate to a reliable fix. This teen's progress is visible differently from three vantage points: the mentor sees relational and behavioral change; the school counselor sees attendance and academic performance; the juvenile justice caseworker (if applicable) sees compliance and incident data. How can the program design data-sharing agreements that allow triangulation across these three vantage points without violating each institution's privacy constraints, and who owns the synthesized view of the youth's progress?
