# Brainstorming Questions from The Questioner: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Cluster 1: Unstated Assumptions About What Mentorship Is

1. **What "safe" actually means**: When the program describes creating "safe" interactions, what specific risks are being guarded against — and whose safety is being centered? What if the safety protocols designed to protect the organization inadvertently communicate distrust to the teens themselves?

2. **Why one-on-one**: The program assumes a one-on-one pairing model is the right structure. What would shift in the program's theory of change if we asked why this model was chosen over peer-group mentorship, near-peer models, or family-inclusive approaches — and whether at-risk teens in this community specifically would thrive in a dyadic relationship with an adult from a faith institution?

3. **What "at-risk" constructs**: The program accepts referrals from school counselors and juvenile justice. What assumptions about teens are baked into that label — and how might those assumptions shape how mentors approach their mentees from the first meeting forward?

4. **Why one year is the right horizon**: The program sets a one-year minimum commitment. What is the reasoning behind that threshold — and what happens to the theory of change if a meaningful relationship forms in three months, or requires three years to take root?

5. **What "meaningful relationship" means operationally**: The stated goal is creating meaningful relationships. What would it mean to know whether a relationship actually became meaningful to the teen — and how would we distinguish a meaningful relationship from a compliant one?

## Cluster 2: The Volunteer-Teen Power Dynamic

6. **Who holds the power to define success**: In a program where adult volunteers are recruited from the congregation and teens are referred through institutional systems, who gets to decide what a successful match looks like — and what mechanisms exist for teens to name what they actually need?

7. **What the faith context signals to teens**: The organization is faith-based. Have we considered what a 13-year-old referred through juvenile justice might hear when they learn their mentor comes from a church — and how that framing might shape the teen's willingness to be honest, ask for help, or name their actual problems?

8. **How the referral pipeline shapes expectations**: Teens enter through school counselors and juvenile justice referrals. What implicit message does that pathway send about who the program thinks the teen is — and what would it look like to design an entry point where teens could refer themselves?

9. **What happens when the mentor's worldview conflicts with the teen's reality**: Volunteers come from the congregation and partner organizations. What if a mentor's values, background, or life experience are genuinely at odds with a teen's family structure, identity, or circumstances? What mechanisms would allow that tension to surface safely, rather than be suppressed or mishandled?

## Cluster 3: Hidden Friction Points in Program Design

10. **What weekly meetings actually require of teens**: A weekly meeting for at least a year sounds like a reasonable ask — but what does that actually require of a 13-17 year-old with chronic absenteeism, unstable housing, or caregiving responsibilities? What if the structure is designed around what works for volunteers rather than what is genuinely accessible to teens?

11. **What training does and doesn't solve**: The program plans a mentor training curriculum. What assumptions are embedded in the belief that training can reliably prepare adults to build trust with teens who've had difficult institutional experiences — and what training approaches might actually be counterproductive?

12. **How outcome measurement reshapes the relationship**: The program wants to measure outcomes without overburdening participants. What if the act of measuring — surveys, check-ins, progress forms — subtly reframes the mentoring relationship as an evaluation, and changes how both mentors and teens show up?

13. **What the $15K seed grant makes possible and what it forecloses**: The program is funded by a $15K seed grant. What design decisions are being made because of funding constraints that are being described as program philosophy — and what would the program look like if this assumption were reversed?

## Cluster 4: Systemic and Institutional Pressures

14. **What juvenile justice involvement means for program culture**: Some teens are referred through juvenile justice. What obligations, data-sharing pressures, or surveillance dynamics might follow those teens into the mentoring relationship — and how would the program ensure that mentors don't become, even inadvertently, an extension of monitoring systems?

15. **What the church's 20-year neighborhood presence enables and constrains**: The organization has deep community roots. What if that long presence means some community members have complicated histories with the institution — and how would the program account for families who trust the neighborhood but not the organization specifically?

16. **Who is not in the referral pipeline**: The program relies on school counselors and juvenile justice to identify teens. What population of teens who would benefit most from mentorship is being systematically missed by those two referral channels — and how might that gap be addressed without overextending organizational capacity?

17. **What happens to the program's integrity when a mentor mishandles a disclosure**: The program will include mandatory reporting training. What organizational culture, not just policy, would need to exist for a mentor to feel safe reporting a concern about a co-volunteer — and have we thought through how the program handles allegations that involve congregation members?

## Cluster 5: Long-Term Sustainability and Honest Reckoning

18. **What success looks like if we're wrong about our theory of change**: The program is designed around the belief that consistent adult mentorship improves outcomes for at-risk teens. What evidence would cause the organization to conclude that this model isn't working — and is the program designed to surface that signal, or to confirm the intervention?
