# Brainstorming Questions from The Storyteller: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Cluster 1: The First Meeting — Setting the Stage for Trust

**The walk through the door**: Imagine a 15-year-old referred by a juvenile justice counselor arriving for her very first meeting with her assigned mentor — what does that moment look and feel like, and how does your program's physical environment, intake ritual, and tone of welcome either open her up or shut her down before a word is exchanged?

**The mentor's origin story**: Picture a 42-year-old congregation member who signed up to mentor because of a memory from his own adolescence — how does your training curriculum help him surface and articulate *why* he's there, and how does that personal narrative become a bridge rather than a distraction in early sessions with his mentee?

---

## Cluster 2: The Arc of a Year — Mapping the Relationship Journey

**The turning-point moment**: Every sustained mentoring relationship has a moment — maybe six weeks in, maybe six months — when the mentee decides the mentor is genuinely trustworthy; what conditions, rituals, or touchpoints does your program design specifically to help that moment arrive, and how will you know when it has?

**The chapter when things go wrong**: Walk through the story of a match that hits a rough patch — the teen stops returning texts, skips two meetings in a row, and the mentor isn't sure whether to push or give space — and describe what your program's support structure looks like from inside that crisis: who does the mentor call, what happens next, and how does the relationship either recover or close safely?

**The end of the commitment**: Imagine the final meeting of a one-year match — both participants know the formal commitment ends today — and consider what your program does to ensure that conclusion feels like a milestone rather than an abandonment, especially for a teen whose history may already include adults who disappeared.

---

## Cluster 3: The Ecosystem of Relationships — Parents, Schools, and the Community

**The parent who didn't ask for this**: Envision a guardian who signed the consent form reluctantly — nervous about a stranger spending weekly time with her son — and trace the story of how your program communicates with her over twelve months: what information does she receive, when, and through what channel, so that by month three she has shifted from wary to invested?

**The school counselor's perspective**: Picture the school counselor who referred three students to your program — she has her own caseload of 400 kids and limited bandwidth — and ask what your reporting and communication design looks like from her vantage point: what does she learn about her referred students' progress, how often, and in what form, such that referring to your program feels worth her professional risk?

---

## Cluster 4: Safety as Lived Experience, Not Just Policy

**The boundary that gets tested**: Let's follow a mentor who is genuinely devoted to his mentee but, eight months in, finds himself bending a boundary — maybe offering a small cash loan, or meeting without the check-in text — and trace what happens: does your program's culture and accountability structure make it easy for him to catch himself and self-correct, or does the drift continue unseen until something goes wrong?

---

## Cluster 5: Outcomes as Human Stories

**The measure that matters in year three**: Fast-forward two years past the formal match closure — your foundation funders want outcome data, but the most meaningful change in a mentee's life may not show up in attendance rates or GPA for years — and consider what story you want to be able to tell then: whose voice carries that story, how was the data gathered in a way that didn't reduce the teen to a metric during the program, and what would count as evidence that the relationship was worth building?
