# Brainstorming Questions from The Analyst: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Cluster 1: Program Structure and Capacity Variables

1. **Volunteer-to-staff ratio and support load**: Breaking this down — with roughly 40 interested volunteers and a part-time administrative staff (in-kind), what is the realistic supervisor-to-mentor ratio the organization can sustain, and at what point does that ratio become a quality control liability rather than an asset?

2. **Cohort sizing and phased rollout**: What evidence or logic should determine whether the program launches all 40 volunteer pairs simultaneously versus staging intake in cohorts — and how does each approach differently distribute risk exposure, administrative overhead, and early match failure rates?

3. **Year-one program throughput model**: The key factors seem to be match initiation, active match duration, and attrition — so what does a realistic throughput model look like for year one, accounting for volunteer drop-off, teen disengagement, and match dissolutions, and what minimum match completion rate would constitute a viable program?

4. **Match duration expectations vs. reality**: Given that the program requires at least a one-year commitment from both parties, what does the attrition curve for similar youth mentorship programs suggest about the distribution of match lengths, and how should the organization design its commitment architecture to reduce early drop-off without coercing participants?

---

## Cluster 2: Screening, Matching, and Decision Logic

5. **Disqualifying criteria tiering**: Background check results exist on a spectrum from absolute bars (e.g., crimes against minors) to judgment calls (e.g., older drug convictions) — what decision framework should the organization use to classify each category of finding, and who in the organization holds authority to adjudicate borderline cases?

6. **Matching variable weighting**: The key factors in mentor-mentee compatibility likely span demographics, interests, geographic proximity, and referral context — what structured weighting or ranking system should govern the matching process, and which variables, if prioritized incorrectly, carry the highest risk of match failure or harm?

7. **Referral source data asymmetry**: Teens referred through juvenile justice come with a richer institutional record than those referred through school counselors — how should the matching and intake process account for this information asymmetry without either over-relying on justice-system framing or under-informing mentors about relevant safety context?

8. **Volunteer self-selection bias**: Looking at the pattern of who volunteers versus who successfully mentors at-risk teens — what screening instruments or structured interview components most reliably differentiate applicants with genuine relational capacity from those motivated by service hours or religious obligation in ways that may not translate to sustained engagement?

---

## Cluster 3: Compliance Architecture and Risk Decomposition

9. **Mandatory reporter training verification**: Mandatory reporter status is legally assigned, but training quality and retention vary widely — what are the component parts of an effective mandatory reporter training for this program (initial training, scenario practice, refresh interval, documentation), and what verification mechanism ensures mentors can operationalize reporting obligations, not just acknowledge them?

10. **Data handling across institutional boundaries**: The program receives referrals from both school counselors and juvenile justice — breaking this down, what distinct data categories flow from each source, what legal regime governs each (FERPA, juvenile records confidentiality statutes), and what data handling architecture prevents cross-contamination or unauthorized disclosure while still equipping mentors appropriately?

11. **Liability exposure mapping**: The organization faces liability exposure across at least three distinct domains — volunteer conduct, organizational supervision failure, and third-party harm — so what coverage components (commercial general liability, abuse and molestation endorsement, directors and officers) address each domain, and which gaps in a standard faith-organization policy are most likely to go undetected until a claim occurs?

---

## Cluster 4: Outcome Measurement and Program Learning

12. **Measurement instrument selection**: The program states a goal of "meaningful relationships" and participant protection — what are the observable, measurable proxies for relationship quality over a one-year arc (e.g., meeting frequency, mentor-reported engagement, school attendance trends, self-report surveys), and how should each metric be weighted to avoid over-indexing on easy-to-collect but low-validity indicators?

13. **Baseline data capture and comparison group**: Without a control group, measuring program impact on outcomes like chronic absenteeism (currently 35% at local schools) is methodologically difficult — what baseline data should the program collect at intake, and what comparison strategy (matched historical cohort, waitlist control, school-wide trend line) gives the most defensible signal of program contribution versus ambient factors?

14. **Feedback loop from match dissolutions**: Match terminations carry concentrated diagnostic information about program design failures — what structured debrief protocol should the organization apply to dissolved matches, and how should findings be systematically fed back into screening criteria, training content, and matching logic rather than treated as isolated incidents?

---

## Cluster 5: Sustainability and Operational Scalability

15. **Unit economics and grant dependency cliff**: The key factors seem to be cost per active match, staff time per mentor, and grant renewal probability — what does the unit cost structure look like at 20, 30, and 40 active matches, and at what match volume does the program become financially viable without full dependence on the seed grant, given the in-kind contributions already in place?
