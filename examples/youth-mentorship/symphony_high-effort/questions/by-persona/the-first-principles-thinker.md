# Brainstorming Questions from The First Principles Thinker: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Cluster 1: The Core Purpose — What Are We Actually Trying to Do?

**What does "at-risk" actually mean here, and does the label help or harm?**: At its core, the term "at-risk" is a proxy for something more specific — chronic absence, justice involvement, family instability. If we were designing this program from scratch without that inherited label, what underlying condition are we actually trying to address, and does framing teens as "at-risk" before they ever meet a mentor shape the relationship in ways that undermine the goal?

**What is the fundamental mechanism by which a mentorship relationship produces change?**: Strip away the program structure, the training curriculum, and the outcome metrics — at its most basic level, what needs to happen between two people for a mentoring relationship to improve a teenager's trajectory? Is it consistent presence, a trusted adult role model, skill transfer, access to networks, or something else entirely? And does the program design actually optimize for that mechanism, or for proxies of it?

**Why one-on-one, and is that assumption worth keeping?**: The one-on-one model is the dominant paradigm in youth mentorship, but it is also the most resource-intensive and most legally complex. Building from first principles: if the fundamental need is a trusted adult relationship, are there configurations — small cohorts, mentor families, peer-plus-adult triads — that achieve the same relational outcome with lower matching failure risk and better resilience when a match ends?

## Cluster 2: Trust Architecture — Who Authorizes Whom, and Why?

**What is consent actually protecting, and does the current framework do that?**: Parental/guardian consent is the legal default for program participation, but the teens referred through juvenile justice may have complex family situations — incarcerated parents, guardian instability, state involvement. The fundamental question is: whose consent is required to protect the teen's interests, and whose consent requirement might actually prevent them from accessing the program? If we were designing a consent architecture from scratch, optimized for the teen's wellbeing and safety, what would it look like?

**What is the irreducible core of a background check regime?**: Background checks exist to prevent harm to minors. The fundamental issue is: which past behaviors are actually predictive of future harm in a mentoring context, and which disqualifying criteria are inherited from risk-aversion logic rather than evidence? If you were building screening criteria from first principles — not by adopting a standard template — what would the minimum necessary and sufficient set of exclusions look like, and how would you validate them?

## Cluster 3: Safety Protocols as First-Principles Design

**What does "safe" actually mean in this context, and for whom?**: Safety protocols in youth programs are typically written to protect organizations from liability, teens from harm, and volunteers from false accusations — but these goals can pull in different directions. The fundamental issue is: if we ranked these three protection goals honestly and built protocols around that ranking, how would the rules differ from conventional "two-adult rule" and public-location requirements? Are we designing for actual safety or for documentable safety?

**What is the simplest possible outcome measurement system that would tell us whether this program is working?**: Outcome measurement in youth programs has become enormously complex — standardized instruments, multi-year longitudinal tracking, academic performance data. Strip it back: what is the single most meaningful signal that a mentor-mentee relationship is achieving its core purpose, and is it actually measurable without requiring burdensome data collection that erodes trust with participants? If we built measurement from first principles, what would we choose to observe?

---

## Cluster 4: The Volunteer Equation — What Do Mentors Actually Need?

**What is the fundamental reason mentor relationships fail, and does training address it?**: Most mentor relationships in youth programs end prematurely, and the most common cause is mentor burnout and disengagement rather than youth dropout. At its core, what makes a volunteer mentor persist through the difficulty of working with a struggling teen — and is that quality something training can instill, or something that has to be selected for in recruitment? If we were building a mentor support system from scratch, what would it prioritize?

**What does a "40-volunteer pool" actually represent, and is it the right resource to build on?**: The organization has ~40 interested adults, which sounds like an asset — but the fundamental question is whether interest translates to fitness for this specific program model. If you were building a volunteer corps from first principles to serve teens referred through juvenile justice and chronic absenteeism, what attributes would you recruit for, what would disqualify beyond legal criteria, and would you rather have 10 deeply committed mentors than 40 moderately interested ones?
