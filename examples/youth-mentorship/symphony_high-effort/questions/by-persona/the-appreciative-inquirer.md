# Brainstorming Questions from The Appreciative Inquirer: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Cluster 1: Discovery — Peak Moments and Existing Strengths

**When mentorship changed a young person's trajectory**: Think about the most powerful story of youth mentorship you have witnessed or heard from this community or a similar one — a moment when a relationship between an adult and a teenager visibly shifted what was possible for that young person. What specifically was happening in that relationship, and what conditions allowed it to unfold?

**What the congregation already does well for young people**: This faith-based organization has maintained a 20-year presence in the neighborhood and already has 40 adults eager to volunteer. What does that sustained trust and volunteer enthusiasm tell us about existing relational assets the program can build on — and how have those assets shown up in practice before any formal program existed?

**When trust was built across institutional lines**: Schools and juvenile justice systems are often experienced as adversarial by the teens they serve. Tell me about a time — here or elsewhere — when a community organization successfully bridged between one of those systems and a vulnerable young person's family in a way that felt safe rather than threatening. What made that bridge work?

---

## Cluster 2: Dream — Aspirational Futures Built on Strengths

**What thriving looks like three years in**: Imagine it is three years from now and this mentorship program has become something the neighborhood actively celebrates — a source of visible pride, a story the teens themselves tell others, and a model that peer organizations are asking to learn from. What would a visitor notice when they walked in the door? What would a mentor say when asked what keeps them showing up? What would a teenager say the relationship gave them?

**The match that exceeded everyone's expectations**: Picture a specific mentor-mentee pair — one of the strongest matches this program will ever make. The mentor is from the congregation; the teen came through a school counselor referral and was initially resistant. Six months in, something has clicked. What does their relationship look like? What does the teen notice that surprised them? What does the mentor say they never expected to learn?

**What safety looks like when it is felt, not just enforced**: Programs often think about safety as rules and compliance — background checks, signed forms, supervised locations. But what would it look like if teens in this program genuinely *felt* safe in their mentor relationships, safety as an experience rather than a protocol? What specific conditions — relational, environmental, structural — would create that felt sense of safety for a 15-year-old who has reason to distrust adults?

---

## Cluster 3: Design — Structures That Reliably Create the Future

**Matching as relationship-making, not just logistics**: Given that the most powerful mentorships seem to hinge on genuine connection, what matching process would reliably surface the conditions for that connection rather than just minimizing mismatch risk? What information about a volunteer's life experience, or a teen's interests and hopes, would be most predictive of a relationship that lasts and deepens past the one-year minimum?

**Training that turns volunteers into confident guides**: The congregation has 40 adults who are willing. Willingness is an asset — but willingness without preparation can lead to burnout or inadvertent harm. What training design would build on volunteers' existing relational strengths (faith community members often have practiced empathy, hospitality, and long-term commitment) while equipping them with the specific skills — mandatory reporting, boundary-setting, trauma-informed listening — that this population requires?

**Measuring what matters without making teens feel like data**: Outcome measurement in youth programs often defaults to attendance counts and test scores, which can feel transactional to participants and capture only a fraction of what actually changes. Given that this program's real goal is meaningful relationship, what measurement approach would honor the depth of the work, remain light enough not to burden mentors or teens, and still give the foundation evidence worth funding?

---

## Cluster 4: Destiny — First Steps and Sustaining Momentum

**Who already has the skills to start this week**: Looking at the 40 interested volunteers, the church's administrative staff, the school counselors ready to refer teens, and the organization's two-decade community knowledge — which specific capacities are already fully in place, right now, that this program could activate immediately? What is the smallest meaningful first step that draws on existing assets rather than waiting for everything to be built?

**How mentors will sustain themselves through the hard stretches**: The one-year commitment is intentionally long because relationships take time to build, but mentoring at-risk teenagers involves real difficulty — silence, setbacks, crises, moments of feeling inadequate. What support structures would allow mentors to move through those stretches without burning out or quietly disengaging, drawing on the mutual accountability and community that a faith organization is already positioned to offer?
