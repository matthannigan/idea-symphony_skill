# Brainstorming Questions from The Empath: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Cluster 1: The Emotional Reality of Showing Up

**What does "being chosen" feel like to a teen who has rarely been chosen?**: When a mentee is matched with a volunteer, there's a moment of being selected — singled out as worth someone's time. My gut says that moment will feel enormous to some of these kids, maybe uncomfortably so. How do we prepare mentors to hold that weight without letting the relationship become defined by pity or savior energy, which these teens will detect instantly and resent?

**What does it feel like to be referred through juvenile justice?**: There's a real difference between a teen who walks in feeling hopeful and one who arrives because a court officer or counselor said they had to engage. My instinct is that some of these kids will enter the program already braced for disappointment, already expecting the adult across from them to eventually leave or give up. How does the program design account for the emotional starting point of a teen who has learned not to trust adult-initiated relationships?

**What happens emotionally when a mentor cancels — or just disappears?**: We know mentor attrition happens. But I keep coming back to how a no-show or a gradual fade-out will feel to a 15-year-old who has already been let down by adults. My gut says this is the wound the program most needs to prepare for. What emotional safety nets exist for the teen when a match doesn't survive — and does the program name this risk honestly upfront, or does it quietly hope it won't happen?

## Cluster 2: The Texture of Trust — Mentors and the Organization

**What emotional contract are volunteers actually signing up for?**: There's what volunteers think they're committing to — weekly coffee, a bit of guidance — and then there's the emotional reality of sitting across from a teenager who is carrying real trauma, family instability, or legal trouble. My gut says many well-meaning adults will be blindsided by how heavy this gets. Does the recruitment and training process create honest expectations, or does it sell a version of mentorship that feels manageable right up until the moment it isn't?

**How do mentors process feelings they're not supposed to have?**: Frustration with a mentee who ghosts them. Vicarious grief when a teen's home situation deteriorates. Quiet resentment when the match feels one-sided. These feelings are real and they don't belong anywhere in the current program structure. Where does a mentor go when they feel something they're embarrassed to admit — and if there's nowhere to go, what happens to that energy inside the relationship?

**What does it feel like to carry mandatory reporting obligations into a relationship built on trust?**: This is a tension I find genuinely uncomfortable to sit with. A teen finally opens up, shares something real — and the mentor may be legally required to act on it in ways that could shatter the relationship or land the family in crisis. My gut says mentors will feel trapped in that moment. How does training prepare them emotionally for that specific rupture, not just procedurally?

## Cluster 3: Belonging, Identity, and the Organizational Vessel

**What does it feel like to be a non-religious teen in a faith-based program?**: The organization has a 20-year presence and deep trust in this community — that's real. But some teens being referred through schools or juvenile justice will arrive without any connection to the congregation's faith identity, and some may actively bristle at it. My gut says this tension will be invisible in the formal program design but will quietly shape whether those teens feel genuinely welcomed or like recipients of charity with strings attached. How does the program hold its faith identity without letting it become a barrier to belonging?

**What emotional signals tell a teen this program is for them — or isn't?**: Before a word of conversation happens, teens will read the room: the faces of the volunteers, the space where meetings happen, how they were explained what this program is. My instinct is that teens from this community will know within the first 20 minutes whether this feels real or performative. What are the sensory and relational cues the program is designing — consciously or not — and which of those cues will make a kid lean in versus shut down?

## Cluster 4: Power, Dignity, and the Shape of the Relationship

**What does it feel like to be studied and measured?**: The program needs outcome data — that's legitimate. But from a teen's side, being asked to fill out surveys about their wellbeing, their goals, their progress can feel like being assessed rather than known. My gut says there's a real dignity question here: how does evaluation feel to a kid who came looking for a relationship, not a program? Which measurement approaches preserve that feeling of being valued as a person, not a data point?

**Where does a teen's dignity live inside a safety-first framework?**: Background checks, parental consent forms, boundary protocols, meeting-location rules — all of this is necessary and right. And yet this scaffolding, seen from the teen's side, can feel like a message: you are a liability to be managed, not a person to be trusted. My instinct is that how these structures are communicated to teens — and whether teens are ever given a voice in shaping any part of the experience — will determine whether the program feels safe or just surveilled. What choices does the teen actually get to make in this relationship?

**What's the emotional shape of a good ending?**: A one-year commitment is the baseline, but eventually matches close — by design or circumstance. My gut says a well-handled ending could actually be one of the most powerful things this program does for a teen who has rarely experienced an adult relationship that ended with intention, care, and closure. What does a genuinely good ending look and feel like, and is the program designing for it the way it's designing for the beginning?
