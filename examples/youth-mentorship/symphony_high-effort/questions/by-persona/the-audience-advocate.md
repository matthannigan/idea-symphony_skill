# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Cluster 1: How Teens Actually Experience Being "Referred"

**Labeling and the referral moment**: When a teen is identified through a school counselor or juvenile justice referral and told they are being offered a mentorship program, what emotional experience does that teen likely have — and how does the language, framing, and messenger of that invitation shape whether they see it as an opportunity, a punishment, or surveillance?

**Opt-in versus opt-out dynamics**: From the community's perspective, when a guardian signs a consent form and a teen is enrolled, what does that teen understand about why they were selected, what they've agreed to, and what happens if they want to stop — and in what ways might the program's process inadvertently communicate that the teen has no meaningful say?

**Stigma within the peer group**: For teens navigating social identity in a high-poverty urban neighborhood where 35% of peers are chronically absent, what does it mean socially to be seen participating in a faith-organization mentorship program, and how might peer perception — of being labeled "at-risk," of being mentored by a church adult — affect whether teens engage authentically versus perform compliance?

## Cluster 2: Mentor Experience and the Relationship Power Dynamic

**Power asymmetry in the one-on-one format**: The one-on-one model places a single adult in a sustained, close relationship with a teenager who likely has complex trauma, institutional distrust, and limited power to exit safely — so what structures does this program need to give teens real recourse when a mentor relationship feels wrong, without requiring them to navigate an adult bureaucracy to do so?

**Cultural and experiential distance**: When a volunteer from the congregation is paired with a teen referred through juvenile justice, what assumptions about the teen's life circumstances, family structure, communication norms, and relationship with authority does the volunteer bring — and how might those assumptions, even well-intentioned ones, register to the teen as judgment rather than curiosity?

**Mentor authenticity versus organizational liability**: From the teen's perspective, what difference does it feel like when a mentor is genuinely curious about who they are versus performing a scripted role designed around the program's compliance and liability needs — and how might over-structuring the relationship to manage risk actually undermine the trust that makes mentorship work?

## Cluster 3: Family and Guardian Inclusion (or Exclusion)

**Whose relationship is this?**: Parents and guardians of teens in this community are navigating their own economic stress, institutional distrust, and possibly prior contact with systems (schools, courts) that have felt adversarial — so how might the program's consent and communication framework land for a guardian who suspects the organization is another institution gathering information about their family, and what would it take to make family engagement feel genuinely collaborative rather than procedural?

**Sibling and household dynamics**: Teens in high-poverty households rarely experience issues in isolation — they have siblings, extended family members, and household responsibilities that compete with weekly mentoring commitments — so how does the program account for the full household context of a teen's life, and in what ways might an individualized one-on-one model inadvertently create tension within the family?

**Information flow and family trust**: When something concerning comes up in a mentoring relationship — whether mandatory reporting is triggered or simply a difficult disclosure is made — what does the teen experience in the moments after, and how does the program ensure that the mechanics of mandatory reporting and guardian notification do not destroy the teen's trust in the mentor before it has a chance to form?

## Cluster 4: Access, Consistency, and Real-World Barriers

**Transportation and scheduling as equity issues**: What people say they want and what they actually do can diverge enormously when logistics get in the way — so for a teen in a high-poverty urban neighborhood, what are the realistic transportation, scheduling, and competing-obligation barriers to attending weekly meetings, and how might a program designed around volunteer convenience unknowingly create dropout pressure that looks like teen disengagement?

**What "chronic absenteeism" actually signals**: Local schools report 35% chronic absenteeism — a statistic the program treats as background context — but from the community's perspective, that number reflects lives shaped by economic instability, housing insecurity, caregiving responsibilities, and distrust of institutions, none of which disappears when a teen enters a mentorship program. How does the program design for the full reality of these teens' lives rather than the idealized participant who can show up reliably every week?

**Matching across difference**: When a teen is matched with an adult mentor, they are being asked to build a close relationship across likely differences in race, class, life experience, and worldview — so what does the matching process communicate to the teen about whether their preferences, comfort, and identity matter, and how does the program surface and act on teen feedback about a match that isn't working before the relationship quietly collapses?

## Cluster 5: Outcomes, Measurement, and Who the Program Is Really For

**Whose definition of success counts**: Organizations typically measure what is legible to funders — attendance rates, school grades, recidivism metrics — but teens in this program may experience growth in ways that don't show up in those measures: shifts in self-perception, a single trusted adult relationship, the ability to imagine a different future. How does the program create space to capture and honor outcomes that matter to teens, not just outcomes that justify renewal of the $15K grant?

**The experience of being evaluated**: For teens who have already been identified, assessed, and processed by schools and juvenile justice systems, what does it feel like to be in a program where their progress is tracked and reported — and how might measurement and evaluation instruments designed with good intentions reproduce the surveillance dynamic these teens already associate with institutional contact?
