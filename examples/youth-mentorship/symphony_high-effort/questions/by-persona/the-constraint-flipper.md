# Brainstorming Questions from The Constraint Flipper: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Cluster 1: Scarcity as Focus — What the $15K Budget Forces You to Get Right

**Radical prioritization**: With only $15K in seed funding and no room for sprawling program infrastructure, what if this constraint forces the organization to identify the single highest-impact element of mentorship — and what would a program look like if it was designed around only that element?

**Volunteer ownership as cost-saving multiplier**: What if the limited budget, rather than being a weakness, becomes a reason to transfer more program ownership to mentors themselves — and how might mentor-led onboarding, peer support groups, and self-organized matching actually produce stronger relationships than a staff-managed model would?

---

## Cluster 2: Restricted Volunteer Pool as a Differentiator

**Congregational trust as a built-in asset**: The volunteer pool is drawn from a faith community with a 20-year neighborhood presence — what if this apparent limitation (no open public recruitment) is actually the program's most credible safety guarantee, and how could that provenance be made explicit and legible to skeptical parents and juvenile justice partners?

**Small-pool matching depth**: Most programs cast a wide volunteer net and match on surface-level criteria. What if having only ~40 volunteers means every match can be deeply and deliberately designed — and how could the organization turn this ceiling into a reputation for the highest match quality in the region?

---

## Cluster 3: Juvenile Justice Referrals — Stigma as Structural Clarity

**Referral constraint as design anchor**: Teens referred through juvenile justice arrive with specific, documented needs and a system already tracking their progress. What if this "difficult population" label is actually a gift — because it provides clearer outcome metrics, existing caseworker relationships, and a built-in accountability structure that volunteer-only programs can never match?

**Mandatory reporting as trust infrastructure**: Mandatory reporter obligations are typically framed as a burden on mentors. What if the program reframed mandatory reporting training as a signal of institutional seriousness — a marker that distinguishes this program from informal mentoring and makes it the preferred partner for schools and courts precisely because it has teeth?

---

## Cluster 4: One-Year Commitment as a Filter, Not a Barrier

**Long commitment as self-selection mechanism**: A one-year minimum commitment will deter some potential volunteers. What if this is exactly the point — and how might the program use the commitment requirement as a deliberate filter that surfaces only the mentors most likely to stay, thereby reducing match disruption for teens who have already experienced abandonment?

**Duration as depth architecture**: What program structures, milestone rituals, and relationship tools could be built specifically around the arc of a year — treating the time constraint not as an arbitrary requirement but as a canvas with a defined shape that mentors and teens can consciously navigate together?

---

## Cluster 5: 35% Chronic Absenteeism as an Entry Point, Not a Symptom

**Meeting the teen where the absence is**: Chronic absenteeism means many target teens are not reliably reachable through school. What if this limitation — the teens are hard to find — reframes the program's outreach strategy entirely, and what non-school touchpoints (barber shops, laundromats, transit stops, faith community youth nights) become the actual recruitment infrastructure?

**Absenteeism data as mentorship intelligence**: School counselors already track absence patterns for referred teens. What if the program used that existing data not to surveil mentees but to help mentors understand a teen's rhythms and design meeting cadences around reality rather than an idealized weekly schedule — turning the documented constraint into a personalized mentorship map?
