# Brainstorming Questions from The Devil's Advocate: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Cluster 1: The Assumption of "Safe" Volunteers

**Volunteer pool as a bottleneck**: With only ~40 interested adults sourced primarily from a single congregation, what happens when attrition, disqualifications, or poor matches reduce that pool — and what structural alternatives exist to avoid a program that is volunteer-constrained from day one?

**Background checks as false assurance**: Background checks catch documented history, but many people who harm minors have no prior record — in what ways might the program's safety design create a false sense of security that actually reduces vigilance around ongoing behavioral signals from mentors?

**Congregation familiarity as a screening bias**: When volunteers are drawn from the same faith community where program staff worship and socialize, how might social loyalty and reluctance to exclude a known community member undermine the rigor of screening decisions, and what safeguards counteract that dynamic?

**Disqualifying criteria ambiguity**: Different types of offenses — decades-old misdemeanors, civil judgments, expunged records — may or may not be disqualifying depending on state law and organizational policy; what process determines where those lines are drawn, who makes those calls, and how are edge cases handled consistently and fairly?

---

## Cluster 2: The One-on-One Model and Its Structural Risks

**Isolation by design**: One-on-one meetings are more intimate and relationship-building than group formats, but they are also the setting where abuse most commonly occurs — what specific structural controls (location requirements, visibility standards, third-party check-ins) are embedded in the model design rather than left to individual mentor judgment?

**Year-long commitment as a pressure point**: A one-year minimum commitment sounds like a program strength, but what happens when a mentor or teen wants to exit a match that is failing, uncomfortable, or potentially unsafe — and does the emphasis on commitment create social pressure to remain in a problematic relationship rather than end it?

**Single-coordinator dependency**: One-on-one programs rely heavily on a coordinator or case manager to monitor the health of each match; if that role is filled by one staff person supported by volunteer administrative help, what is the failure mode when that person leaves, burns out, or misses signals across many simultaneous pairs?

---

## Cluster 3: Systemic and Institutional Tension Points

**Juvenile justice referrals and coerced participation**: Teens referred through juvenile justice may not be participating entirely voluntarily — how does the program distinguish between a teen who genuinely wants a mentor and one who is complying under pressure, and what happens to match quality and teen safety when motivation is coerced rather than intrinsic?

**Data privacy collision across systems**: Schools, juvenile justice, and the faith organization each operate under different data regimes (FERPA, juvenile-records law, and general nonprofit practice respectively) — when a mentor learns something about a teen that crosses institutional lines, what is the protocol for containing that information, and who in the program actually understands these distinctions?

**Mandatory reporting and relationship trust**: Mandatory reporter obligations require mentors to report abuse or neglect they learn of from a teen, but disclosure to a mentor often depends on the teen trusting that the relationship is private — how does the program communicate mandatory reporting obligations to teens in a way that is honest about limits without preemptively chilling the disclosure of serious harm?

---

## Cluster 4: Outcomes, Measurement, and Organizational Incentives

**Grant dependency distorting program decisions**: A $15K seed grant from a regional foundation is meaningful but modest — what specific decisions about program design, target population, and scale are being made now that will be difficult or costly to reverse once the program is shaped to satisfy that funder's reporting requirements?

**Measurement proxy substitution**: Outcome metrics for mentorship programs often default to proxies like attendance at meetings or mentor self-reported satisfaction because genuine youth outcomes (school performance, recidivism, emotional resilience) are hard to attribute and slow to materialize — what risk exists that the program optimizes for metrics that are easy to collect rather than outcomes that matter?

**Success story selection bias**: Faith-based organizations often share program narratives publicly through congregational communications, social media, and foundation reports — what safeguards ensure that youth whose stories are shared have genuinely consented (not just their guardians), and that program communications do not create reputational pressure to present a rosier picture of outcomes than the data supports?
