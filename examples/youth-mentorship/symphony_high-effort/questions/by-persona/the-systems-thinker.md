# Brainstorming Questions from The Systems Thinker: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Cluster 1: Relationship Dynamics and Self-Sustaining Cycles

**Mentor commitment depth**: When a mentor invests deeply in a teen's progress, does that investment create the conditions for its own continuation — or does it create a ceiling where the mentor's sense of ownership makes it harder for the teen to outgrow the relationship?

**Early success and long-term drift**: If the program celebrates early relationship milestones — a teen re-engaging in school, a first job, a conflict resolved — how does that early success change mentor behavior over the following months? Is there a pattern where early wins reduce the urgency that drove initial mentor effort, creating a slow drift toward shallow check-ins that the program's metrics don't detect?

**Match quality as a self-amplifying signal**: A strong mentor-mentee match builds trust, which encourages the teen to show up, which gives the mentor positive feedback, which increases mentor effort. But a weak match does the opposite — low engagement signals failure, the mentor pulls back, the teen disengages further. What screening and early-intervention mechanisms could catch a deteriorating match before the downward cycle becomes self-reinforcing?

**Volunteer enthusiasm and institutional dependence**: If the program is effective, it may attract more volunteers, which enables it to serve more teens, which strengthens its reputation for results. But at some scale, the coordination demands grow faster than the volunteer base can absorb — more complex matching, more training, more case management. At what point does program growth begin to hollow out the relational quality that made it work in the first place?

---

## Cluster 2: Referral Pipelines and Gatekeeping Dynamics

**Referral source bias and population shaping**: The program draws teens through school counselors and juvenile justice referrals. Each source applies its own selection logic — counselors flag students they believe are reachable; juvenile justice refers youth with court involvement. Over time, what kind of teenager does the program actually serve, versus who it was designed to reach? And if referral sources learn what the program accepts, do they start pre-filtering to protect their relationships with the program?

**Institutional alignment and drift**: The school system, juvenile justice, and the faith organization each have their own success metrics. A school counselor's definition of "progress" (attendance, grades) may conflict with the juvenile justice system's (compliance, recidivism) and the church's (relationship and moral growth). When these definitions pull in different directions, who does the program actually optimize for — and does the teen fall into the gaps between misaligned institutional incentives?

**Consent architecture as a funnel**: Parental or guardian consent is required, but for teens referred through juvenile justice, the guardian landscape may be complex — incarcerated parents, rotating foster placements, overextended relatives. How does the consent requirement function not just as a legal protection, but as an unintended filter that systematically excludes the teens most in need of the program?

---

## Cluster 3: Safety Protocols and Behavioral Adaptation

**Mandatory reporting and relationship trust**: Training mentors as mandatory reporters is legally necessary and protective. But mandatory reporting obligations, if not handled carefully in how they are communicated to teens, can create a chilling effect — teens who know their disclosures may trigger official intervention may withhold exactly the information the mentor needs to help them. How should the program design its mandatory reporting framework so that legal compliance does not erode the relational honesty that makes mentorship work?

**Safety protocols and relationship texture**: One-on-one interactions with minors require documented meeting locations, no-private-contact rules, and activity logging. Each of these protocols is individually justified. Together, they shape the texture of the relationship — a mentorship that feels observed and procedural is a different relationship than one that feels personal and trusted. How should the program calibrate safety infrastructure so it protects participants without converting mentorship into a supervised interaction that teens experience as another institutional contact?

**Reporting burden and mentor attrition**: If mentors are required to log meetings, complete check-in surveys, and document concerns, the administrative load accumulates over time. Mentors who joined to have a human relationship with a young person may find the paperwork erodes their motivation. What is the tipping point where data collection designed to improve the program begins to drive away the kind of volunteer the program most needs — and how would the program know it had crossed that threshold?

---

## Cluster 4: Outcome Measurement and Unintended Incentives

**What gets measured and what gets valued**: If the program measures attendance rates, school grades, and recidivism as primary outcomes, mentors will — consciously or not — orient their effort toward those indicators. But the most valuable thing a mentor may do for a teen is provide consistent, unconditional presence during a period of instability. That effect is nearly invisible in standard metrics for months or years. How does the program measure what matters without inadvertently creating pressure to optimize for what is easy to count?

**Success criteria and the definition of a good match**: The program will develop an implicit or explicit model of what a "successful" match looks like. That model will then influence future matching decisions — pairs that resemble past successes will be preferred. Over time, this can narrow the population the program effectively serves toward teens whose profiles predict measurable success, systematically excluding teens whose needs are more complex. What safeguards prevent the program from gradually becoming a program that serves teens who would have done reasonably well anyway?

---

## Cluster 5: Institutional Position and Long-Term Sustainability

**Faith-based identity as asset and constraint**: The organization's 20-year neighborhood presence is a genuine asset — it has trust, relationships, and cultural credibility that a new organization cannot buy. But a faith-based identity also shapes which volunteers come forward, which families engage, and which teens feel comfortable participating. What populations does the organization's identity draw toward the program, and what populations does it inadvertently push away — and does that mismatch matter for the program's stated goals?

**Seed funding dependency and program design**: The program is launching on a $15K foundation grant. Grant funding creates a natural incentive to design the program for what funders measure and value. If the organization builds its outcome framework, training curriculum, and reporting infrastructure around the first grant's requirements, how hard will it be to adapt those systems when the next funder has different priorities? Is the program designing for the teens it serves, or for the renewal application it will need to write?
