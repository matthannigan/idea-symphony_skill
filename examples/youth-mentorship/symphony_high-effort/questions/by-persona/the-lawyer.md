# Brainstorming Questions from The Lawyer: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Cluster 1: Mandatory Reporting Architecture

**Mandatory reporter status and training scope**: State mandatory reporter statutes (e.g., California Penal Code § 11165.7, similar provisions in other states) enumerate specific categories of persons — does your jurisdiction's statute cover volunteer mentors at a nonprofit, or only licensed professionals? If mentors fall outside the enumerated categories, what contractual mechanism (volunteer agreement, code of conduct) should you use to impose a parallel reporting obligation as a matter of policy, and how do you document that training occurred?

**Escalation chain design under mandatory reporter law**: When a mentor forms a reasonable suspicion of abuse or neglect, the statutory obligation typically runs to a designated report with child protective services — not up an organizational chain of command. Does your program's escalation protocol risk interposing the organization between the mentor and the mandated report in a way that could create liability for obstruction or delay? How do you design an internal notification pathway that satisfies the reporting obligation without creating a de facto gatekeeper?

**Training documentation as liability shield**: Several states require documented mandatory reporter training as a prerequisite for covered persons. What training format (in-person, online certification, attestation) satisfies the evidentiary standard your jurisdiction would apply if the organization were later investigated for failure to report? Should training completion be logged in a separate record system with a defined retention period?

---

## Cluster 2: Juvenile-Records Privacy and Data Governance

**OJJDP and state juvenile-records confidentiality statutes**: Teens referred through juvenile justice channels come with a privacy wrapper — most states have juvenile-records confidentiality statutes (e.g., under frameworks aligned with the Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention Act) that restrict re-disclosure of case information. What specific information, if any, is the juvenile justice agency authorized to share with your program at referral, and under what consent mechanism? Does a blanket intake consent form satisfy the statutory re-disclosure standard, or must consent be case-specific?

**Data minimization design for intake records**: Beyond what juvenile justice may share, your program will generate its own records — meeting logs, incident notes, progress assessments. What is the minimum data set necessary for program operation, and does collecting anything beyond that minimum create retention liability? How long should records be kept, and does your state have a specific destruction-of-records requirement for information about minors?

**Third-party data handling when minors are involved**: If you use a volunteer-management platform, a case-management tool, or even shared cloud storage to hold mentee records, that third-party processor now holds sensitive data about minors. What data processing agreement (DPA) must be in place with each vendor, and have you assessed whether any of those platforms' data practices are inconsistent with juvenile-confidentiality obligations — even if HIPAA and FERPA do not technically apply to your organization?

## Cluster 3: Liability Exposure and Structural Protections

**Sexual misconduct liability and the "two-deep" standard**: One-on-one adult-minor programming creates the highest category of sexual misconduct liability exposure for nonprofits. MENTOR (the national mentoring organization) and many state youth-serving nonprofit regulations effectively require observable, interruptible meetings — the "two-deep" or "observable and interruptible" standard. Is this a hard legal requirement under your state's youth-serving nonprofit statute, or a best-practice standard derived from insurance underwriting requirements? Either way, how does your meeting-location policy operationalize it, and what does non-compliance trigger under your D&O or general liability policy?

**General liability and sexual misconduct endorsement**: A standard nonprofit general liability policy (typically $1–2M per occurrence) frequently excludes or sublimits sexual misconduct claims unless a specific endorsement is purchased. Does the organization's current policy include a sexual misconduct endorsement, and if so, does it cover volunteer acts or only employee acts? What is the claims-made vs. occurrence distinction for this endorsement, and does a $15K seed-grant budget support the premium for adequate coverage?

**Volunteer status versus contractor status for liability purposes**: Under your state's tort law, the organization's respondeat superior liability exposure is shaped by whether mentors are treated as volunteers, employees, or independent contractors. Volunteer protection acts (the federal Volunteer Protection Act of 1997, 42 U.S.C. § 14501, plus state analogs) generally shield individual volunteers from personal liability for acts within the scope of their duties — but they do not shield the organization. Does the VPA apply to your state's faith-based nonprofits? What indemnification language in the volunteer agreement protects the organization if a mentor acts outside defined program boundaries?

## Cluster 4: Parental and Guardian Consent Frameworks

**Consent architecture across multiple referral channels**: Teens arrive from at least two institutional channels — school counselors and juvenile justice — each of which may have its own consent or notification requirements for referrals. On top of that, your program needs independent parental or guardian consent for participation. How do you design a consent stack that satisfies each upstream channel's requirements without creating conflicting consent documents, and who holds legal authority to consent for a teen who is a ward of the state or placed in foster care?

**Ongoing consent and the right to withdraw**: A one-year commitment creates a longitudinal consent question — can a parent or teen withdraw consent mid-program, and what obligations does withdrawal trigger (data deletion, notification to referral source, match termination)? Is there a risk that treating the match as a contractual commitment creates an unenforceable or unconscionable clause when the party is a minor?

## Cluster 5: Background Check Compliance and Volunteer Screening

**Background check statutory floor and disqualifying criteria**: Several states have enacted statutes specifically governing background checks for volunteers in youth-serving organizations (e.g., California's requirements under the Child Abuse and Neglect Reporting Act ecosystem, or Pennsylvania's Child Protective Services Law Act 153 requirements). Does your state impose a mandatory screening standard — fingerprint-based FBI check, statewide sex offender registry check, or child abuse history check — as a condition of operating a youth-serving program? What are the per se disqualifying offenses, and does your policy match or exceed that floor?

**Adverse action process under the Fair Credit Reporting Act**: If you use a third-party background check vendor (a "consumer reporting agency" under the FCRA, 15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.), you are legally required to follow the FCRA's pre-adverse-action notice and dispute process before disqualifying a volunteer applicant based on the report. Does your screening workflow include the mandatory pre-adverse-action notice, the waiting period, and the final adverse-action notice with dispute rights? Failure here creates direct FCRA liability against the organization, not just the volunteer.
