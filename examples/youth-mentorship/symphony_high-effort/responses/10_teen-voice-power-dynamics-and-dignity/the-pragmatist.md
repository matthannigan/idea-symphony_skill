# Teen Voice, Power Dynamics, and Dignity - The Pragmatist

---

## Power Asymmetry, Real Recourse, Worldview Conflict, and Referral Pipeline

* **Establish a simple, teen-controlled pause mechanism.** Build a 30-day "cooling-off" option into every match where a teen can request a change without explanation, processed in 3 business days. Cost: staff time for intake interviews with replacement mentors (roughly 2-4 hours per teen who exercises it). This doesn't solve deep power asymmetry, but it removes the catastrophic cost of exit — a teen no longer risks disappointing an adult before they know them. Start with one person owning the process to keep it simple.

* **Separate teen-stated needs from referral-source signal.** During intake with the teen (not the referring counselor), ask three concrete questions: What do you want help with? What would success look like to you? Is there anything your counselor mentioned you don't actually care about? Write their answers in their file. This takes 20 minutes and makes referral messaging visible to staff. When mentors see teen-owned goals alongside the counselor note, they can navigate conflicting expectations explicitly.

* **Create a teen advisory group that sits on match reviews.** Recruit 2-3 teen alumni or current participants who have been in the program 6+ months. They attend quarterly staff meetings to review flagged matches and provide feedback on what "real recourse" actually feels like. Budget: modest stipend ($50-100 per meeting) and schedule flexibility. They'll tell you within weeks what your intake process actually communicates to new teens.

* **Design a mentor self-selection step before assignment.** After intake, show the teen 2-3 mentor profiles (photo, brief bio, one thing they enjoy doing) and ask which person they'd want to try meeting. Mentors confirm willingness. This is not a full veto, but it moves the teen from "assigned to" to "choosing among." Operationally: requires mentors to provide profiles and mentors to confirm — moderate friction, but it shifts the relational tone before the first meeting.

* **Make worldview conflict discussable in training.** During mentor orientation, name specific scenarios: "Your teen's family is in a church you think is harmful. You strongly disagree with their parents' politics. You don't understand their gender identity claim." Train mentors in the core skill of "I respect your choice even though I think differently" — not as a value claim but as a relational competency. Add a one-page guide mentors can reference. This doesn't solve conflict, but it stops mentors from being blindsided and helps them avoid performative agreement.

## Stigma, Social Identity, and Cost of Being Seen in Faith-Organization Mentorship

* **Keep the program's faith identity visible but secondary.** Don't hide the church affiliation (teens will know anyway), but frame mentorship as the church's action in the neighborhood, not as recruitment. In outreach materials, lead with "mentors who get your neighborhood" over "faith mentors." In meetings, don't require faith participation; just don't hide that the program is church-sponsored. Teens' social reading is sophisticated enough to spot false neutrality faster than honesty. Budget: revise outreach templates and coach staff on messaging consistency.

* **Offer meeting locations teens control.** Don't require all meetings to happen at the church building. Mentors meet teens at coffee shops, parks, or other neutral spaces the teen suggests. This cuts the visual "being seen at the church" marker by default. Some pairs will gravitate to the church anyway; others won't. Cost: clarify liability and background-check requirements (same whether on-site or off), and brief mentors on outdoor-meeting norms. Implementation is straightforward.

* **Create a "What Your Peers Might Wonder" handout.** Be direct: "Some people might ask why you're meeting with an adult from a church. Here's what I'd say..." and offer three clear scripts (spiritual, practical, just because). Distribute during intake. This sounds paternalistic, but teens are already rehearsing stories. Offering one removes the weight of inventing one from scratch while they're figuring out whether to trust the program.

* **Build peer cohesion among participating teens.** Host optional monthly group hangouts (pizza, games, low-stakes time) where mentored teens see each other. Peer normalization is more powerful than any staff messaging. Teens who know other mentored teens in their school no longer feel isolated or labeled. Budget: food and space, 2-3 hours monthly. Attendance is voluntary but signals belonging. This directly counters the "at-risk weirdo" social cost.

* **Give mentors a clear "how to talk about the program" brief.** Mentors meeting teens' friends or peers will get questions. Brief them: "If a friend asks, keep it simple — I'm a mentor from a community program, we hang out and talk about [whatever they're talking about]." Don't hide the program, but also don't position it as remedial or special. Train mentors to normalize it through their own ease and understatement.

## The Emotional Cues Teens Read in the First Twenty Minutes

* **Audit the intake moment with a teen consultant.** Before designing your intake ritual, have a teen (or two) walk through your space and describe what they notice. "How do you know this is legit vs. performative?" They'll notice things staff never would — overly clean spaces that read as sterile, volunteer enthusiasm that reads as fake, questions that feel like paperwork vs. conversation. Schedule one afternoon of feedback and revise based on what surfaces. Budget: modest payment to the teen consultant and willingness to hear hard feedback.

* **Keep the physical space honest.** If it's a church building, it's a church building — don't disguise it. Make sure it's genuinely welcoming (clean, reasonably comfortable, not institutional). Avoid excessive signage about the program or values. Have actual human adults in the space who are not just program staff — someone making coffee, a volunteer doing something real — so the teen sees it's a functioning place, not a presentation for them.

* **Create a two-person intake protocol.** Mentor matches shouldn't happen in a clinical interview. Instead: (1) intake staff collects basics (name, contact, referral source, goals), (2) mentor meets the teen for 20 minutes in a parallel space doing something low-stakes (playing a game, sitting outside, getting food). The mentor notes how the teen showed up — did they relax, did they stay guarded, did they ask questions back? This tells you more than an intake form and signals to the teen that the mentor is being assessed by the program too.

* **Train staff and mentors in noticing shutdown.** Teach the distinction between "quiet because that's how I am" and "quiet because this feels unsafe." Specifics: does the teen ask questions? Do they make eye contact? Are they there willingly or looking at the door? Brief staff on how to name it gently if needed: "You seem less talkative today — you good?" This signals that staff are paying attention to the teen's actual vibe, not just their attendance.

* **Document the intake moment as a data point, not a judgment.** After intake, staff write one sentence: "Alex came in nervous, warmed up when we mentioned basketball, asked about whether his mentor would judge his mom." This note helps the assigned mentor know what the teen was managing on day one and signals to the teen that their anxiety was noticed and matters. Over time, these notes show how teens are settling in.

## Coerced Participation and Motivation Not Intrinsic

* **Distinguish at intake: "You have to see a mentor" vs. "You get to pick this mentor."** For teens referred through juvenile justice, make crystal clear the non-negotiable (program requirement, time commitment) and the flexible (which mentor, what you talk about, where you meet). This transparency often reduces resentment — teens know the boundary and can invest in the choices they do have. Budget: one-page referral letter that breaks this down for teens, and staff training on how to explain it.

* **Track motivation shifts over time.** At month 2, month 4, and month 8, ask the teen privately: "Are you doing this because you have to, or because you want to?" No judgment, just data. For teens who stay coerced, check whether the match is working (is the mentor attuned? is the space safe?) or whether the teen is just complying. This prevents wasted matches and tells you early if a teen needs a different mentor or a different program.

* **Create quick wins early for coerced-entry teens.** If a teen's motivation is external, design the first 4-6 weeks for concrete, visible progress: a skill learned, a goal named, a problem solved together. This builds initial momentum that might shift motivation. Don't load heavy emotional work onto coerced pairs; build trust through action. Budget: brief mentor training on "first-month goals" for referred teens.

* **Separate match quality from motivation with honesty.** A strong mentor + coerced teen might still build something real; a weak mentor + coerced teen will fail. When a match isn't working with a coerced teen, don't assume the teen is the problem. Mentor skill, match chemistry, and external pressure are all variables. Budget: staff capacity to reassess mentors alongside teens and make mentor changes as often as teen changes if needed.

* **Build in realistic exit for coerced teens.** If a juvenile justice requirement is 12 months, design the program for 12 months and then natural closure. If a teen wants out before that, understand it: "The program isn't working for me" is valid even when participation is legally required. Work with the referring entity on what "completion" means. Some teens will finish strong; some will complete the letter of the law. That's okay.

## Reversed Pairing: Teens Select and Vet Adult Mentors

* **Pilot a teen-led mentor selection process with a subset.** Don't flip the entire program overnight. Instead, recruit 5-6 mentors willing to be "auditioned" and give 8-10 teens a chance to meet them and give feedback on who they'd want. Mentors are told upfront: "These teens choose whether to work with you." This is psychologically harder for mentors (rejection feels real), so start with people who can handle it. Budget: reframe mentor recruitment messaging and brief mentors on the expectation.

* **Create a structured "teen interview" of mentors.** Give teens a simple framework: "What should I know about you? What do you think I should know about how you'd work with someone? What would you want me to tell you if something wasn't working?" This flips the power asymmetry instantly. Teens see that mentors can be questioned, nervous, and trying to prove themselves — which is psychologically closer to how adult relationships actually work than "the adult assesses you."

* **Be transparent about what this reveals.** Yes, it reveals that the current model is designed for adult comfort. But also acknowledge: mentors need some minimum standard (background check, training) — you can't let teens select a predator. The question is where to put the gate: after full mentor vetting (current model) or after teen vetting (this model). Budget: revised mentor recruitment that's honest about this being a harder ask, and staff preparation for mentors who decline to participate.

* **Track which mentors teens actually choose and why.** After a few months of this, you'll have data: teens chose mentors who were most direct, or most fun, or most similar to them. Use this to train the rest of your mentor pool. Don't change recruitment wholesale based on three months of data, but it tells you something real about what resonates versus what seems good in theory.

* **Start with low-stakes mentors, then expand.** Use this model first with mentors who are less critical (group programs, informal mentors, peer mentors). Once you know how teens operate as selectors, you can expand to core one-on-one matches. This lets you learn the friction and benefits without betting your whole program.

## Deliberately Making Mentors Easier to Remove

* **Design a 14-day "no questions asked" exit window after first meeting.** Teen meets mentor once, then has 14 days to request a change without explanation. This reveals mismatches early (personality, distance, schedule) without requiring the teen to articulate why. Budget: have a second mentor lined up or a waiting list so the process doesn't stall. Implementation: notify both teen and mentor that this window exists.

* **Create a simple "it's not working" mechanism.** After that 14 days, a teen can still request a change, but you may ask for brief context ("Things just aren't clicking" is enough). You don't require mediation or "working it out." If the teen wants a change, grant it unless there's a safety concern. Mentor stays in the program; just match them differently next time. Budget: willingness to accept that some mentors won't work for this program and cycles of reassignment.

* **Track removal data honestly.** Keep records: how many matches end before 12 months? How many are teen-initiated vs. mentor-initiated? Do certain mentors get removed repeatedly? This data tells you whether your exit architecture is protecting teen safety or whether you have a mentor vetting problem. Budget: simple spreadsheet and quarterly review of patterns.

* **Separate "match ended" from "program failed."** A match can end and still have been valuable. A 3-month pairing where a teen got what they needed is a success, even if it's not the "year-long relationship" you imagined. Reframe this in your metrics and mentor training to reduce the shame around matches that don't last 12 months. Budget: revised success criteria and staff messaging.

* **Train mentors to handle rejection gracefully.** Some mentors will feel hurt if a teen requests a change. Brief them: "This isn't about you. It's about fit. You might work better with a different teen." Offer them a second assignment option or an exit. Mentors who can't handle a teen saying "this isn't working" shouldn't be in a program designed around teen agency anyway. Budget: honest conversation and acceptance that some mentors self-select out.

## The Walk Through the Door

* **Script the first 10 minutes, not as a greeting, but as a reset.** A 15-year-old arriving from juvenile justice is already managing fear, guardedness, and a dozen assumptions about what this program wants from her. Before anything programmatic happens, she needs to know: "There's a bathroom, help yourself to water or a snack if you want it. My name is [mentor]. Thanks for coming. Before we talk about anything official, what should I know about your day?" This signals that her immediate human needs and her actual headspace matter more than the program agenda.

* **Audit your intake space through her eyes.** Is there a waiting area where she'll be seen by others? Are there staff watching the door? Is there something to do with her hands while talking (a table, something to fidget with) or is she supposed to sit still and perform attention? Is the space actually warm (someone making actual coffee, not just a mentorship poster) or sterile (too clean, too quiet, too much intentional design)? Change whatever doesn't feel like an honest space.

* **Make the mentor introduction human, not formal.** The mentor doesn't lead with the program pitch or goals. She mentions something that happened to her that day, asks the teen one actual question about her life, and shows up as a real person. This takes 5 minutes and signals: "I'm not here to evaluate you. I'm here because this matters to me." A teen from a background of institutional surveillance recognizes the difference between genuine and performed instantly.

* **Clarify what "being here" actually means, without jargon.** Don't say "This is a mentorship program designed to build resilience through structured relationship." Say: "I'm an adult who agreed to spend time with you, hang out, talk about things that matter to you, help if I can. We'll figure out what that looks like together." The teen needs to know the deal is simple, not complicated. Budget: one-page mentor brief on how to explain the program in plain language.

* **Make physical comfort visible and optional.** "There's a vending machine around the corner, bathrooms are down the hall, we can sit here or go outside if you want" — said in the first five minutes, shows the teen that she has agency even over tiny logistics. She doesn't have to ask permission. This small concession of control often opens someone who is otherwise braced for control. Budget: staff attention and a willingness to bend small environmental rules for individual comfort.
