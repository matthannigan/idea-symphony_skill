# Volunteer Screening, Background Checks, and Disqualifying Criteria - The Audience Advocate

---

## Minimum Disqualifying Criteria and the Borderline Case

* **Frame disqualifying criteria through the teen's experience of betrayal and trust-breaking.** From the community's perspective, the question "Is this volunteer safe?" is inseparable from "Will I (or my child) ever trust an adult again after this relationship?" Teens referred through juvenile justice or school counselors have often experienced adult betrayal—whether from a caregiver, authority figure, or predatory acquaintance. A borderline case that your organization resolves as "probably fine" becomes a teen's lived trauma if that gamble fails. Disqualifying criteria should be conservative enough that a young person can genuinely believe the organization vetted this person thoroughly, and the documentation should be clear enough that staff can explain *why* to a teen or guardian without sounding evasive.

* **Disqualifying criteria should reflect what teens and families fear, not just what organizations fear.** Legal liability and risk-aversion frameworks often lead to criteria that avoid obvious liability but miss what families actually dread. Families are asking: "Has this person been convicted of violence or sexual offenses? Does this person have unexplained patterns of isolation with minors? Has this person been fired from youth-serving roles? Does this person have a substance-abuse history they're hiding?" Those are the questions to build criteria around. A volunteer with an old drug conviction 20 years ago might carry different risk from a volunteer whose references include nobody who's observed them with young people, or someone who describes kids as uniquely "special" or specially receptive to mentorship compared to other adults.

* **Adjudicating authority should include representation from the community being served.** If the organization alone decides borderline cases, you risk excluding people who are actually trustworthy but have demographic or historical circumstances (prior poverty, immigration status, prior involvement with juvenile justice themselves) that bias institutional decision-making. Consider whether a panel that includes a teen-serving community advocate or a peer mentor (someone with lived experience in the neighborhood) who reviews borderline cases brings a perspective that catches when criteria are being applied with hidden double standards.

## Background Check Statutory Floor, FCRA Pre-Adverse-Action, and the Screening Workflow

* **Families need to know exactly what you checked and why someone was or wasn't hired—and they have a right to that transparency.** From the community's perspective, screening is opaque: a volunteer appears, gets matched to a teen, and the family has no visibility into what was actually verified. If your organization uses a third-party background check agency and someone is rejected, FCRA requires you to tell them why; what's less obvious is that families of *matched* teens also deserve a clear statement (in writing, in plain language) of what your screening process actually entailed. "We ran a background check" means very different things depending on whether that's a name search, an FBI fingerprint check, a state database query, or a multi-state record review. Be explicit so families can judge whether *they* feel confident in your process.

* **The workflow itself communicates whether screening is perfunctory or serious.** Teens and families experience screening through whether they're told results, how long matching takes, and what it feels like when a background flag comes up. If you skip FCRA pre-adverse-action steps because you think "nobody will sue," you're signaling that you cut corners. If you move fast from application to match because the volunteer pool is small, you're telling families speed matters more than rigor. A process that moves deliberately—that takes time to verify, that documents findings, that gives applicants a chance to explain findings—signals to the community that you take their safety seriously even if it slows things down.

* **Use independent verification for your own staff, not just volunteers.** Families often don't realize that the person running the mentorship program may have had a lighter screening than the volunteer mentors. Consider a policy where everyone (staff and volunteers) undergoes the same background-check process and disqualifying-criteria review. This removes the implicit message that organizational staff are presumed trustworthy while volunteers are suspect, and it signals that the organization holds itself to the same standard it demands of others.

## Documented History Misses, Congregation Familiarity Bends, and Structural Safeguards

* **Safeguards need to address the specific pattern of abuse in faith communities: known members committing unreported harm.** The teens being served may have experienced prior abuse or boundary violations in institutional settings (schools, prior programs, family-adjacent spaces). They are joining a church-based program where the volunteers are known within the congregation. From their perspective, the structural safeguard they need is evidence that the organization takes threats from *within* the community seriously, not just threats from strangers. This means: documented peer-observation channels where volunteers report concern about other volunteers (anonymous reporting option available), periodic check-ins with matched youth that include trained staff asking open-ended questions about whether boundaries feel safe, and a clear pathway for teens to raise concerns about a mentor that doesn't put them in a position of "reporting on an elder" to people who worship with that elder.

* **Create rotating or independent adjudication to interrupt the social-loyalty drift, and make the process visible to applicants.** Families of teens need to know that the person approving volunteers isn't someone who also attends Bible study with the applicant. Consider a policy where screening decisions are made by a committee that includes at least one person without social ties to the applicant, and where the applicant knows this structure exists. The very fact that independent adjudication is visible to the community signals that you've thought about the drift problem and built in a counter-balance.

* **Implement ongoing structured observation, not just initial screening.** The biggest safeguard against documented-history misses and familiarity bends is not gatekeeping at the door but continuous attention during the mentorship itself. Require mentors to complete brief check-in forms after meetings (location, activities, tone—not invasive, but enough to catch pattern shifts). Have trained staff conduct unannounced observational visits to a random sample of mentor-mentee pairs. Institute a "concern escalation" protocol where staff or peers can flag a mentor for closer review if they notice boundary drift, isolation patterns, or changes in behavior. Make this ongoing observation visible to mentors (not as surveillance, but as normal oversight) and to families (as part of how you protect their kids).

## Recruiting for Relational Capacity, Not Just Willingness—and the 40-Volunteer Ceiling Versus Deep-Design Canvas

* **From the teens' perspective, a small pool of deeply vetted mentors is vastly preferable to a large pool of available bodies.** At-risk teens often have histories with unreliable adults—people who promised connection but didn't follow through, or who came with unmet needs of their own that made them unpredictable. A mentorship program that starts with 40 moderately interested volunteers and quickly pairs teens to whoever is available is setting teens up for another relational failure if that volunteer burns out, gets transferred, or was never actually committed. A program that says "We have 10 mentors who have demonstrated they can sustain real relationships over time" may serve fewer teens initially, but each match is more likely to actually be sustainable and healing. Ask the community directly: do you want rapid access to a high-quantity program where mentor reliability is uncertain, or patient access to a smaller program where every mentor can credibly say "I'm in this for the long term"?

* **Screening instruments should assess relational *history*, not just relational *intention*.** Many screening tools ask "Why do you want to mentor?" and "How do you handle difficult conversations?" as if enthusiasm and self-awareness predict actual follow-through. What actually predicts relational capacity is evidence: Does this person have stable relationships in their own life (not necessarily perfect, but enduring)? Can they talk about relationships where they made mistakes and how they repaired them? Do their references include people they've known for years, or only recent acquaintances? Have they sustained volunteer commitments before, or is this their first rodeo? Have them spend time with a youth (a group activity or volunteer shift) before matching, and talk to the teen afterward: "Does this person feel safe? Do they seem interested in you as a person?" The teens themselves are excellent judges of whether someone's relational capacity is genuine.

* **Be explicit about the "savior" screening, because it's where good intentions create the most harm.** Some volunteers come motivated by a genuine desire to "rescue" at-risk teens—to be the adult who "fixes" the teen's life. From a teen's perspective, this is suffocating: they become a project rather than a person, and the mentor's emotional investment in "success" (however defined) overshadows the teen's agency in their own life. Screening should include questions that surface this pattern ("Tell me about a time when you wanted to help someone but they chose a different path") and interviews should include a frank conversation about what success looks like (better grades? More stable housing? Graduation? Criminal-justice involvement decline?)—because mentors motivated by *rescue* and teens focused on *survival* often have mismatched definitions that will blow the relationship apart.
