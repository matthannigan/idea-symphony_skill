# Volunteer Screening, Background Checks, and Disqualifying Criteria - The Pragmatist

---

## Minimum Disqualifying Criteria and the Borderline Case

* **Three-tier classification with delegated authority.** Create a tiered system: absolute bars (any conviction for child abuse, sexual assault, or felony violence within 10 years — automatic disqualification); red flags (arrests without conviction, non-violent felonies over 5 years old, misdemeanor assault — escalate to a screening committee); and green flags (traffic violations, expungements over 7 years old, juvenile records after age 25 — approve unless pattern emerges). Assign the committee a specific charter: "Committees must document each decision with reasoning tied to program safety, not general risk-aversion. Re-evaluate decisions annually."

* **Evidence-based criteria audit before launch.** Rather than adopting generic risk frameworks, conduct a brief 4-week audit: review 10-15 mentorship programs in your state, extract their actual disqualifying lists (contact program directors directly), identify which criteria cluster together (e.g., domestic violence + substance-related arrests), and benchmark against your organization's risk tolerance. Document why you accept or reject each criterion. This grounds the policy in regional practice and builds defensibility if a decision is challenged.

* **Rotate adjudicators annually to reduce familiarity bias.** If the screening committee is three people and one is the executive director (who knows many congregation members), rotate the committee membership every 12 months. A fresh reviewer may ask different questions about borderline cases. Document decisions publicly within the organization (e.g., "We approved Jane D. despite a 2010 DUI because our statute of limitations is 7 years and no pattern exists") so staff understand the reasoning and spot drift.

* **Establish a written appeals process for borderline cases.** If a candidate is rejected for a judgment call (e.g., a 2005 non-violent felony), allow them to submit a brief written statement and request committee reconsideration once per year. This surfaces new information (e.g., court records now show the case was dismissed), and gives the organization a defensible stance: "We apply consistent criteria fairly and transparently."

## Background Check Statutory Floor, FCRA Pre-Adverse-Action, and the Screening Workflow

* **Map your state floor before choosing a vendor.** Call your state's child-serving program office (often in social services or education) and ask: "What is the minimum background check required for volunteers working with minors?" Write down the exact requirements (fingerprint-based FBI check, state police check, sex-offender registry, child-abuse registry, etc.). For Pennsylvania, this is Act 153 (FBI fingerprinting required); for California, Live Scan plus the state Trustline registry is mandatory. Create a one-page checklist and share it with the board. This takes 30 minutes and prevents you from running screening that is *compliant but insufficient*.

* **Use a vendor that handles FCRA pre-adverse-action automatically.** If you are contracting with a third-party background-check service (e.g., Checkr, Sterling, First Advantage), confirm in writing that their workflow includes: (1) notification to the candidate that they may receive an adverse decision, (2) a dispute window (typically 5-7 business days), (3) final written notice if the decision stands, and (4) documentation of all steps in your records. Verify that the contract specifies which party bears liability if FCRA steps are skipped. Do not use an in-house volunteer to run checks; the liability clarity is not worth it.

* **Create a one-page screening workflow document and lock it into your volunteer manual.** Document: "Step 1: Collect application + three references. Step 2: Submit to [Vendor Name] by [date]. Step 3: Receive preliminary results. Step 4: Send pre-adverse-action notice if concerns exist. Step 5: Wait 5 business days for dispute. Step 6: Send final notice. Step 7: Approve or deny. Step 8: Retain records for 3 years." Post the timeline (e.g., "Process takes 4-6 weeks") on your recruitment page so volunteers know when to expect decisions. This reduces pressure to "speed up" screening and cuts down on informal approval shortcuts.

* **Quarterly review of rejected candidates to spot patterns.** Track why people are rejected (e.g., "no conviction but lived at address associated with arrest," "felony over 10 years old," "reference checked out, but background shows prior lie"). After 8-10 screening cycles, review the data to see if the criteria are working as intended or if you are rejecting disproportionately (e.g., overly strict on old convictions). This is defensible post-hoc refinement, not initial guesswork.

## Documented History Misses, Congregation Familiarity Bends, and Structural Safeguards

* **Blind-review committee for internal volunteers.** If a volunteer is a long-time congregation member, have their screening materials reviewed by a committee member who does *not* attend the congregation or hold a relationship with them. For external volunteers, assign a committee member who does. The goal is that at least one reviewer enters the decision without prior familiarity. This does not require recusal; just ensures a different lens. Document which committee member "owned" the decision (for accountability) and which one provided the "fresh eyes" review.

* **Structured telephone reference check with a standard question set.** Rather than letting references volunteer impressions ("Jim is wonderful"), use a checklist: "Has Jim ever disclosed any trouble with the law? Has he mentioned strained relationships with family members or colleagues? Have you known him to work with children or young people before? What would you want to know if you were placing your own child with him?" This surfaces inconsistencies (e.g., a reference says "Jim is great with kids," but Jim's own application says he has no childcare experience). Trained staff conduct all reference calls, and the answers are documented in writing.

* **Ongoing peer-observation protocol during mentor training and quarterly spot-checks.** During the mandatory mentor-training workshop, have staff and experienced mentors observe newcomers in low-stakes settings (group icebreakers, role-play scenarios). Staff note any flags (e.g., boundary-testing language, discomfort with "no" from a teen actor, over-familiarity). This catches red flags that a background check and interview miss. After mentors are matched, conduct unannounced check-ins every quarter where staff sit in on a mentor meeting (with both parties' consent) and document observations. This is cheaper than litigation.

* **Annual re-screening for active mentors, especially long-term congregation members.** Every 12 months, run a background check on active mentors (quick check for new arrests or incidents). For congregation members, have an independent staff member (not the director) conduct a brief confidential conversation: "Any changes in your life, health, or circumstances we should know about?" Document the conversation (even if it's a simple "All good"). This creates a regular touchpoint and makes clear that screening is ongoing, not one-time.

## Recruiting for Relational Capacity, Not Just Willingness — and the 40-Volunteer Ceiling Versus Deep-Design Canvas

* **Structured interview with relational-capacity rubric.** Add a 30-minute one-on-one interview with specific prompts: "Tell me about a time you listened to someone who disagreed with you without trying to fix them immediately." "Describe a relationship where you had to accept that the other person's goals were different from yours." "When have you failed at something you cared about, and how did you cope?" Score responses on a three-point scale (low/medium/high) across three dimensions: (1) comfort with ambiguity (can sit with a teen's uncertainty without pushing solutions), (2) reciprocity (genuine interest in the teen as a person, not as a project), and (3) boundary awareness (understands limits of a mentor's role). This takes 2-3 hours per volunteer to administer and score, but filters for relational depth rather than enthusiasm.

* **Intentional downsizing: 15-20 deeply matched pairs vs. 40 unmatched volunteers.** A practical approach might be: Launch with 15-20 mentors you screen intensively and match deliberately based on interview data, shared interests, and neighborhood proximity. This creates better outcomes (deeper relationships, lower dropout) and is manageable for a staff of 2-3. Set an explicit ceiling ("We can serve 20 pairs well") and use a waitlist. This also shifts the pitch: instead of "We need 40 volunteers," say "We're recruiting 15-20 mentors who are ready for 1-year, once-weekly commitment" — this filters out the "I have service hours to complete" crowd. As you build capacity (staffing, data on what works), expand.

* **Motivation screening: three-question filter at intake.** Before a formal interview, have a 10-minute phone screen with three open-ended questions: (1) "Why did you sign up for this program specifically?" (Looking for authentic reasons tied to kids, community, or personal history — not vague religiosity or obligation.) (2) "What concerns do you have about working with an at-risk teen?" (Looking for realistic awareness of challenges, not savior fantasy.) (3) "What do you hope to get out of being a mentor?" (Looking for relational learning, not resume-building or feel-good service.) Score based on whether answers suggest relational readiness. Reject or probe deeper on any answer that suggests obligation, status-seeking, or rescue fantasy. This is a 15-minute filter and eliminates ~20-30% of candidates early.

* **Trial mentorship pairing: 8-week pilot with explicit go/no-go decision.** After interview, pair a candidate with a teen for 8 weeks of weekly meetings. At week 8, debrief with the teen (confidentially, with youth worker present), the mentor, and the placement coordinator. Ask: "Is this working? Do both of you want to continue for the full year?" If the teen seems hesitant (even if polite), treat it as a "no." This sounds harsh but catches mismatches early. Some volunteers will realize they don't actually enjoy the work. Some mentors will emerge as naturals. This costs 8 weeks per placement, but beats 12 months of a strained relationship that harms the teen.
