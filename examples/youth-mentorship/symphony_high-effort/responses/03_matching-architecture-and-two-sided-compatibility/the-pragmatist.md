# Matching Architecture and Two-Sided Compatibility - The Pragmatist

---

## Matching Variables, Weighting, and Match Quality Prediction

* **Start with observed patterns, not theory.** Before designing a weighting system, spend your first 3-4 matches tracking outcomes: Which pairs stuck past month 6? Which dissolved quietly? What did intake worksheets show about the successful ones? With a $15K budget and 40 volunteers, you can manually manage the first 10-15 matches while collecting data. Document what actually correlates with durability (not compatibility on paper, but real sustained engagement) and use that to iteratively weight your matching criteria. Theory about personality fit is cheaper than algorithms, but observation data is more reliable.

* **Three tiers of variables with explicit harm thresholds.** Create a tiered system: (1) Non-negotiable safety filters (background check pass, mentor stability, teen's stated safety needs), (2) High-weight predictors (shared geography, teen's actual interests from intake conversation, mentor's availability consistency), (3) Nice-to-have alignment (demographic similarity, shared hobbies). Assign harm weights inversely: mismatching on safety is disqualifying; mismatching on hobbies is noise. This gives intake staff clear rules rather than discretionary judgment that varies by coordinator mood.

* **Elicit teen preferences through structured conversation, not forms.** Teens referred through juvenile justice will not fill out a Likert scale about "What kind of adult would help you?" with honesty. Instead, have your intake person conduct a 30-minute semi-structured interview with a clear script: "Tell me about an adult who's helped you before — what did they do?" "What's something you're good at that you don't think many people know?" "If you could pick anyone to hang out with once a week, what would you want to do together?" Transcribe these (with teen consent) and use them as your primary matching signal, overriding demographic similarity when preferences contradict demographics.

## Matching As Relationship-Making, Not Logistics

* **Reverse the assignment direction.** Most programs match mentors to teens. Instead, pitch volunteer cohorts to teen cohorts and let teens choose (or ranking system with mutual preference). Explain to mentors: "You'll see profiles of three teens we think you might click with. Tell us: who interests you most and why?" Teens get: "We have volunteers who do X, Y, Z. Who sounds like someone you'd want to know?" This surfaces genuine attraction early rather than revealing mismatch after three awkward meetings. Yes, some teens won't find someone; then you brainstorm with that teen directly about what they're looking for and recruit specifically.

* **Track predictors of durability explicitly.** After six months, you'll have data. Analyze: Did matches with shared hobbies last longer than demographics-based matches? Did the mentor's willingness to be vulnerable (sharing their own struggles in intake) predict durability? Did teen-initiated contact between meetings correlate with month-12 retention? Use these patterns to refine your matching algorithm. With 40 volunteers and maybe 20-30 teens in year one, you'll accumulate enough data by year two to know what actually predicts relationships that deepen.

* **Prototype with existing relationships.** Before launching formal matching, ask your volunteer cohort: "Do any of you know teens in this community who might benefit from mentoring?" Mentor 2-3 existing relationship pairs (volunteer + teen they already know) in parallel with your formal matching process. These will likely be your strongest matches and will provide proof-of-concept and testimonials. The formal matching process is for pairs with no prior connection; use your existing-relationship pilots to validate what "strong connection" looks like in your specific context.

## Referral Source Data Asymmetry and Cultural-Experiential Distance

* **Surface the asymmetry in intake, don't hide it.** When a volunteer meets their teen, tell them directly: "This teen was referred through [juvenile justice / school counselor]. Here's what that means for how they might experience being matched with you." Briefly explain: JJ-referred teens may have experienced authority figures as punitive; school-referred teens may see adults as academics; either way, clarify that curiosity about the teen's life beats assumptions about their family or values. This explicit naming prevents the well-intentioned mentor from accidentally performing rescue energy that triggers defensiveness.

* **Create parallel intake streams that surface different information.** School counselor referrals come with academic context; JJ referrals come with institutional risk assessments. Don't pretend they're equivalent. Ask school counselors specifically about interests and strengths; ask JJ case managers about safety context and previous positive adult relationships. Build a profile template that explicitly marks which information came from which source, so mentors understand they're getting a partial picture and should ask their teen directly about gaps rather than filling them in from assumptions.

* **Establish a "match check-in" protocol at week 2 and week 6.** Brief conversation with the teen: "How's it going with your mentor? Anything weird or off? Anything you want to change?" Offer the teen an easy exit: "If this isn't working, that's okay — we can try a different match." This lets you catch misalignment before it hardens into quiet resentment. With small cohorts, you can do this by phone or in-person before formal quarterly check-ins. Data from early check-ins will also show you which referral-source combinations tend to misalign.

* **Recruit mentors with explicit cultural-proximity intention.** You have 40 volunteers from a congregation plus partner orgs. Ask each: "What communities or experiences are you familiar with from living through them (not just learning about)?" An adult with lived experience of poverty, foster care, or encounter with the justice system brings different credibility than someone whose justice-system knowledge is entirely academic. This doesn't mean only matching "similar" backgrounds — it means being intentional about which mentors have existing cultural bridge-building capacity and pairing them where referral-source distance is greatest.

## What "Being Chosen" Feels Like to a Teen

* **Reframe the match moment as mutual choice, not assignment.** Teens hear "You were selected for mentoring" as "Adults think you need fixing." Instead: "We thought you and [mentor] might click — both of you are interested in basketball, he works in construction, you've been thinking about job training. We're going to see if you want to get to know each other." Position the mentor as someone who actually chose them, not someone assigned out of obligation. Have the mentor say explicitly: "I wanted to work with you because of X" (something specific the teen shared in intake).

* **Prepare mentors for the weight of being chosen through role-play.** In mentor training, do a brief scripted exercise: mentor says "I'm excited to meet you" and the teen responds with defensiveness, disbelief, or emotional flatness. Debrief: "Notice how you almost apologized for being enthusiastic? That's the trap. This kid hasn't had reliable adults. Your genuine excitement is not pity — it's permission for them to matter." Give mentors language: "I'm looking forward to seeing you because I want to know you better" beats "I'm here to help you change."

* **Track mentor motivations and address savior narratives early.** During intake, when mentors share why they want to volunteer, listen for savior energy: "I want to make a difference in a struggling kid's life." Gently interrupt: "What if the relationship changed you as much as it changes them?" Suggest reframing: "I want to get to know someone with a different life experience than mine." Small shift, big difference in how the teen will experience the relationship. Do this again in month-one check-in if you detect the mentor framing the relationship as rescue.

## What If The Match Were The Program

* **Build a "match design sprint" — dedicate 60% of program energy to pairing, 40% to everything else.** Most programs spend 20% of effort on intake and 80% on curricula, training, and metrics. Reverse that. Invest deeply in understanding each volunteer and teen, in surfacing preferences, in having real conversations. Once the match is solid, even mediocre training and loose protocols often work. Once the match is weak, perfect training and strict safety protocols can't save it. Allocate resources to match design: coordinator time for deep intake interviews, data tracking, iteration.

* **Create a "match success statement" that replaces program goals.** Instead of "Increase school attendance by 15%" or "Improve mentor retention to 80%," write: "90% of matches report genuine connection by month 6; 85% continue past year one." Make match strength your north star. Let everything downstream (training curriculum, check-in frequency, outcome metrics) serve match durability rather than the reverse. This is a reorientation, not a strategy, but it shifts every micro-decision toward relational architecture.

* **Design the first meeting as a "friendship prototype," not an intake.** Instead of forms, protocols, and goal-setting, have mentors and teens spend their first two hours together doing something the teen chose: grab food, shoot hoops, walk around the neighborhood. The mentor's job: be present, be curious, share something real about themselves. Capture notes afterward: What did you learn about them? What surprised you? What do you want to do next time? Build the relationship first; layer in structure once you know what you're protecting.

## The Turning-Point Moment

* **Identify the moment empirically after the fact, then design for it.** Interview your year-one successful matches at month 6 and ask: "When did you decide this person was genuinely trustworthy?" Document the answers — was it when the mentor admitted failure? When the teen felt the mentor listened without judgment? When they laughed together? Use these stories to train year-two mentors: "Here's what trustworthiness looks like in practice. Watch for the moment you stop trying to impress and just show up as yourself."

* **Design a deliberate vulnerability moment around week 4-6.** By week 4, initial awkwardness is fading but the relationship is still fragile. Create a low-stakes moment for the mentor to show authenticity: "Tell your mentee about a time you struggled with something" or "Share a failure you learned from." This gives the teen permission to be real too. It also tests the mentor's actual character: Can they be vulnerable without centering their own need for sympathy? Can they listen to the teen's response without pivoting back to advice? This is your proxy for trustworthiness.

* **Create a ritual around week 8-10 that marks relationship transition.** Maybe it's a small meal together at the mentor's home, or introducing the mentee to someone important to the mentor (a friend, family member, coworker), or a shared commitment to something the pair chose together. Mark it explicitly: "We've known each other for a few weeks now. I like how this is going. What about you?" This conversation surfaces whether the turning-point moment has arrived. If it hasn't, you have time to adjust the match before month 3.

## Matching Architecture From Labor Markets

* **Adapt the residency-match algorithm structure to your scale.** The residency match (NRMP) works by collecting mutual preference lists: residents rank programs, programs rank residents, and a stable-matching algorithm pairs them such that no unpaired pair would both prefer each other to their assigned match. You don't need the computational complexity, but you do need the information: Ask each mentor to rank their top 3 teen prospects based on intake profiles; ask each teen to rank their top 3 mentors. Compare the lists; prioritize matches where preferences overlap. Leave gaps if mutual preference is low rather than forcing mismatched pairs.

* **Structure mentor and teen intake to elicit preferences explicitly.** Mentor intake: "Tell us about a meaningful relationship you have outside your family. What made it work?" "What kinds of challenges or backgrounds do you think you'd be good at understanding?" "How much structure and planning do you like in relationships vs. spontaneity?" Teen intake (with a trusted interviewer, not a form): "Who do you admire — could be someone you know or someone famous? What do they do?" "What's something you're curious about right now?" "If you got to pick an adult to hang out with, what would that person be like?" Use these answers to create matchable preference signals.

* **Surface communication style and availability alignment explicitly.** Two variables that often surprise people: How quickly does the mentor need responses? How does the teen prefer to communicate (in-person only, text okay, phone awkward)? These logistical misalignments derail matches as often as personality clashes do. A mentor who wants daily check-ins paired with a teen who communicates in monthly dumps will create friction. Ask these directly and weight them heavily in matching.

## Match Quality as Ecological Fit Problem

* **Distinguish between screening and niche-fitting.** Background checks and training address the fundamental niche — "Can this person legally be a mentor?" Realized niche — "Will this pair actually thrive?" — comes from studying what conditions enable genuine developmental relationships. Separate these in your thinking. Everyone who passes screening can theoretically mentor any teen. But realized quality depends on personality fit, communication style, shared geography, mutual curiosity, and peer dynamics within the broader volunteer cohort.

* **Use a "realized niche" matching template.** For each mentor, document: What types of life challenges do they handle well? What communication style brings out their best mentoring instincts? Do they do better with structured activities or unstructured hanging out? For each teen, document: What kinds of adults have they connected with before? What topics light them up? Are they more comfortable with structure or flexibility? Match on realized-niche factors, not just fundamental-niche screening.

* **Monitor for mismatch signals and intervene early.** At month 2 check-in, ask: Is the mentor enjoying this? Is the teen initiating contact or waiting to be contacted? Is there laughter in your conversations? These are ecological-fit signals. If the mentor is going through the motions and the teen is passive, you likely have a fundamental-niche match (they're both "fine" mentors and mentees) but a realized-niche mismatch. Offer to reorganize early rather than letting it coast until month 12 when renewal decisions get harder.

* **Recruit for niche diversity, not homogeneity.** You have 40 volunteers and maybe 15-20 teens in year one. You need mentors with different realized niches: some who work best with reserved teens, some with talkative ones; some who thrive with structure, some with spontaneity; some best with civic-minded activities, some with recreation. Build this diversity intentionally so you have matching options. A coordinator's job is niche-matching, not persuading people to work outside their realized niche.
