# Outcome Measurement, Evidence, and Program Learning - The Pragmatist

---

## Outcome Measurement Without Overburdening Teens or Making Mentors Feel Surveilled

* **Three-touchpoint pulse check system.** Deploy a 3-4 question pulse survey every 6 months (not quarterly—that's fatigue-inducing) administered by a neutral third party (school counselor, not your staff), asking only: (1) "Do you feel heard by your mentor?" (5-point scale), (2) "Has [mentor name] helped you think through something that mattered?" (yes/no), (3) "Would you keep meeting if you could choose?" (yes/no/unsure). Takes 5 minutes, requires no data processing by mentors, and surfaces early warning signs (declining "yes" rate signals match trouble). Pair with 2-3 structured mentor reflections per year where mentors write (not submit, just write) about a specific conversation that felt meaningful—this builds mentor self-awareness without external judgment.

* **Mentor observation log as leading indicator.** Have mentors track one simple metric: "number of times this week I initiated contact or brought something intentionally to discuss (not just showed up)." This is feasible, mentor-controlled, and directly observable. A 6-month trend of declining initiation signals relationship drift faster than any teen survey. Pair with monthly "spot check" conversations (15 minutes, phone, not written) where a program coordinator asks each mentor one open question: "What's one thing you've learned about [teen] that surprised you?" This generates qualitative data without survey fatigue, gives mentors a reflective forum, and costs minimal staff time.

* **School-based proxy without program ownership.** Partner with school counselors to flag attendance and discipline data quarterly (with guardian consent built into enrollment). You don't collect the data; the school confirms it. Teen doesn't feel like your program is surveilling them separately from the school systems already doing so. You get three data points per year from an institution that already has the teen's attention. This honors the "light touch" constraint while giving foundation funders a concrete metric. Frame it as: "We partner with schools to understand context, not to generate program metrics."

## Instrument Selection, Weighting, and the Pull Toward Easy Proxies

* **Design the measurement weight backward from the program theory.** Start with: "The mentor-teen relationship is the core mechanism; everything else is a side effect." Then weight your measurement as: (1) relationship quality signals = 50% (pulse check, mentor observation of engagement, counselor note of behavioral shift), (2) teen-reported outcome = 30% (academic or behavioral metric that the teen cares about, self-identified at intake, not imposed), (3) organizational metric = 20% (attendance at meetings, mentor retention, program safety incidents). This weighting forces honest conversation about what you're actually measuring. The trap is that easy metrics (attendance, mentor satisfaction) naturally float to 70%+ because they're cheap to collect. Weighting them low from the start prevents drift.

* **Create a "metric distortion risk" log.** Every month, have your program lead ask: "Which of our three metrics could a mentor unconsciously optimize for at the expense of the teen's genuine growth?" Example: if you heavily weight "teen attends 48 meetings per year," mentors might over-schedule or push participation even when the teen has homework or conflict. Document the risk, then ask: "What guardrail prevents this?" (In this case: mentors have authority to cancel or reschedule without penalty; you measure compliance via mentor-initiated contact, not teen attendance). This log becomes part of quarterly evaluation and helps you catch metric creep before it distorts practice.

* **Separate "success story" metrics from "program health" metrics.** Temptation is to use the same instruments for both impact reporting and internal learning. Instead: program health metrics (relationship quality, mentor burnout, match dissolution reasons) are for internal use and get scrutiny; impact metrics (GPA change, attendance, recidivism) are for external funders and are framed conservatively ("we observed X improvement in participants who completed 12+ months"). This separation prevents the unconscious pull to oversell internal metrics when external funders want a story.

## Baseline Data, Comparison Strategy, and Alignment with Evidence-Registry Trajectories

* **Lean baseline plus matched-cohort design.** At enrollment, collect only what's actionable: (1) teen's self-identified goal or concern, (2) current school attendance rate (from school record), (3) one mentor observational prompt ("What does this teen care about?"). Don't build a 30-field intake form—that creates false precision and overwhelms staff. For comparison: enroll a "wait-list control" cohort (youth identified but not yet matched) and compare attendance trends over a 12-month window using school data. Cost: negligible. Validity: high enough for a $15K grant report. Funders increasingly expect this structure because NIH and NSF registries flag it as credible.

* **Start with "What Works Clearinghouse Tier 2" design, plan to Tier 3.** WCC Tier 2 = matched historical cohort or waitlist control with clear pre/post measures. Your year 1 design (new program, limited data infrastructure) should target Tier 2: simple pre measure, 12-month outcome, school data as external validation. Document your design choices in a one-page "evaluation protocol" and send it to your funder 30 days after launch—shows rigor, invites funder feedback early, and prevents the year-2 surprise where they ask "why didn't you collect X?" Plan for year 2 or year 3 to scale to Tier 3 (randomized selection into wait-list control) if the funder signals interest and you have the infrastructure.

* **Use the school district as your baseline repository.** Don't build a separate database. Work with school counselors to agree on three data points pulled from their systems quarterly: (1) attendance rate, (2) discipline incidents, (3) counselor observation of engagement (one sentence per teen). You get longitudinal data that predates your program (year-prior history), continuity after the teen ages out, and zero duplication. Cost to your program: a data-sharing agreement (one-time lift) and a quarterly 30-minute call with the district. Massive credibility gain with future funders.

## Match Dissolution Feedback Loop and Selection Bias in Public Success Stories

* **Structured debrief protocol with teeth.** When a match dissolves, conduct a 20-minute conversation within two weeks between (1) the mentor, (2) the teen (separately), (3) the coordinator. Use a simple template: "What worked? What didn't? What surprised you? What would you do differently?" Synthesize findings into a one-page "dissolution note" that includes: probable cause (mismatch, mentor boundary drift, teen life disruption, external pressure), early warning signs (had we caught this earlier), and one specific change to screening or training as a result. File it. When you train the next mentor cohort, surface 2-3 dissolution stories anonymously as case studies: "Here's what we learned when a match didn't work." This prevents the pattern where dissolutions are treated as failures to bury rather than data to harvest.

* **Youth consent for any story shared beyond staff.** Policy: any match featured in congregational communications, social media, or foundation reports requires signed consent from the teen (not just guardian) explicitly stating what will be shared and where. If the teen is under 15 or from juvenile justice referral, raise the bar: they must have the option to review the story and edit it. This shifts the power—the teen becomes a co-author rather than a subject—and catches the natural organizational bias toward "success" narratives (dissolutions, messiness, ambiguity rarely get shared).

* **Anonymous aggregate learning loop.** Create a simple one-pager distributed quarterly to mentors and staff: "3 things we learned this quarter from match feedback." Example: "Mentors who checked in about homework struggles before they escalated avoided match tension; we're adding this to training." "Teens consistently said unstructured time (walking, sitting) felt safer than structured activities; coordinators are being coached to encourage flexibility." This keeps learning visible, prevents it from siloing in the program director's head, and models continuous improvement as normal rather than reactive.

## What If We Measured Outcomes by What Mentors Learned

* **Mentor learning outcomes as formal assessment.** Flip your outcome framework: for each mentor, identify one specific area of growth that the mentorship asks them to build—sitting with ambiguity, understanding systemic barriers facing low-income teens, tolerating different communication styles, navigating institutional systems (schools, juvenile justice). Use a simple pre/post instrument: 5-point scale, "I can tolerate prolonged discomfort when a teen is processing conflict" or "I understand the barriers teens in this neighborhood face with school discipline." Administered at enrollment and month 12. You'll likely see 1-2 point improvement in mentors and nearly zero change in a control group (waitlist). This data shifts your narrative from "we fixed teens" to "we built adult capacity."

* **Document the specific shifts mentors underwent.** Invite willing mentors into a brief qualitative interview (30 minutes, recorded with consent) at month 12: "Tell me about a moment when you realized you didn't understand this teen's perspective, and what you learned." Transcribe 3-5 of these and share anonymously with your funder and board. The richness reveals what generic outcome metrics hide: mentors learning to sit with not-knowing, mentors realizing their assumptions about what "at-risk" means, mentors discovering their own adolescent wounds. This becomes your "secret" outcome—not in the formal report, but in the community-facing story that attracts the next cohort of volunteer mentors.

* **Community-facing messaging about mutual growth.** Reframe public messaging: "We pair caring adults with teens who challenge us all to grow." Instead of positioning mentors as saviors, position them as learners in relationship with teens who are also learners. This attracts a different volunteer pool (people curious about their own growth, not just people wanting to "help") and lowers the implicit pressure for mentors to have answers.

## What If We Measured Outcomes as Shifts in Community Social Physics

* **Network mapping pre and post.** At year 1 and year 3, conduct a simple network survey with youth and adults in the community: "Name up to 5 people from different ages/groups you trust or spend time with. Before our program existed, would that list have included [mentor's age group/different background]?" You're measuring weak-tie formation across demographic lines. You likely can't attribute causation (selection bias is real), but the trend becomes visible. If mentors and teens report increased cross-age trust, that's a community-level signal worth documenting.

* **Institutional relationship deepening as outcome.** Track the health of partnerships—depth of school engagement, frequency of juvenile justice case worker check-ins, whether community health providers are now aware of the program. These aren't teen metrics; they're system metrics. A pragmatic proxy: count the number of school counselors, police officers, or family court personnel who proactively refer youth to your program by year 2. This signals institutional buy-in and suggests the program has shifted how adults in the system perceive their role.

* **Long-term tracking of mentor persistence and ambassador effect.** Five years out, how many of your first-year mentors are still active or have become advocates/donors/trainers? How many non-participant community adults know about the program because a mentor talked about it? This is community social physics: are mentors creating ripples? Track it through program retention, word-of-mouth referrals (ask new mentors "how did you hear about us?"), and a biennial "what happened to past mentors" survey. The outcome isn't necessarily that teens improved—it's that a new generation of adult relationships to youth became normalized in the neighborhood.

## The Measure That Matters in Year Three

* **Collect the teen's own narrative at match end and again at year 3.** At month 12 (match end), ask each teen: "Write (or record, or draw) what was important about this relationship and what changed for you. Anything." Don't coach the answer; let them define the outcome. Archive these. At year 3, reach out to teens who are now 18-20 and ask again: "Looking back, what did that mentorship mean?" Pair their year 3 voice with their year 1 voice—that's your longitudinal outcome story. It won't generalize; it won't appear in impact matrices. But when a funder asks "did this work?" the story is in the teen's own words across time.

* **A third-party oral history approach.** Hire a local journalist or graduate student ($2K-3K) to conduct 8-10 recorded interviews with successful match closures at year 2, asking open-ended questions: "Tell me about a moment when things shifted." Transcribe, synthesize themes, and produce a 15-page narrative document (not a formal report) that captures how the program showed up in teens' lives. This costs little, generates rich qualitative data that surveys miss, and becomes a fundraising artifact that moves potential donors in ways outcome matrices cannot.

* **Mentor and teen co-reflection as closing ritual.** Instead of a final survey, design a structured closing conversation between mentor and teen (facilitated by coordinator if tension exists) asking: "What worked in how we connected? What would help the next pair? What surprised you?" Document this conversation and file it. The outcome data isn't in answers to your questions—it's in the quality of the relationship itself, evidenced by willingness to reflect honestly together at the end.

## Outcomes as Lagging Indicators and the Need for Leading Proxies

* **Weekly mentor initiative as primary leading indicator.** The single strongest predictor of relationship quality is whether the mentor initiates contact or brings intentional content to meetings—not whether the teen shows up. Measure: "How many weeks this month did the mentor initiate (texted, called, suggested activity) without waiting for the teen to respond?" Target: 3+ weeks per month. This is detectable by week 3 and predicts month 12 outcomes far better than attendance. Train mentors on this: initiation is relationship-building; if they're waiting for the teen to want to meet, the match is already fraying.

* **School-based behavioral leading indicators.** Partner with school counselors to share three early signals visible within 8 weeks: (1) teen turned in homework even once when they hadn't before, (2) counselor noticed teen engaged in a conversation or group activity, (3) zero new discipline incidents (or decline from baseline trend). These aren't program outcomes; they're observable signals that something shifted. They correlate strongly with sustained mentorship relationships because they reflect a teen's increased engagement with school—a leading indicator of future attendance and GPA improvement.

* **Mentor self-reported confidence and belonging as proxies.** Ask mentors monthly: "On a scale of 1-5, how confident do you feel understanding this teen's perspective?" and "How much does this relationship feel like a genuine friendship (not charity)?" Upward trend in these two metrics (especially the second) by month 4-6 predicts long-term match success. When these decline, it's a 6-week leading indicator of match dissolution. This data is cheap to collect (mentors email one line to coordinator), actionable (declining confidence triggers check-in), and directly tied to program theory (genuine relationship is the mechanism).

* **Frequency of unprompted teen communication.** Has the teen texted or called the mentor about something non-scheduled? Are they sharing struggles or asking advice? By month 3, successful matches show at least 1-2 instances of teen-initiated contact. This is a leading indicator because it reflects psychological safety and trust formation—core relationship precursors to later outcomes. Track it through mentor logs or mentor-reported anecdotes (not surveillance).

## Cross-System Data as Triangulation

* **Create a simple data-sharing protocol with three institutions.** Develop a one-page agreement with (1) your partner school district, (2) local juvenile justice (if applicable), and (3) a community health provider. The protocol specifies: who can request data about which teens, what data points are exchanged, what stays confidential, and when. For data points, propose a narrow set: (1) school: attendance, GPA, discipline incidents (quarterly), (2) juvenile justice: compliance status, incident reports (if applicable, quarterly), (3) health provider: whether teen kept scheduled appointments (aggregate, not detailed diagnosis). Each institution provides data; you don't manage cross-institution access (you're not the hub).

* **Synthesized "progress view" owned by the teen (with guardian approval).** At month 6 and month 12, invite the teen into a 30-minute conversation with the program coordinator where you review together the three views: mentor observations of engagement and growth, school attendance/discipline trend, and (if applicable) juvenile justice compliance or health provider follow-through. The teen becomes the curator of their own progress narrative. This honors their agency, prevents any single institution from defining their trajectory, and creates a safeguard against data being used against them (e.g., juvenile justice using program metrics in sentencing).

* **Privacy-first: mentor view only, no central database.** The mentor holds a simple "progress snapshot" that they share with the teen periodically: "Here's what I've noticed about how you're showing up at school," "I heard from your counselor that..." Mentors don't have access to raw data; they get one-sentence summaries that protect privacy. The program coordinator holds the full picture (with explicit consent) for evaluation, but this data is not shared institution-to-institution without explicit teen/guardian opt-in per exchange. This design prevents the mission creep where program data intended for relationship-building gets weaponized by institutional systems.
