# Outcome Measurement, Evidence, and Program Learning - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Outcome Measurement Without Overburdening Teens

* **Risk: Measurement creates micro-surveillance that replicates the assessment trauma teens already experience.** A "lightweight" survey tool, even administered quarterly, can subtly shift the mentor relationship from unconditional regard toward evaluation. The teen learns: "I am here to provide data about my improvement," not "I am here to be known." A light touch isn't light enough if it reinforces the institutional systems that already surrounded the teen. Consider instead: mentors document *their own* observations in a simple shared journal, shared with the teen. The teen sees what the mentor noticed about them. Measurement becomes mutual visibility rather than one-way assessment.

* **Risk: "Leading indicators detectable within weeks" is a seductive trap that assumes rapid relationship change is the goal.** Many teens who most need mentoring will show no visible progress for months—they're testing boundaries, building trust through absence of consequence. Measurement systems biased toward early wins may bias mentors toward teens already on an upward trajectory (selection bias) and away from those who need the longest runway. Mitigate by establishing a "measurement-free observation window" of the first 6 months, during which only the mentor and teen know whether progress is happening. Formalize measurement only after trust is established.

* **Risk: Separating "measurement" from "the relationship itself" assumes they can be kept apart.** They cannot. Any measurement framework will shape mentor attention. Instead of trying to measure lightly, embed evidence into the natural rhythm of the relationship: mentors and teens co-create a personal goal (chosen by the teen, not prescribed) and check in on it monthly. The goal itself becomes the metric, owned by both parties. This approach measures what the teen actually cares about and avoids the surveillance feeling.

* **Risk: Foundation funders need numbers, so "light measurement" is actually a time bomb.** After 18 months, the foundation will ask: "What outcomes did you achieve?" If you have only anecdotes and journal notes, you will face pressure to retroactively construct metrics or face funding loss. This pressure will then corrupt next year's program design. Frontload this conversation: use the seed-grant period to co-design measurement with the foundation. Propose a "narrative-plus-data hybrid" where you capture one structured outcome (e.g., school enrollment status at year-end) plus monthly mentor reflections. This gives you real data while preserving relationship integrity.

* **Risk: Different teens have different meaningful outcomes.** For a teen in court-involvement, "compliance with court orders" matters. For a high-school dropout, "re-enrollment" matters. For one dealing with family trauma, "days without crisis contact" or "initiating mentorship messages" might be the leading edge. A one-size metric will fail some teens while over-measuring others. Mitigate by letting each teen's intake process identify 1-2 domains that matter most to their situation, then measure those. This is personalized measurement, not light measurement.

## Instrument Selection, Weighting, and the Pull Toward Easy Proxies

* **Risk: "Mentor self-reported satisfaction" is the easiest metric and therefore the most dangerous.** Mentors will unconsciously (or consciously) rate their work higher if the program is asking them for assessments. This is well-documented in volunteer research—self-reported effort and impact are inflated by 40-60%. You will measure your mentors' satisfaction with themselves, not program effectiveness. Mitigate: triangulate every metric. Pair mentor self-assessment with teen self-assessment and with third-party observation (school counselor or JJ caseworker). A metric unsupported by at least two other sources gets weighted to zero.

* **Risk: Attendance at meetings is the worst possible proxy and will dominate measurement by default.** Why? Because it's easy to count, mentors can influence it directly, and funders understand it. But attendance is not relationship quality—a teen might show up while withdrawn; a teen might miss one meeting but have sent three voice messages to the mentor. The worse the risk, the more likely you'll optimize for the wrong metric. Propose instead: shift to "consistency of connection" where mentors log all forms of contact (text, call, in-person, social media check-in), weighted by frequency and mentor-initiated vs. teen-initiated. This captures what actually matters while remaining countable.

* **Risk: Weighting schemes are invisible assumptions that embed program bias.** If you weight "GPA improvement" at 40% and "attendance" at 30%, you've just told mentors that academic outcomes matter twice as much as relationship consistency. Teens will sense this. Mentors will steer toward SAT prep. By what calculation did you decide on 40/30? Most likely: gut feeling or foundation proposal requirements. Mitigate by publishing your weighting logic and the evidence behind it. For each metric, state: "We weight this at X% because research shows..." This transparency lets you adjust midstream and prevents the weights from calcifying into dogma.

* **Risk: What gets measured will deterministically shape effort over time.** Mentors are not cynical, but they're human. If your program tracks and reports "% of teens with GPA improvement," mentors will increasingly focus on homework help. If you track "weekly meeting attendance," they'll prioritize scheduling. This isn't corruption; it's natural response to incentives. You cannot design a measurement system that doesn't change behavior. Acknowledge this. Propose measuring a *process* metric instead of an outcome metric: "frequency and quality of conversations touching on domains the teen identified as important." This avoids the trap of saying "we measure outcomes" when you're really measuring "what mentors do."

* **Risk: "What gets measured" also shapes who gets served and who gets excluded.** Mentors might unconsciously preferentially match with teens they perceive as higher-likelihood success stories (because success feels good and gets measured). Teens in the deepest crisis stay unmatched longer. Mitigate with a "measurement equity audit" early in year one: disaggregate all metrics by teen profile (referred through school vs. JJ, first teen served by this mentor vs. repeat, family stability level). If outcomes cluster by profile, you've discovered that your measurement system is biasing selection. Adjust immediately.

## Baseline Data, Comparison Strategy, and Evidence-Registry Alignment

* **Risk: A baseline without a comparison group is just a snapshot—it doesn't prove change.** You collect baseline school attendance on intake (let's say 65% of program teens have chronic absenteeism at enrollment). One year later, baseline group improved to 75%. Did the mentorship cause this, or would they have improved anyway? Without a comparison group (matched school cohort not in mentoring, or a waitlist control), you're measuring correlation without causation. Foundations increasingly want to see rigorous evidence, and this approach won't survive scrutiny. Mitigate: use a delayed-enrollment waitlist comparison. Recruit 60 teens, mentor 30, put 30 on a waitlist for year 2. Compare the two groups' one-year outcomes. This gives you causal inference without withholding service.

* **Risk: "Matched historical cohort" is methodologically weak and looks like we're trying to avoid rigor.** If you say "we looked at teens who enrolled in 2024 and compared them to the 2023 cohort," you've introduced a dozen confounds: changing school policies, different principal, different economic conditions, different volunteer pool. Funders trained in evidence will spot this. Propose instead: a "synthetic control" approach. Find three school cohorts from similar neighborhoods (similar poverty, school size, demographics) that didn't have a mentoring program. Use their trajectories as a comparison baseline. This is more rigorous and still feasible with a $15K budget.

* **Risk: Alignment with What Works Clearinghouse standards is a constraint that may distort your program.** WWC wants randomized controlled trials, strong validity of measures, and pre-registered hypotheses. Your program is faith-based, serving 30 teens, with a committed volunteer mentor pool (you can't randomize who gets mentored without ethical issues). Trying to fit into WWC frameworks will force you to: shrink your program to enable randomization, measure only outcomes that are "standardized instruments," and treat mentors as intervention delivery vehicles rather than relationships. Mitigate: choose a different evidence bar that fits your context. Propose a "SAMHSA replication study" framework where you document your program model in detail, measure the specific outcomes you hypothesize, and allow other organizations to replicate and compare findings. This is rigorous without being constraining.

* **Risk: Baseline data collection can traumatize teen participants.** Asking a 14-year-old who's been referred through juvenile justice to sit through an intake survey covering family structure, substance exposure, mental health, academic history, etc., replicates the processing they've already experienced. Baseline data is necessary, but the *how* matters enormously. Mitigate: embed baseline into the first few mentorship conversations, not an intake interview. The mentor gradually learns the teen's background over the first month, documenting key domains (school enrollment, living situation, JJ status, self-reported well-being) through natural conversation. Data collection becomes part of relationship building, not a discrete surveillance event.

* **Risk: Comparison strategy assumes the program's outcomes are linear and measurable within one year.** Mentorship's real effects may be invisible at 12 months and fully visible at 24 or 36 months. A year-one comparison showing "no significant effect" may lead to program termination right when effects are beginning. Mitigate by proposing a phased funding model: year-one funding is "relationship and trust building," year-two measurement focuses on "leading indicators," year-three assessment addresses "long-term outcomes." Communicate to funders upfront that some impacts won't be visible in year one. Request a three-year funding commitment or structured re-evaluation option.

## Match Dissolution Feedback Loop and Selection Bias in Public Success Stories

* **Risk: Match terminations carry the most important diagnostic information in the program, and you will have pressure to bury them.** When a mentor-teen pairing dissolves, that's failure data. But in a faith-based context where the congregation receives communications about the program, there will be social and reputational pressure to frame dissolutions quietly ("the match wasn't a good fit") or attribute them to teen behavior rather than program design. This pressure will prevent you from learning. The same match will fail again with the next pair. Mitigate: require a structured "dissolution debrief" within two weeks of any match ending, conducted by the program director with both mentor and teen (separately). Document: Why did it end? What warning signs appeared? What would have prevented this? Store these reports in a secure, confidential database. Quarterly, run a synthesis to identify patterns (e.g., "mentors without childcare support have 40% higher dissolution rates"). Feed findings directly into training and screening.

* **Risk: Selection bias in reported successes will be severe, and it will be invisible.** The mentor-teen pairs you publicize are guaranteed to be non-representative. You'll share stories about the teen who got into college, the mentor who "changed a life," the kid who re-enrolled in school. You won't share the story of the match that ended in month 6 because the mentor's father died and they couldn't focus. You won't share the teen whose situation is unchanged but who now trusts one adult. This bias is not malice—it's narrative human nature. But it creates a false public understanding of what the program achieves, which then shapes recruitment of "promising" teens and mentors. Mitigate: before sharing any story publicly, audit it for selection bias. What percentage of the program do successful outcomes represent? If 60% of matches are steady or positive and 40% dissolve or plateau, your public narrative should reflect this ratio. Share a story of a match that didn't "work out" as often as you share a triumph.

* **Risk: Teen consent for sharing is assumed, not verified.** The guardian signs consent to enroll in the program. The program then shares the teen's story on social media or in a foundation report. Legally compliant? Maybe. Ethically sound? Different question. A 13-year-old didn't knowingly consent to having their educational progress publicized to strangers. As they mature and see their story online, they may feel violated—privacy is a form of respect they never got to give. Mitigate: separate "program participation consent" from "story-sharing consent." Obtain explicit opt-in (from both teen and guardian) for any public narrative. When soliciting, explain exactly how the story will be shared (social media, foundation report, congregation newsletter). Offer anonymity as default. Let the teen write their own narrative if they want to share. This changes the power dynamic from program-documenting-teen to teen-choosing-disclosure.

* **Risk: The success story becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy filter.** You publish stories about the mentors and teens you're most proud of. Recruitment campaigns feature these success stories. New volunteers apply saying "I want to have the impact I saw in that story." New teen recruits are screened partly on whether they fit the profile of past successes. Over time, your program becomes homogeneous—recruiting the "mentorability" teens and the "naturally gifted" mentors. The teens in deepest crisis, hardest to serve, remain underrepresented. Mitigate: use outcome data to identify the *gaps* between reported success stories and actual program composition. Does 90% of your public narrative come from 20% of your program? Which teens or mentors are never in the stories? Deliberately create a "counter-narrative" featuring a match that took months to establish trust, a mentor who struggled, a teen whose progress was invisible until month 10. This shows the actual work of mentoring.

* **Risk: Foundation reports driven by success narratives will create pressure to misrepresent baseline outcomes.** When the foundation calls asking for results, you'll feel subtle pressure to highlight gains and minimize non-progress. "This teen's attendance improved from 60% to 68%"—technically true, but you omit that she was also arrested in month 9. This isn't lying; it's selective framing. It's also systematically biased. Mitigate: use a third-party evaluator (even a low-cost grad student) to audit your reported outcomes against your raw data quarterly. The evaluator submits a confidential report to the board identifying any framing that misrepresents outcomes. This creates accountability that prevents drift.

## What If We Measured Outcomes by What Mentors Learned

* **Risk: Proposing "measure mentor learning instead of teen outcomes" sounds like we're avoiding accountability.** The foundation didn't fund adult development; it funded teen outcomes. If you shift the frame, they'll suspect mission drift or that you've given up on the teens. Funders and boards will reject this, even though the idea has real merit. Mitigate: don't replace teen measurement with mentor measurement; add it. Propose a "dual measurement system": teen-focused outcomes (attendance, engagement, leading indicators of change) tracked for accountability, *plus* mentor-learning outcomes tracked for program depth. The teen outcomes address the funder's primary goal; the mentor learning is the added value that makes the program sustainable and replicable.

* **Risk: "Measuring what mentors learned" is vague and requires developing entirely new instruments.** How do you quantify a mentor becoming more comfortable with discomfort? You'd need psychometric tools (Self-Compassion Scale, Distress Tolerance Inventory, something custom). These cost money and expertise you don't have. You'd end up with weak self-report surveys that measure satisfaction, not actual learning. The data would be unconvincing. Mitigate: if you pursue this, partner with a local university research team (masters student, faculty advisor) to design valid instruments. Offer them: access to your program as a research site, consent from mentors to participate in a study on mentor development, support for their thesis. This leverages university expertise while serving their needs.

* **Risk: Measuring mentor change assumes mentors have capacity to be changed by this experience.** Some mentors come in wanting to be helpers, advisors, authority figures. A teen's perspective won't shift them; it will frustrate them. If they leave the program feeling "I did my best but this kid wasn't ready," they haven't learned anything. They've blamed the teen. Measurement won't capture this resistance. Mitigate: front-load training that explicitly names that mentors will be challenged, and that growth involves discomfort. Recruit mentors with language like: "Are you willing to be unsettled?" Measure mentor learning through structured interviews (not surveys) where they reflect on moments they felt challenged or surprised. This captures growth in a way that surveys won't.

* **Risk: Inverting the measurement direction can inadvertently diminish teen agency and positioning.** If the program narrative becomes "the church members learned compassion from the teens," the teen is repositioned as a teaching object for adult development rather than a primary stakeholder. This sounds more respectful than "measuring teen metrics," but it's subtly paternalistic—the teen becomes a teaching tool. Mitigate: if you pursue dual measurement, ensure the teen learning system is equally robust and equally publicized. Use language that treats the relationship as mutual: "Both mentors and teens grow through this work." Include teens in evaluation design so they help decide what mentor learning should look like.

## What If a Hundred Successful Matches Changed the Neighborhood's Social Physics

* **Risk: Community-level outcomes are difficult to attribute, expensive to measure, and almost impossible to connect to your program's causal contribution.** If neighborhoodwide adult-youth relations improve, was it because of your 30 mentor pairs or was it because a new community center opened, or the economy improved, or a charismatic new school principal? The counterfactual is unknowable. A funder asking for evidence of your program's impact will be unsatisfied with "the neighborhood social fabric changed" as a outcome claim. Mitigate: don't try to claim causality for neighborhood-level change. Instead, reframe your program as a "seed intervention" that models new relationship norms, and measure its replication and local diffusion: How many other adults in the congregation report that they changed how they relate to young people after seeing mentor pairs? How many mentors report new conversations with people outside the program about what they're learning? This is community change through cultural diffusion, not direct program effect.

* **Risk: Measuring "social physics" requires ethnographic and sociological methods that are expensive and don't produce the quantified numbers that foundations want.** You could hire an evaluator to do qualitative research documenting shifts in how adults and youth interact across the neighborhood—interviews, observational notes, network analysis. This would be rich and meaningful. It would cost $40-80K over two years, which is 2.5-5x your total program budget. You won't have the resources. Mitigate: partner with a university. Offer a sociology or social work faculty member access to your community for research in exchange for conducting the evaluation. Many academics want fieldwork sites; your program offers theirs. This solves the resource problem and produces publishable research, which benefits both parties.

* **Risk: Optimizing the program for "neighborhood social physics" may cause you to neglect the individual teen's immediate needs.** If the goal is "community transformation," you might place mentors in ways that maximize neighborhood visibility (pairing mentors and teens in shared community spaces) rather than in ways that maximize individual teen benefit (which might mean mentors visiting the teen's neighborhood, their community center, their world). You could inadvertently use teens as props in a community development story. Mitigate: keep the primary goal at the individual level (meaningful relationship, teen-identified support) and treat community-level change as an emergent property you observe and document, not an outcome you engineer. This prevents instrumentalizing the teen.

* **Risk: The vision of "community transformation" is compelling but vague enough that it becomes a story staff tell themselves rather than a measurable program aim.** By year three, the program director reports that "the neighborhood has transformed" and points to anecdotes. The board nods. But did anything actually change? Could it be measured? Is the improvement sustainable? Without definition, this becomes magical thinking. Mitigate: if you pursue this theory of change, define it concretely early. What would a "transformed neighborhood" look like? (e.g., "30% increase in cross-generational mentoring relationships outside this program," "school attendance in the service area increased by 5 percentage points," "community members report increased trust across racial/generational lines on a validated social cohesion scale"). Measure these defined outcomes. This keeps the vision grounded and accountable.

## The Measure That Matters in Year Three

* **Risk: "The measure that matters" in year three might directly contradict the measures you're tracking in years one and two.** If year one is measuring "attendance consistency," year two is measuring "teen self-reported trust," and year three is asking "has the teen's life materially changed?"—these are different measurement systems. Year-three data might reveal that teens who attended every meeting didn't report high trust, and teens with high trust didn't show academic improvement. You'll face a reckoning: which measures actually mattered? Mitigate: build a "measurement strategy map" before year one starts, connecting all measures in a causal chain. What's the theory of change? Relationship quality (leading indicator) → trust (mid-term indicator) → durable life changes (long-term outcome). Every measure you collect should fit this chain. When you analyze year-three data, the chain either holds or it doesn't, and you learn something crucial about your program.

* **Risk: Foundation funders will want year-three data to look like success, and there will be pressure to choose measurement endpoints that fit that narrative.** If you measure "high school graduation," you'll know in year three if it happened. If you measure "long-term social connection," that requires a 10-year study. Funders will push toward the former because it's conclusive and looks good. But if the real outcome is "the teen has an adult they can call in crisis," that may not show up in graduation rates. You'll measure the wrong thing to satisfy funding pressure. Mitigate: negotiate measurement endpoints with the funder at the start. Propose a portfolio: (1) one hard outcome (e.g., graduation or no new JJ charges), (2) one mid-range outcome (e.g., trust score, school engagement), and (3) one narrative outcome (open-ended teen reflections on how the relationship mattered). This diversified approach prevents over-reliance on any single proxy.

* **Risk: The teen's voice will be mediated by adults throughout—mentors reporting on teens, counselors assessing teens, researchers collecting data from teens.** By year three, whose voice tells the story? If it's the mentor's story ("this teen has grown"), the teen might agree or might feel misrepresented. Whose voice counts as "evidence that the relationship was worth building"? Mitigate: commit early to co-authoring the year-three story. In year two, ask each teen: "What would you want people to know about this relationship in two years? What would count as success to you?" Capture their answer (voice message, written reflection, interview). In year three, weave their framing into the report alongside the data. Use statements like "The program's evidence includes both quantitative outcomes (X reduced, Y improved) and participant narratives. Here's what teens said mattered: [direct quotes]." This shares interpretive authority.

* **Risk: Data gathered in a way that reduced the teen to a metric *during* the program will poison the year-three story.** If years one and two involved surveys, intake forms, progress reports, and repeated assessment, the teen experienced themselves as a data object, not a person. By year three, asking them to reflect on a meaningful relationship contradicts the objectifying experience they just had. They might say "it was helpful" because they've learned that's the expected response, not because the relationship was actually transformative. Mitigate: use years one and two to build a different measurement culture. Make data collection relational, not surveillance-based. Let the teen see the data about themselves. Involve them in interpretation. By year three, when you ask "what mattered?", they'll have experienced a relationship, not a study. Their answer will be more authentic.

* **Risk: "The story you want to tell in year three" will inevitably be shaped by which teens completed the program successfully.** The match that dissolved in month 4, the teen who aged out and aged out of the program, the mentor who had a health crisis—these teens' stories won't be prominent in your narrative. You'll tell the story of the 60% who stayed matched. This isn't deception, but it's selection bias. The "measure that matters" will systematically exclude the hardest cases. Mitigate: make a deliberate choice to tell at least one complete story of a non-completion—a match that ended, a teen whose situation stayed unchanged, a mentor who struggled. Include this in your year-three report with the same depth as your success narratives. This prevents the narrative from calcifying into distortion.

## Outcomes as Lagging Indicators and the Need for Leading Proxies

* **Risk: "Leading indicators" sounds like prediction, but most leading-indicator proposals are actually just surrogate outcomes that appear earlier without being validated as predictive.** Mentor self-reported trust might appear in month 2. But does it predict graduation in year 3? You don't know until you run a cohort through the full timeline. If you optimize the program for early leading indicators without validating their predictive value, you might boost something that doesn't actually drive long-term outcomes. This is a well-documented problem in metrics design (Campbell's Law). Mitigate: if you propose leading indicators, commit to a validation study. Measure the leading indicator in early cohorts, then follow those cohorts longitudinally to see if the indicator predicted the lagging outcome. Only after validation can you confidently use the leading indicator as a program signal.

* **Risk: "Frequency of mentor-initiated contact" is better than attendance, but it's not neutral—it will subtly pressure mentors to perform busyness.** A mentor trying to rack up weekly messages might send low-quality, surveillance-like contact that damages the relationship. A mentor who checks in less frequently but with thoughtful, deep conversations will look worse on the metric. You've just incentivized quantity over quality. Mitigate: pair frequency metrics with quality filters. Instead of "mentor-initiated contact count," measure "mentor-initiated contact, weighted by teen responsiveness." If the teen doesn't respond or responds negatively, the contact doesn't count or counts as negative. This prevents the metric from rewarding one-way performance.

* **Risk: "Teen self-reported trust score" sounds like it measures something real, but self-reported trust is vulnerable to demand characteristics—the teen will report high trust because they know it's being measured and they want to please the mentor or the program.** Month 2 trust score is likely inflated by this dynamic, not predictive of actual relationship depth. Mitigate: triangulate trust with behavioral indicators. Does the teen initiate contact? Do they disclose something personal without prompting? Do they attend without being reminded? Create a "trust composite" that includes both self-report and behavioral markers. This is more robust.

* **Risk: "Counselor observation of behavioral change" requires the counselor to notice change and attribute it to mentoring, which introduces interpretation bias.** A school counselor who knows a teen is in the mentoring program will notice positive changes and attribute them to mentoring (confirmation bias), while missing changes that don't fit their expectations. They're not being dishonest; they're human. Mitigate: use a "blind observation" approach where possible. Have a school counselor note classroom attendance and engagement for all students, without knowing which are in the mentoring program. Disaggregate the data by participation status afterward. This removes the attribution bias.

* **Risk: Leading indicators designed to "act on signals rather than waiting for lagging outcomes" will lead to program churn and misallocation of resources.** If a mentor-teen pair shows "warning signs" at month 3 (low contact frequency, low teen engagement score), you might intervene—reassign the mentor, change the match, increase support. But the pair might have just been in an early cooling period and would have bonded by month 6. Your intervention disrupts what would have succeeded. Without a lagging-outcome validation period, you can't know which early signals actually predict failure. Mitigate: establish a "wait-and-observe window" for the first six months, even if leading indicators look weak. Only if signals persist into month 6-9 do you intervene. Collect both early indicators and later outcomes, then analyze which early warnings were actually predictive. This prevents over-reactive program management.

* **Risk: Measurement systems designed around leading indicators will make months 1-6 hyper-visible while months 7-12 become invisible.** The program will focus on the early relationship and trust-building, because that's where the leading indicators live. What happens in months 9-12, when the relationship deepens but the leading indicators plateau? The program will have shifted its attention away, even though that period is crucial. Mitigate: design leading indicators that capture the *entire* relationship arc, not just early trust. Include: early indicators (trust, contact frequency), mid-term indicators (depth of conversation, teen-initiated problem-sharing), and late indicators (teen articulates personal growth, mentor reports relationship is "settled and real"). This keeps the program's attention distributed across the full program year.

## Cross-System Data as Triangulation

* **Risk: Data-sharing agreements that cross from school to mentoring program to juvenile justice system will require navigating three different privacy regimes (FERPA, program-specific confidentiality, and JJ system restrictions), and the legal and administrative burden may be prohibitive.** Each system has different consent requirements, different data retention rules, and different confidentiality standards. FERPA allows schools to share with organizations serving the student, but JJ systems are often locked down. You might spend 6 months negotiating and still end up with agreements so restrictive that data-sharing isn't practical. Mitigate: start with a pilot. Work with one school and one JJ caseworker on a single teen, documenting every privacy barrier you hit. Use this case to develop a template agreement. Once you've worked through the barriers once, replicating it is easier.

* **Risk: "Triangulation across vantage points" assumes that mentor, school, and JJ perspectives are measuring comparable constructs, but they're not.** The mentor sees "relational change" (trust, openness, willingness to be vulnerable). The school sees "institutional engagement" (attendance, classroom behavior, assignment completion). The JJ caseworker sees "legal compliance" (probation adherence, no new offenses). These are different things. Combining them into a single "progress view" requires translation and weighting decisions that are inherently subjective. You might synthesize data in a way that hides real conflict (mentor sees growth, school sees continued disengagement, JJ sees stability but no improvement). Mitigate: don't synthesize into a single view. Present the three perspectives side-by-side, noting where they align and where they diverge. A synthesis that says "this teen is succeeding" when JJ sees no change is misleading. Let the conflict be visible.

* **Risk: "Who owns the synthesized view" is a power question disguised as a technical one.** If the program director synthesizes and controls the integrated view, they shape how the teen's progress is interpreted. If the school controls it, academic framing dominates. If JJ controls it, compliance framing dominates. Whoever owns the synthesis owns the interpretation. This will become a point of institutional conflict. Mitigate: structure governance so no single actor owns the synthesis. Form a "teen progress team" that meets quarterly (mentor, school counselor, JJ caseworker if applicable, teen when appropriate) and collectively reviews the triangulated data. Decisions about intervention or adjustment come from the team, not from one stakeholder. This distributes power and prevents interpretation monopoly.

* **Risk: Cross-system data-sharing, even with good intentions, can increase surveillance of teens who are already over-surveilled.** A teen referred through JJ is already tracked by the court system. Adding mentoring data, school behavioral data, and JJ compliance data creates a more comprehensive surveillance apparatus. The teen experiences this as intensified observation. Transparency helps, but it doesn't eliminate the chilling effect. Mitigate: frame data-sharing as *the teen's tool*, not the system's. Position the integrated view as "here's how your mentors, school, and caseworker are seeing your progress," shared with the teen first, before it's used in any decision. Let the teen respond: "That's accurate," or "That misses X," or "I don't want that shared." This gives the teen some control over how they're known across systems.

* **Risk: Privacy constraints may be tighter than you anticipate, leaving you with no triangulation.** JJ records are confidential from schools and vice versa. A FERPA release might not cover juvenile justice sharing. You might design an elegant data-sharing plan only to learn that legal constraints make it impossible. Mitigate: begin this work with a conversation with a privacy lawyer (pro bono if possible). Ask: "What data can legally flow between these three systems?" before you design the measurement system. Build measurement around what's legally permissible, not around the ideal design.
