# Consent, Data Privacy, and Cross-Institutional Information Flow - The Lawyer

---

## Informed Consent for Teens Referred Through Juvenile Justice

* **Case-specific consent documents tied to statutory re-disclosure standards.** Most states align juvenile-records confidentiality with the Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention Act (JJDPA), which restricts re-disclosure of case information. Create a consent mechanism that (1) separates general program enrollment (parental/guardian signature only) from case-information sharing (teen + guardian co-signature, case-specific), and (2) explicitly references the state statute protecting the teen's records. The consent should state what specific information will be shared (e.g., "school attendance notes" vs. "court-ordered probation status") and that the program will not re-disclose case information except as required by law or with explicit written permission. This approach satisfies the statute's re-disclosure standard because the teen and guardian have consented to the specific transfer, and the program has not created or stored unlawful secondary copies.

* **"Assent" interviews separate from guardian consent to detect coercion.** Even when a guardian has signed, the statute's spirit—and the program's integrity—requires that the teen genuinely chooses to participate. Use a standardized assent script (administered by someone other than the referring case manager) in which the teen is asked directly: "Is this something you want to do?", "What do you think will happen?", "Are you worried about anything?", and "Can you stop anytime?" If the teen's answers suggest coercion or confusion, the program should decline enrollment regardless of guardian signature. Document these assent interviews; they become evidence that consent was informed and voluntary.

* **Data minimization in the intake form itself.** Only collect information strictly necessary for matching and safety assessment—not the full case narrative. If the juvenile justice system has referred the teen, collect: age, stated interests in a mentor, family structure (who has custody), and any safety flags relevant to matching (e.g., "prior substance use" without details; "trauma history" as a category, not a narrative). Do not request or store case disposition, offense details, or probation terms. This minimization automatically limits what the program can accidentally re-disclose.

* **Explicitly named authority to consent.** In the case of teens with multiple custodians or shifting guardianship, the intake process must clarify: whose signature is legally sufficient? This often requires checking with the juvenile justice caseworker ("Is the teen a ward of the state?", "Who has legal custody?") and documenting the answer on the enrollment form. Many teens in the juvenile justice system are still in parental custody even while under supervision; others are in foster care or state custody. The consent form's signature block must name the specific person(s) who can authorize the teen's participation.

## Consent Stack Across Multiple Referral Channels and Ward-of-State Edge Cases

* **Three-layer consent sequencing with non-conflicting documents.** Design the stack as: (1) Institutional Clearance (school or juvenile justice certifies the teen is eligible to participate and releases the teen's enrollment to the program), (2) Parental/Guardian Consent for Program Participation (standard enrollment, covers mentorship activities, data sharing with the mentor), and (3) Case-Information Sharing Consent (if applicable; teen + guardian acknowledge that specific case details will be shared with the mentor for safety). Layer 1 is signed by the school or case manager; layers 2 and 3 are signed by the teen (if age 16+) and guardian. This structure prevents a single blank form from trying to cover incompatible institutional requirements.

* **For wards of the state, use Department of Human Services as consenting authority.** If a teen is in state custody (foster care, residential placement, etc.), the DHS caseworker or facility director holds legal authority to consent to the program. The consent form should have two signature blocks: one for "parent/guardian" (to be signed if the teen's parents retain parental rights while in state custody) and a separate block for "state agency representative" (if the state has custody). The program must confirm at enrollment which block applies and ensure the form is routed to the appropriate authority.

* **Mid-program withdrawal protocol with data deletion schedule.** Specify in advance: If a teen withdraws, what happens in 30 days? 90 days? Define a clear destruction schedule (e.g., all mentee records deleted 30 days after match termination; mentor notes deleted after 90 days unless a mandatory report was filed). For data already shared with a referring agency, the program's deletion cannot undo that; document that the program has destroyed its copies and notify the teen and guardian. If the withdrawal is requested by the juvenile justice system or school, follow that instruction immediately.

* **Consent architecture optimized for the teen's wellbeing, not institutional risk.** Instead of asking "What does the program need to protect itself?", ask "What does the teen actually need to trust this relationship?" A teens-first consent architecture would (1) minimize the consent burden on the teen (one simple signature on one document, not multiple forms), (2) explain clearly what information goes where and why (not legal jargon), (3) give the teen real agency to refuse specific disclosures (e.g., "I'm okay with my mentor knowing I'm in the school counseling program, but not with telling them about my mom"), and (4) allow for revocation—the teen can withdraw consent for case-information sharing at any time and the mentor continues, just with less contextual information. This approach builds trust from the start.

* **Foster-care complexity and multiple-placement histories.** Many teens in the target population move between placements. Consent authority may shift mid-match if a teen moves from parental custody into foster care, or from one foster home to another. The program should proactively check-in at enrollment and annually: "Is this still the person with legal authority to consent?" If authority changes, the program re-consents with the new guardian before the match continues. Do not assume a signed form from year one is still valid if custody changed.

## Data Minimization, FERPA / JJDPA / State Shield-Law Convergence, and Third-Party Processor Risk

* **FERPA-compliant data collection from schools.** FERPA (Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act) permits schools to share "directory information" (name, age, grade, participation in school activities) with third parties without written consent, but case-sensitive information requires either written consent or a legitimate educational interest exception. The mentorship program qualifies as an educational partner, but only if the school has a written agreement with the program. The program's intake form should request only directory information from the school (student name, age, grade, interests in academic support) and ask the school to refer the teen and parent directly to the program for enrollment rather than passing a full school record. This minimizes FERPA risk.

* **JJDPA and state juvenile-records statutes: prohibition on re-disclosure.** Most states classify juvenile case information as strictly confidential and prohibit disclosure to "unauthorized persons" (anyone not a party to the case, law enforcement, or court officer). The mentorship program, as a third-party volunteer organization, is not authorized to receive juvenile case details except through explicit consent and a formal data-sharing agreement with the juvenile justice system. The agreement should specify: (1) which data elements the program is authorized to receive (e.g., "age, referral reason" but not "case disposition" or "probation terms"), (2) that the program will not re-disclose this information except as required by law or to the teen's mentor under signed confidentiality agreement, and (3) that the program will destroy this information on a defined schedule. Violating this agreement can trigger both civil liability (tort) and potential criminal prosecution for unauthorized disclosure.

* **Third-party processor agreements for volunteer-management and case-management platforms.** If the program uses any online system (Slack, Airtable, Salesforce for CRM, Google Drive for file storage) to track mentees, store mentor notes, or manage match information, that vendor becomes a "processor" of minor data under FERPA, state data-breach notification laws, and potentially COPPA if the system is web-based and collects data directly from minors. For each vendor, the program must have a written Data Processing Agreement (DPA) that specifies: (1) the vendor will not use the data for its own purposes (targeted advertising, analytics, etc.), (2) the vendor will encrypt data at rest and in transit, (3) the vendor's security practices meet industry standards (SOC 2 compliance), (4) the vendor will notify the program within 24 hours of a suspected breach, (5) the program can audit the vendor's security, and (6) the vendor will delete or return all data on program request. Many vendors offer a standard DPA; if not, the program should negotiate one or find an alternative vendor.

* **Data retention and destruction protocol.** Define concrete retention schedules: mentee records retained for 3 years after match termination (to defend against liability claims); mentor evaluation records retained for 1 year; mentee case notes (if containing juvenile justice information) destroyed 60 days after match ends. For minors, shorter retention is better—assume data will be breached and delete what you don't need. Create an automated process (calendar reminder or logging system) to execute deletions on schedule; do not rely on manual review.

* **Consent design for regulatory future-proofing.** State privacy laws are tightening. California's CCPA, Virginia's VCDPA, and new state child privacy laws (like Illinois' BIPA for biometric data) are expanding. Rather than collecting blanket consent ("we may use your data for any lawful purpose"), collect specific consent ("the program will share case information with your assigned mentor and store it in [our system]; we will not sell or market your data"). This specificity makes it easier to comply with future regulations without renegotiating every consent form if a new law passes.

## Information Flow and Family Trust When Something Hard Surfaces

* **Mandatory reporting protocol communicated before it's triggered.** The mentor and mentee should both know before the relationship begins: "If I learn something that suggests you're being harmed, I am legally required to report this to child protective services or police. I will tell you that I'm making a report and what I'm reporting (with the exception of situations where telling you first would put you at risk)." This upfront clarity, while uncomfortable, is less damaging to trust than a surprise report that the teen experiences as betrayal. Many teens expect mandatory reporting; the shock comes from not knowing.

* **Immediate follow-up after mandatory reporting.** The moment a report is filed, the mentor and program should have a protocol for the next 24-48 hours: a brief, calm conversation with the teen explaining what was reported, why, what happens next, and reassuring the teen that the relationship continues and the mentor is still in their corner. This is not legal advice or CYA documentation; it's relational repair. The conversation might sound like: "I reported what you told me about [X]. That doesn't change my commitment to you. Here's what will happen next from the child protective side. I will be here for you through this." A teen who hears this is more likely to trust the mentor afterward than one who experiences a report as secret collusion.

* **Distinguish between mandatory reporting and discretionary disclosure.** Mandatory reporting (physical abuse, neglect, sexual abuse, exploitation) is required by law and must be reported. Discretionary disclosures (the teen is struggling in school, the family is facing eviction, the mentee is considering a risky decision) are up to the program's and mentor's judgment, and they can be handled through family conversations rather than state agencies. Many hard moments fall in the discretionary category, and handling these with the teen and family first, before escalating to authorities, builds trust and is often more effective.

* **Family-partnership framing from the start.** The program should actively position itself as a support to the family, not a substitute or shadow agency. This means: inviting guardians to program events, updating guardians regularly on how the relationship is going, asking the family what they hope the mentee will gain, and treating the mentor and guardian as collaborators in the teen's success. When trust exists between the program and family before something hard surfaces, the family is more likely to work with the program on the problem rather than blame the mentor for reporting it.

* **Cultural competence in mandatory reporting conversations.** Some communities experience state agencies (child protective services, police) as harmful, not protective. A mentor saying "I'm required to report this" to a teen whose family has experienced discrimination from child protective services triggers trauma, not reassurance. The program should provide mentors with culturally-informed guidance on how to frame mandatory reporting in ways that acknowledge this history while still being truthful.

## What If We Invented a New Kind of Transparency That Replaced Consent Forms

* **Continuous assent model: relational contracts instead of documents.** Instead of a consent form signed at enrollment, implement ongoing check-ins (e.g., quarterly, or semi-annually): "How is this mentorship working for you?", "Is there anything about how we're handling information that doesn't feel right?", "Do you want to change anything about what your mentor knows about you?" These conversations, documented briefly in a shared journal or conversation log, become a living record of the teen's agency and choice, far richer than a signed one-time form. The consent is continuously re-negotiated, not frozen at intake.

* **Mutual transparency agreements, not unilateral terms.** A relational transparency practice would be symmetrical: the teen knows what information the mentor has and why; the mentor knows what the teen has asked the program to keep private; the family knows what will be shared; and everyone understands what triggers mandatory reporting or information-sharing. This is co-created transparency, not a legal document imposed on the teen. The mechanism might be a one-page "understanding" co-signed by the mentor, mentee, and parent—not a 5-page liability-focused consent form.

* **Teen-visible data architecture.** If the program uses any data system (online or paper), the teen should be able to see, update, and annotate their own record. The teen might see a note from the mentor ("You mentioned you're interested in engineering") and can correct it ("Actually, I'm more interested in coding") or add context ("I also want to learn about environmental engineering"). This visibility makes data collection feel collaborative, not extractive. The teen becomes a curator of their own record.

* **Preference declaration instead of blanket consent.** Rather than asking "Do you consent to [long list]?", ask the teen to make active choices: "Who do you want to know about your participation? (check all that apply): your parents, your school counselor, your probation officer, no one else." For each relationship, the teen decides what information travels across it. This forces the program to think about information flow relationally and gives the teen genuine agency.

* **Community-based governance of transparency norms.** Instead of the program unilaterally deciding consent architecture, convene a group of current and former mentees, parents, mentors, and staff to co-design what transparency looks like. Ask: "What would make you trust that your information is being handled the way you'd want?" and "What would feel like a betrayal of privacy?" Build the consent and data architecture from those answers, not from liability templates.

## Consent as a Handshake Protocol

* **Four-node sequencing with mutual verification.** Design the handshake as a sequence of agreements that each verify the previous: (1) Institutional Node (school or juvenile justice): Certifies the teen's eligibility and that consent authority is identified. (2) Guardian Node: Confirms authority to consent and understands the program's commitment. (3) Teen Node: Assents to participation and understands what information will be shared. (4) Mentor Node: Acknowledges receipt of the teen's information and commits to confidentiality. Each node only moves forward once the prior nodes have confirmed consent. If any node withdraws or fails (e.g., the school informs the program the teen is no longer eligible), the protocol pauses and the program verifies that the other nodes are still in alignment before continuing.

* **Redundant verification checks to prevent silent corruption.** At three points—enrollment, 90 days in, and annually—the program verifies with each node: "Are you still on board with this mentorship?" This catches situations where, for example, a teen's case has been closed in juvenile justice but the program is still operating under old case-information-sharing agreements, or a guardian's consent is now invalid because custody changed. Annual re-verification is standard in regulated fields (medical consent, educational trust) and protects against silent drift.

* **Explicit withdrawal mechanics at each node.** Each node should have the ability to withdraw consent at any time, and the program should have a clear protocol for what that triggers: If the school withdraws (e.g., the teen is no longer a student), the match may continue if the guardian, teen, and mentor all agree. If the teen withdraws, the match ends. If the guardian withdraws, the match ends immediately. If the mentor or program withdraws, both the teen and guardian are notified within 48 hours. These rules should be stated upfront so no one is surprised.

* **Audit trail for each handshake stage.** Maintain a simple document (not a data dump) that tracks: date each node confirmed consent, what version of the consent agreement each node signed, when each node was last verified, and any changes to the terms (e.g., "mentor changed; new confidentiality agreement signed on [date]"). This audit trail makes it easy for the program to confirm consent is still valid and helps defend the program if a dispute arises later.

* **Failure modes and rollback procedures.** Explicitly define: If the juvenile justice system withdraws but the school and parent remain, what data must be deleted? (All case information.) If the mentor withdraws, what happens to the notes the mentor has taken? (The program retains one copy for program evaluation and teen safety; mentor's copies are destroyed.) If the guardian's authority is challenged (e.g., another parent claims custody), what does the program do? (Pause the match pending clarification of legal authority; do not continue the match with mixed or uncertain consent.) These edge cases should be decided in advance, not in a crisis.

## Trust Calibration Across Institutional Logics

* **Information-sharing map with explicit seams.** Create a data flow diagram showing: what information comes from the school, what comes from juvenile justice, what comes from the family, what goes to the mentor, what goes where if something needs to be reported. The diagram should explicitly label the seams (places where information crosses between institutions) and note what rule governs each seam: "School information to mentor: subject to FERPA (consent required, educational purpose only)"; "Juvenile justice information to mentor: subject to state JJDPA (consent required, case-specific agreement required)"; "Family information to school if needed: requires guardian authorization". This map is not a legal document; it's a communication tool to ensure everyone understands the pathways.

* **Data governance agreement with each institutional partner.** Formalize relationships with the school district and juvenile justice system with a written Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) or Data Sharing Agreement (DSA) that specifies: (1) What data elements each institution will provide (e.g., school: "referral reason, grade level"; juvenile justice: "referral reason, case worker contact"). (2) How the program will use that data (matching, program evaluation, mandatory reporting if applicable). (3) Who can see that data (the assigned mentor, program staff, no one else). (4) How long the program will retain it. (5) What happens if there's a breach. These agreements often must be approved by legal counsel and a school board or court system, so build this time into the startup phase.

* **Mentor training on institutional boundary-crossing.** Mentors need to understand which data belongs to which institution and which boundaries they cross by talking to the teen about school, family, or case information. A mentor who asks "How is probation going?" crosses from general mentoring into juvenile justice data; the program should provide a framework: "You can ask about the teen's future and goals; you can ask about their family and interests; you should avoid asking directly about their case or probation because that's confidential information the court system controls. If the teen volunteers this information, listen and support them, but don't probe." This training reduces accidental boundary violations.

* **"Firewall" practice for sensitive information.** If the program's leadership knows sensitive information (e.g., a teen's case history), designate which information flows to the mentor and which stays with program leadership only. The mentor might know "this teen was referred by juvenile justice because they needed support" but not the specific offense or case details. This firewall is not distrust; it's appropriate boundary-setting. The mentor can be an effective supporter without knowing the full case narrative.

* **Reconciliation protocol when institutional rules conflict.** Institutions sometimes have incompatible rules: the school wants to share information that juvenile justice says must stay confidential; the state has new privacy rules that the program's existing data-sharing agreement didn't anticipate. Establish a protocol: (1) Convene the partner institutions and the program. (2) Name the conflict explicitly. (3) Apply the most restrictive rule (if juvenile justice says "don't share," the program doesn't share that element even if the school is okay with it). (4) Document the resolution in writing. This proactive approach prevents the program from being caught between incompatible obligations.
