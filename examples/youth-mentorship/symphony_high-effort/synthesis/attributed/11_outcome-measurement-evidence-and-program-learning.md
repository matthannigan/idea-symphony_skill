# Brainstorming Synthesis: Outcome Measurement, Evidence, and Program Learning - With Attribution

## Synthesized Insights by Question

### Question 1: Measurement that honors depth without burdening or surveilling

**Full question** [User Q]: The program's real goal is meaningful relationship, but standard metrics (attendance, GPA, recidivism) are lagging proxies that can feel transactional and surveillance-like to teens already assessed and processed by schools and juvenile justice. What measurement approach honors the depth of the work, stays light enough not to burden mentors or teens, captures leading indicators detectable within weeks, preserves a teen's sense of being valued as a person rather than a data point, and still gives the foundation evidence worth funding?

* **Make structured mentor observation, not teen surveys, the primary instrument.** All seven perspectives converge here. The teen experiences only the relationship deepening while the program collects real signal: mentors log brief, specific observations monthly (a teen volunteering something personal, initiating contact, asking for help beyond logistics) in 3-5 minutes, never shared as a "score." This is feasible, mentor-controlled, and harder to game than a survey because it requires the mentor to stay present and notice. Reserve any direct teen contact for a few concentrated, conversational check-ins rather than recurring micro-surveys.
  * **Narrative observation system; mentors note moments the teen showed up differently.** Borrowed from theater critique and clinical session-rating scales. *—The Analogist*
  * **Relational check-ins that feel like conversation; monthly 5-minute reflection never shared with the teen.** Separates "what I did" from "what I noticed." *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Position the program as the anti-assessment space by refusing to survey teens directly; document one micro-moment per week.** Paradoxically more trustworthy because the teen never knew they were assessed. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Mentors document their own observations in a shared journal the teen can see; measurement becomes mutual visibility, not one-way assessment.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Mentor observation logs as the primary signal; trust-based philanthropy increasingly accepts structured, honest mentor observation as valid evidence.** *—The Futurist*
  * **Mentor observation log tracking one metric: times this week the mentor initiated or brought something intentional to discuss.** Declining initiation signals drift faster than any teen survey. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Conversational sampling and a self-directed "proof pile"; data comes from connection, not extraction.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Let the teen co-define the metric that gets tracked.** Six perspectives converge on inverting surveillance into agency: at intake, the teen picks one thing that would matter to them to see shift (trying something scary, sleeping better, talking to a counselor, staying out of court). The program collects a few light snapshots on that custom marker, reframing the frame from "the program is assessing you" to "we're tracking something you care about."
  * **Treat the teen as co-designer of the metric they want tracked; not every teen picks the same thing.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Opt-in teen voice via one open prompt ("What's felt different lately?"), in their own language.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Co-create a personal goal chosen by the teen; the goal itself becomes the metric, owned by both parties.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Invite teen and mentor quarterly to name one metric the teen cares about; inverts surveillance into agency.** *—The Futurist*
  * **Teen chooses self-directed progress markers ("days I felt heard," "times I tried something new").** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Different teens have different meaningful outcomes; let intake identify 1-2 domains per teen.** This is personalized measurement, not light measurement. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **A light-touch system without a foundation-facing anchor is a funding time bomb.** A single dissenting caution: anecdotes and journal notes alone will not satisfy a foundation at the 18-month mark, and that pressure will retroactively corrupt program design. Co-design measurement with the funder during the seed period and pair the relational data with at least one structured outcome.
  * **"Light measurement" is a time bomb; frontload the conversation and propose a narrative-plus-data hybrid with one structured outcome (e.g., year-end enrollment status).** *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 2: Instrument selection, weighting, and the pull toward easy proxies

**Full question**: Outcome metrics often default to proxies like meeting attendance or mentor self-reported satisfaction because genuine youth outcomes are hard to attribute and slow to materialize, and what gets measured shapes how mentors orient effort. What are the observable proxies for relationship quality over a one-year arc, how should each be weighted to avoid over-indexing on easy-to-collect but low-validity indicators, and what risk exists that mentors steer effort toward what is easy to count rather than what matters most?

* **Weight relational leading indicators heavily and easy proxies deliberately low.** All seven converge on designing the weighting backward from the program's theory of change: the relationship is the mechanism, so relationship-quality signals (teen initiates contact, shares something personal, mentor can name what the teen cares about beyond the presenting problem) carry the majority of the weight (roughly 50-70%), while attendance and mentor satisfaction are downweighted on purpose (often below 15%) precisely because they are cheap to collect and easy to game.
  * **Weight measures that predict long-term outcome (sense of being known, willingness to stick through difficulty); downweight gameable ones.** Borrowed from healthcare true-north vs. vanity metrics. *—The Analogist*
  * **Weight relational leading indicators 70%, behavioral proxies 30%; tell mentors the relationship is the intervention.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Two-tier system openly downweighting self-reported satisfaction to 15% because "it's too easy to game"; documented relational moments at 50%.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Relational before behavioral; weight relationship-quality signals above 50%, attendance below 15%.** *—The Futurist*
  * **50% relationship-quality signals, 30% teen-defined outcome, 20% organizational metric.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Hierarchy of evidence: Tier 1 teen-reported known-ness, down to Tier 4 attendance used only for fidelity.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **What gets measured will deterministically reshape mentor effort, so design the metric to redirect effort toward depth.** All seven engage the core risk that metrics shape behavior, even among well-meaning volunteers. The mitigations cluster: make weighting logic transparent so it can be adjusted midstream, measure a process metric ("conversations touching domains the teen identified") rather than a gameable outcome metric, and cap the metric list severely so the few metrics become operating principles rather than compliance noise.
  * **Invert the incentive by measuring what mentors resist measuring (a paragraph of something surprising the teen said); the act of articulating surprise forces presence.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Name explicitly what mentors should never optimize for ("you will not be evaluated on attendance or grades"); build a quarterly forum to flag metric creep.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Constrain to only three metrics per year and make proxy drift visible via a quarterly "what we measured vs. what we learned" chart.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **What gets measured deterministically shapes effort and even who gets served; publish weighting logic and run a "measurement equity audit" disaggregated by teen profile.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Design weighting to redirect effort, not track it; quarterly metric refresh based on early signal.** *—The Futurist*
  * **Keep a "metric distortion risk" log; ask monthly which metric a mentor could optimize at the teen's expense, and name the guardrail.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Map how each metric shapes behavior, then unweight metrics that incentivize the wrong effort; add a "mentor burden cost" variable and a hard three-metric cap.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Mentor self-reported satisfaction and meeting attendance are the most dangerous proxies precisely because they are easiest.** A pointed single-perspective caution backed by volunteer research: self-reported effort and impact are inflated by 40-60%, and attendance dominates by default because it is countable and funder-legible. The fix is to require triangulation (any metric unsupported by two other sources weighted to zero) and to replace raw attendance with "consistency of connection" weighted by teen responsiveness.
  * **Self-reported satisfaction inflated 40-60%; attendance is the worst proxy; weight any metric lacking two-source support to zero, and measure consistency of connection instead.** *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Tailor the metric intensity to the cohort rather than applying uniform measures.** A distinctive single-perspective reframe drawn from venture-capital power-law logic: not all matches will be transformative, so measure deeper relational outcomes for high-trust pairs and weight engagement and consistency for early-stage ones. Stop pretending uniform metrics work.
  * **Apply the VC power-law insight; measure and weight differently by cohort stage.** *—The Analogist*

### Question 3: Baseline data, comparison strategy, and evidence-registry alignment

**Full question**: Without a control group, attributing impact to outcomes like chronic absenteeism (currently 35%) is methodologically difficult. What baseline data should the program collect at intake, what comparison strategy (matched historical cohort, waitlist control, school-wide trend line) gives the most defensible signal, and should measurement design align with What Works Clearinghouse / SAMHSA evidence-registry trajectories that larger funders increasingly require?

* **Keep the intake baseline lean and pull objective data from existing systems instead of surveying the teen.** Strong convergence across six perspectives: collect only a handful of actionable baseline points (current attendance, justice-referral status as yes/no, one teen-stated goal, one mentor impression) and retrofit objective history from school and court records the teen has already generated. A heavy intake form creates false precision, burdens staff, and replicates the processing the teen has already endured.
  * **Retrofit school and court records for baseline rather than surveying; reduces being "surveyed into existence."** *—The Analogist*
  * **Collect only what you genuinely need; learn emotional baseline through the relationship; lighter intake is itself dignity data.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Collect only leading indicators at baseline (does the teen have a trusted adult? report loneliness?), not a retrospective GPA pull.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Embed baseline into the first month of conversation rather than an intake interview, to avoid re-traumatization.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Lean baseline: self-identified goal, attendance from school record, one mentor prompt; no 30-field form.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Four baseline points only; teen provides subjective baseline, systems provide objective baseline.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Use a matched cohort or naturally occurring waitlist as the comparison, and name the limitation openly.** Most perspectives accept that no rigorous control is feasible on a $15K budget and converge on quasi-experimental comparison, with two main routes. A matched historical cohort (same school, demographics, baseline absenteeism) is cheap and defensible if its assumptions are stated. A delayed-enrollment waitlist (demand exceeds 40 slots) is more causally defensible and ethically sound because the wait is finite, and it doubles as an equity intervention.
  * **Cohort plus interrupted time series against matched non-participants from the same zip code; name the bias openly.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Matched historical cohort preserves community integrity; supplement with qualitative comparison.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Each teen as their own within-person control; report variation in response honestly.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Matched historical cohort as primary comparison; increasingly accepted by mid-size funders who can't require RCTs.** *—The Futurist*
  * **Waitlist-control cohort compared on school attendance; high enough validity for a $15K report.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Waitlist control as both method and equity intervention; collect identical baseline for both arms.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Whether to design for evidence-registry standards now is a genuine, unresolved split.** This is the cluster's sharpest divergence. One camp argues for early alignment: map data collection to What Works Clearinghouse standards from the start (even hiring a consultant or contacting reviewers) because larger funders will require it within a few years and retrofitting is costly. The opposing camp argues registry alignment is a downstream consideration that, pursued first, makes programs measure what registries want while missing what matters locally, and may force distorting compromises like shrinking the program to enable randomization.
  * **Design for the WWC review upfront; hire a $5K research consultant in year one to map to their standards.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Contact WWC reviewers early and design around their minimal requirements rather than inventing your own framework.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Prepare for evidence-registry harmonization now; build in registry-defined outcomes (chronic absenteeism, engagement) you'll measure anyway.** *—The Futurist*
  * **Start at WWC Tier 2 (matched cohort/waitlist), plan toward Tier 3; send a one-page evaluation protocol to the funder 30 days post-launch.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Submit a narrative theory-of-change to registries as emerging practice; be transparent about limits rather than inflating claims.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **WWC standards are a constraint that may distort the program; choose a SAMHSA-style replication framework that fits a 30-teen faith-based context.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Evidence-registry alignment is downstream, not a constraint; get local measurement right first, then map it in year three.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Plan for impact to be invisible at twelve months and negotiate a multi-year horizon up front.** A distinctive timing caution: a year-one comparison showing "no significant effect" may trigger termination exactly when effects are beginning. Propose a phased funding model (year one trust-building, year two leading indicators, year three long-term outcomes) and secure a three-year commitment or structured re-evaluation.
  * **Comparison strategy wrongly assumes linear, one-year-visible outcomes; request a phased, multi-year funding horizon.** *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 4: Match dissolution feedback loop and selection bias in the public success story

**Full question**: Match terminations carry concentrated diagnostic information about program-design failures, and faith-based organizations often share program narratives publicly. What structured debrief protocol should the organization apply to dissolved matches so findings feed back into screening, training, and matching rather than being treated as isolated incidents, and what safeguards ensure that youth whose stories are shared have genuinely consented (not just their guardians) and that communications do not present a rosier picture than the data supports?

* **Run a no-blame structured debrief on every dissolution and feed the findings back into screening and training.** All seven converge: within one to two weeks of a match ending, conduct a structured conversation (mentor and teen separately, neutral facilitator) using a fixed question set, then synthesize de-identified patterns quarterly so the learning changes program logic rather than dying as anecdote. Failed matches carry the densest diagnostic signal in the whole program.
  * **NTSB no-blame accident-investigation model; a dissolution debrief database queried monthly; dissolutions spiking at six weeks indicts the matching process.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Treat dissolved matches as diagnostic goldmines; specific findings (teens resisting church venues, mentors unprepared for ADHD) feed screening, training, matching, and location.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Mandatory case conference within one week; close the loop all the way to screening, not just training.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Structured debrief within two weeks; quarterly synthesis surfaces patterns ("mentors without childcare support have 40% higher dissolution rates").** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Structured debrief feeding the matching algorithm and training; repeated patterns are urgent diagnostic information, not failure.** *—The Futurist*
  * **20-minute debrief producing a one-page "dissolution note" with probable cause, early warning signs, and one specific change; surface anonymized cases in next-cohort training.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Dissolution-by-design-phase metric: timing of the ending reveals which program system (screening, matching, support, transition) underperformed.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Require the teen's own consent, separate from the guardian's, for any public story, with refusal treated as unremarkable.** All seven converge: guardian consent at enrollment does not authorize public storytelling. Build a separate, revocable, age-appropriate teen consent specifying exactly what is shared and where, with anonymity as the default and the teen able to review or edit. A teen who declines to be a success story is exercising dignity, and that choice is respected categorically.
  * **"Consent micro-renewal": refresh teen consent three months before publication, asking "Is this how you want to be known?"** *—The Analogist*
  * **Consent at the point of sharing, not enrollment; the teen's choice to decline is unremarkable and unquestioned.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Tiered consent separating youth assent from guardian consent; authentic stories actually strengthen fundraising.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Separate participation consent from story-sharing consent; explicit opt-in from teen and guardian, anonymity as default, teen may write their own narrative.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Active, ongoing, granular, separately revocable consents for participation, internal learning, and public sharing.** *—The Futurist*
  * **Signed teen consent for any featured story; if under 15 or justice-referred, the teen may review and edit.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Annual consent process for youth voice (yes / no / yes-but-not-my-real-name), documented independently of parents.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Build structural safeguards against the reputational pull to bury negative data.** Strong convergence that faith-based, donor-facing contexts create real pressure to silence failures and over-tell triumphs, producing a false public narrative that then biases recruitment toward "promising" teens and "gifted" mentors. Mitigations cluster around mandated honesty: require dissolution rates and unmet needs in board and donor reporting, deliberately tell at least one non-completion story at the same depth as successes, and use an independent auditor to check reported framing against raw data.
  * **Use military After-Action Review format for all stories, including dissolutions; shifts the narrative from "we are great" to "we are learning."** *—The Analogist*
  * **Require that anyone presenting outcomes to the board includes dissolution rates and barriers; pressure to hide negative data is reportable.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Separate "lessons learned" data from public storytelling; choose shared stories by criteria unrelated to "success."** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **A confidential full-spectrum story archive reviewed annually lets leadership answer "how's it going?" with integrity.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Use outcome data to find the gap between public stories and actual composition; deliberately build a counter-narrative; have a third-party evaluator audit reported outcomes against raw data.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Anonymous quarterly "3 things we learned" one-pager to mentors and staff; keeps learning visible and out of the director's head alone.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Beware the shift-the-burden dynamic that blames the teen ("wasn't ready"); treat every dissolution as a design signal and say so publicly.** *—The Systems Thinker*

### Question 5: What if we measured outcomes by what mentors learned

**Full question**: Every outcome framework here focuses on teens. What if the program also measured what mentors learned and how they changed: whether the 40 congregation adults became more capable of sitting with discomfort, navigating unfamiliar institutional systems, and tolerating ambiguity? What does inverting the measurement direction reveal about who the current framework implicitly assumes is the developed person and who is the developing one?

* **Add a parallel mentor-learning measurement stream as a real outcome, not a footnote.** All seven converge: track mentor transformation explicitly through pre/post reflection on cultural humility, systemic awareness, discomfort tolerance, and institutional navigation, collected as short interviews or audio reflections rather than psychometric surveys. This reframes the program as building durable adult community capacity, which is harder to refute than fragile teen-outcome claims and surfaces that development is not one-directional.
  * **Apprenticeship and participant-observer framing; quarterly mentor reflection on what the teen taught them; pre/post scale on ambiguity tolerance and empathetic listening.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Reverse mirror capturing how mentors' understanding of the neighborhood expanded; aggregate as community asset development.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Mentor learning log inverting the measurement logic; the program is really an adult-education and perspective-shift intervention delivered through mentorship.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Asset-based mentor-growth stream evaluated at intake and 12 months using the same relational indicators used for teens.** *—The Futurist*
  * **Pre/post 5-point instrument on discomfort tolerance and systemic understanding at enrollment and month 12, against a waitlist control.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Same questions at intake and one year on systemic barriers and blind spots; if 30 of 40 mentors shift, that is evidence of community capacity to see structural injustice.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Frame mentor learning as community capacity and advocacy, not private growth.** A complementary thread: the deeper outcome is 40 adults becoming structurally literate, expanding cross-class and cross-racial networks, and in some cases becoming advocates who show up at school board meetings or challenge stereotypes in their congregation. Measure that diffusion explicitly as a primary community asset.
  * **Functional-redundancy framing; mentors carry expanded capacity back into congregations, workplaces, and families, redistributing social capacity.** *—The Analogist*
  * **The unintended curriculum: the program educates 40 adults about the neighborhood; survey whether their mental model and policy stance shifted.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Reframe mentor learning as capacity-building for advocacy; measure what percentage take action outside the program to address barriers they witnessed.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Frame mentor learning as the program's real output to funders: 40 adults trained as long-term advocates for systemic change.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Inverting the frame too far can quietly turn the teen into a teaching object, and funders may read it as mission drift.** A load-bearing dissent: a narrative of "church members learned compassion from the teens" is subtly paternalistic and repositions the teen as an instrument of adult development, and a funder who paid for teen outcomes may suspect avoidance of accountability. The resolution is to add, never replace: keep a robust teen-outcome stream, use mutual-growth language, and involve teens in designing what mentor learning should look like.
  * **"Measure mentor learning instead of teen outcomes" reads as avoiding accountability and risks instrumentalizing the teen; pursue a dual system and ensure the teen stream is equally robust and publicized.** *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 6: What if a hundred successful matches changed the neighborhood's social physics

**Full question**: Most mentorship programs measure outcomes teen by teen. Imagine instead that the real output emerges at the community level: a new social fabric, or a shift in how adults and young people relate across the neighborhood, not just within program pairs. What outcomes would you measure if the unit of transformation were the community rather than the individual, and how would you design the program differently if that were the goal?

* **Measure the relationship network itself, not just individual teens.** All seven converge on a community-level unit of analysis: map weak-tie and bridging-capital formation across age, class, and demographic lines at baseline and again at year two or three. The outcome is the density and connectedness of caring adult-youth ties, measurable without intrusive individual surveillance, and a leading indicator of durable neighborhood change.
  * **Network science of weak ties and structural holes; measure new adult-teen connections and cross-system communication.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Reverse-mirror at the congregational level: 40 adults shifting perception of the neighborhood may matter more than any GPA.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Constrain the unit from individual outcomes to network density; a simple "who do you trust?" survey shows the trust network growing denser and less siloed.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Map the network geometry as the output; bridging capital across community divides is measurable without surveillance.** *—The Futurist*
  * **Pre/post network survey of cross-age, cross-background weak-tie formation.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Measure whether mentoring becomes a neighborhood norm: informal mentoring forming outside the program, mentors on school committees, youth mentoring youth.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Design the program to be visible and porous so it generates its own momentum, and track institutional ripple effects.** Complementary insights: community change requires the program to operate in public (community learning nights, shared spaces, a teen advisory board) and to track whether partner institutions change their practices because relationships formed (schools responding to youth relationally rather than punitively, caseworkers advocating for relationship-focused reentry).
  * **Third places and mutuality-vs-extraction; measure whether teens shape how mentors and the organization see the world.** *—The Analogist*
  * **"Social bridge" construction: how many isolated teens gained an institutional relationship; the outcome is the bridge, not what happens on it.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Measure institutional responsiveness shift via interviews with school and justice partners.** *—The Futurist*
  * **Institutional relationship deepening and a mentor ambassador effect; count system staff who proactively refer by year two.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Design the program to be visible and generate momentum; the dyad becomes a network as pairs overlap, and network density is a leading indicator of community change.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Community-level claims are nearly impossible to attribute, can be unaffordable, and may instrumentalize the teen, so treat the shift as emergent, not engineered.** A load-bearing dissent: neighborhood change has an unknowable counterfactual (a new community center, the economy, a new principal), rigorous ethnographic measurement can cost 2.5-5x the program budget, and optimizing for "social physics" can turn teens into props in a community-development story. Keep the primary goal at the individual level, define any community outcome concretely if pursued, reframe as cultural diffusion rather than direct effect, and partner with a university to absorb the research cost.
  * **Don't claim causality for neighborhood change; reframe as a seed intervention and measure local diffusion; define "transformed neighborhood" concretely or it becomes magical thinking; keep the primary goal individual so teens aren't used as props.** *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 7: The measure that matters in year three

**Full question**: Fast-forward two years past formal match closure. Funders want outcome data, but the most meaningful change in a mentee's life may not show up in attendance or GPA for years. Whose voice carries that story, how was the data gathered in a way that did not reduce the teen to a metric during the program, and what would count as evidence that the relationship was worth building?

* **Center the teen's own longitudinal narrative, collected respectfully across time, as the year-three evidence.** All seven converge: the truest evidence is the young adult's own retrospective account of what shifted and why, gathered through low-pressure interviews or self-authored reflections rather than surveys they never took at intake. Pair a match-end narrative with a year-three narrative in the teen's own words, and let teens author or co-author the story rather than having it interpreted for them.
  * **Narrative-medicine and post-test-only retrospective attribution; a dozen genuine narratives teach a funder more than an attendance table; let youth join the evaluation team.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Longitudinal touch points framed as staying connected, not extracting data; the carrying voice should be the teen's own.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Delegate the year-three narrative to the teen's own voice (voice memo, letter, video); build a narrative archive rather than a metrics dashboard.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Deferred storytelling: gather rich data during the program, curate the narrative only in year three; teen as author, not subject.** *—The Futurist*
  * **Collect the teen's own narrative at match end and again at year three; pair the two voices as the longitudinal story.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **The evidence that counts is the evidence the teen kept; the diversity of teens' own interpretations is itself the evidence the program worked.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Treat persistence of the relationship and continued contact as a core outcome.** A complementary thread several perspectives raise: whether the match survived informally past the formal endpoint, and whether mentor and teen still show up for each other, is meaningful presence-based evidence that standard metrics miss.
  * **Longitudinal sociological case studies; track high-school completion, post-secondary, and shifts in self-perception years out.** *—The Analogist*
  * **The best year-three evidence may be a mentor still in relationship or a teen who now mentors a younger teen.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Measure persistence of the relationship as the outcome; what percentage of formal matches became ongoing informal ones.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Measure the unmeasurable through presence and continuity; relationship resilience as program sustainability.** *—The Futurist*
  * **A low-cost oral-history project (journalist or grad student, $2-3K) at year two captures what surveys miss and doubles as a fundraising artifact.** *—The Pragmatist*

* **Build a coherent measurement chain and a deliberate non-completion story up front, or year-three data will contradict itself and exclude the hardest cases.** Two pointed cautions: year-one, year-two, and year-three measures may turn out to track different things (perfect attenders without trust, trusting teens without academic gains), so map all measures into a single causal chain before launch; and the year-three story will systematically omit dissolved matches and aged-out teens unless one full non-completion story is told at equal depth.
  * **Build a measurement-strategy map connecting all measures in a causal chain before year one; negotiate a portfolio of one hard, one mid-range, and one narrative outcome with the funder; deliberately tell one full non-completion story.** *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 8: Outcomes as lagging indicators and the need for leading proxies

**Full question**: School attendance, reduced recidivism, and academic advancement are lagging indicators that take one to three years to materialize. What leading indicators, detectable within weeks or months of a match, can predict whether a relationship is on track to produce those long-term outcomes, and how should the measurement system be designed to act on leading signals rather than waiting for lagging outcomes?

* **Track relational momentum (especially teen-initiated contact and mentor initiation) as the predictive leading indicators.** All seven converge on a small set of early signals detectable within weeks: frequency of mentor-initiated contact between meetings, whether the teen initiates contact unprompted, a short teen "felt-known" score, the conversation shifting from logistics to meaning, and counselor-observed behavioral change. When these trend up by month three, lagging outcomes are likely to follow even before GPA moves.
  * **Phase-change and microstructural indicators; a monthly two-minute mentor survey on initiation, trust trend, and advice-seeking predicts month-nine outcomes.** *—The Analogist*
  * **The relational-asymmetry flip: the teen begins asking questions and initiating; observable within weeks.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Constrain the measurement window to weeks 4, 8, and 12, each asking "Is this relationship worth continuing?"** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Trust frequency and quality, and engagement in new contexts, as leading signals with documented predictive power; act before dissolution.** *—The Futurist*
  * **Weekly mentor initiative (3+ weeks per month) and frequency of unprompted teen communication as primary leading indicators; detectable by week three.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **A monthly leading-indicator dashboard of mentor initiation, teen felt-known-ness, and mentor confidence; if all three trend up, the match is on track even if GPA hasn't shifted.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Separate genuine relationship signals from environmental noise and from low-quality "busyness."** Complementary refinements: high leading indicators with declining attendance may mean the relationship is intact but the teen's context destabilized, which is diagnostic rather than failure; and frequency metrics must be weighted by teen responsiveness so mentors are not rewarded for performative contact.
  * **Treatment-fidelity checks and syndromic surveillance catch fidelity drift and at-risk matches early.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Mentor confidence and "feels like genuine friendship, not charity" as proxies; declining scores are a six-week leading indicator of dissolution.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Distinguish "relationship is weak" from "context is getting in the way"; build a risk-stratification system (on track / building / at risk) at month three.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Leading indicators are unvalidated surrogates that can mislead, so resist over-reaction and consider a protected early window before intervening.** A load-bearing dissent that runs against the act-early consensus: most proposed leading indicators have not been shown to predict the lagging outcome (Campbell's Law), self-reported and counselor-observed signals carry demand and confirmation bias, and intervening on a weak month-three signal can disrupt a pair that would have bonded by month six. The proposed safeguards are a validation study, blind counselor observation, behavioral triangulation of self-reported trust, and a "wait-and-observe window" through roughly month six before acting.
  * **Leading indicators are unvalidated surrogates (Campbell's Law); commit to a validation study, weight contact by teen responsiveness, triangulate self-reported trust with behavior, use blind counselor observation, and hold a six-month wait-and-observe window before intervening.** *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 9: Cross-system data as triangulation

**Full question**: A teen's progress is visible differently from three vantage points: the mentor sees relational and behavioral change, the school counselor sees attendance and academics, the juvenile justice caseworker sees compliance and incidents. How can the program design data-sharing agreements that allow triangulation across these vantage points without violating each institution's privacy constraints, and who owns the synthesized view of the youth's progress?

* **Triangulate three vantage points through discrete, tiered consent without building a central database the program owns.** All seven converge: pull objective data from systems that already hold it (school attendance and discipline, justice compliance) so the teen is not re-surveyed, govern it with granular per-stream consent rather than blanket permission, and keep each system's data under its own control rather than consolidating it into one program-owned hub.
  * **Trilateral positioning via a data-sharing MOU specifying exact elements, conditions, and format; differential privacy and a FHIR-style schema enable aggregate pattern analysis.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Multiple observers means less intrusive measurement for the teen; build a consent hierarchy by data sensitivity.** *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Discrete per-stream consent and a "tri-vantage map" used for internal understanding only, never aggregated into one owned database.** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Consent tiering established from the start, separate and revocable; prevents building surveillance infrastructure that contradicts the relational purpose.** *—The Futurist*
  * **One-page protocol with school, justice, and a health provider specifying who can request what; the program is not the hub.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Start with a single-teen pilot to surface every privacy barrier, then template the agreement; begin with a pro bono privacy lawyer on what can legally flow.** *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Make the synthesized view a gift owned by the teen, governed collaboratively, not a tool any single system controls.** Strong convergence on the ownership question: the triangulated picture should be handed to the teen ("here's what the adults who care about you notice"), reframing data from surveillance into something the teen carries forward, and governance should be distributed (a stewardship board or progress team) so no one institution monopolizes interpretation.
  * **A community data stewardship board governs cross-system sharing; a "data Rosetta Stone" translates terminology across systems.** *—The Analogist*
  * **The synthesized view belongs to the teen, not to systems; deliver it as a synthesis document at 6 and 12 months.** *—Audience Advocate*
