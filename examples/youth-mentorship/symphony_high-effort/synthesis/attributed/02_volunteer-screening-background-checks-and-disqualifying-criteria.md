## Synthesized Insights by Question

### Question 8: Minimum disqualifying criteria and the borderline case

**Full question** [User Q]: Background check results sit on a spectrum from absolute bars (crimes against minors) to judgment calls (old non-violent offenses, expunged records). What should the minimum disqualifying criteria be, what decision framework classifies each finding, who in the organization holds adjudicating authority for borderline cases, and how do we validate criteria from evidence rather than inheriting risk-aversion logic?

* **Adopt a three-tier classification that separates automatic bars from judgment calls.** All but one of the seven personas converge on the same structure: a small set of non-negotiable disqualifiers decided by clear rule, a middle band of findings escalated to a review committee, and a low-risk band approved under standard oversight. Crimes against minors, sexual offenses, and recent violent felonies (commonly a 5-to-10-year lookback) sit in the absolute-bar tier. Old non-violent offenses, financial crimes, substance-related convictions, and expunged records fall into the judgment-call tier where context, recency, and demonstrated change matter. The value of the tiered model is that it converts intuition into an escalation path: the screener never silently decides a borderline case alone.
  * **Aviation hazard-classification matrix (catastrophic/critical/major).** Crimes against minors are catastrophic (permanent bar); non-violent or substance offenses are critical (allowable only with enhanced training, supervision, or role restriction); minor or expunged matters are major (standard oversight suffices). *—The Analogist*
  * **Three-tier system with delegated authority and a committee charter.** Absolute bars (child abuse, sexual assault, felony violence within 10 years); red flags (arrests without conviction, non-violent felonies over 5 years, misdemeanor assault) escalate to committee; green flags (traffic, expungements over 7 years) approve unless a pattern emerges. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Risk stratification with graduated authority.** Crimes against minors and recent violent felonies are automatic (single authority holds veto); DUIs, property crimes, and expunged offenses go to a three-person peer-review committee; everything else requires a conversation plus supervisor sign-off. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Absolute bars are easier to defend than judgment calls.** Crimes against minors, sexual offenses, and violence within 10 years should be automatic; substance and financial crimes need context-specific thresholds (exclude within 5 years; older convictions require added vetting). *—The Lawyer*
  * **"Crimes against minors" is the one clear hard line, but absence of conviction is not safety.** Any conviction involving a minor should disqualify; the tiered approach is sound but cannot be the whole defense. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Move adjudication of borderline cases to an independent or rotating panel, not a single gatekeeper.** Six of the seven personas (and the seventh on adjudication composition) call for distributing the decision across multiple people, with at least one member structurally outside the applicant's social world. The recurring rationale: a single screener absorbs both the emotional burden and the social pressure of the decision, and a purely internal committee in a faith community is the configuration most likely to rationalize a known member through. The panel typically includes a records/background expert, a safeguarding or risk specialist, and an external or community voice.
  * **Clinical-ethics multidisciplinary review panel.** A three-person panel pairs a background-check expert, a safeguarding specialist, and a community voice, distributing authority and surfacing any single reviewer's pattern-blindness. *—The Analogist*
  * **One external member, term-limited to prevent capture.** Staff-plus-pastor-plus-congregant committees are exactly the mix vulnerable to "I've known him 20 years" logic; add an external member (social worker, police liaison) with no prior relationship and a 12-month term. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Rotating committee distributes the emotional weight.** A single gatekeeper will burn out or grow defensive; rotation forces each borderline case to be re-argued without years of personal relationship calcifying the judgment. *—The Empath*
  * **Three-person committee (ED, board member, external consultant), records retained 5 years.** Distributed authority insulates the organization from claims that one staffer negligently approved a harmful volunteer. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Rotate adjudicators annually and document decisions internally.** A fresh reviewer asks different questions; publishing the reasoning ("approved despite a 2010 DUI because no pattern exists") lets staff spot drift. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Adjudication should include a voice from the community being served.** A teen-serving advocate or peer mentor on the panel catches when criteria are applied with hidden double standards against people with prior poverty, immigration status, or their own juvenile-justice history. *—The Audience Advocate*

* **Validate criteria against your own outcomes rather than inheriting another organization's risk tolerance.** Four personas independently insist that disqualifying criteria be written down, grounded in regional or program-specific evidence, and tested on a schedule. The shared worry is that nonprofits copy a template, never measure whether it predicts actual harm, and end up simultaneously over-protective in some areas and under-protective in others. The remedy is a documented feedback loop: record every screening decision and its reasoning, record every concern or incident during mentorship, and review on a cadence whether the criteria caught or missed the right people.
  * **Reverse-engineer criteria from actual mentorship failure modes.** Map boundary drift, grooming tells, and capacity collapse first, then ask what history predicts each; track every borderline decision for 18 months and feed outcomes back yearly. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Quarterly test against real outcomes.** Document each decision and reasoning, each reported concern, then ask quarterly "did our process catch this person or miss this person?" to surface blind spots. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Pre-launch evidence audit of regional programs.** Spend four weeks reviewing 10-15 in-state programs' actual disqualifying lists, benchmark against your risk tolerance, and document why you accept or reject each criterion. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Regulatory-sandbox and clinical-trial registration.** Pilot-test a borderline criterion against historical cases before locking it in, and publish criteria before interviews begin so referrers and applicants know the standard in advance. *—The Analogist*

* **The hardest failure is social-loyalty drift, and the emotional cost of rejecting "good people" is the real adversary.** Distinct from the structural fixes above, two personas name the felt dynamic directly: committee members invested in known volunteers will experience rejecting them as a personal cost heavier than the abstract risk to a stranger's child, and the first borderline approval quietly licenses the next. Making criteria public and defensible is partly an emotional-narrative defense, because the story ("they let someone through with a sketchy past") travels faster than the technical decision.
  * **Loyalty will quietly shift judgment toward known volunteers.** The legal framework is rarely the problem; the emotional weight of declining a congregation member is. *—The Empath*
  * **The first approval sets precedent and circulates as a story.** Each decision authors an implicit account of what the organization values; keep criteria visible because the narrative outruns the rationale. *—The Empath*

* **Honor expungement as a legal reset while still evaluating the underlying behavior pattern.** A targeted point raised by two personas: expungement must be respected as the law requires, but the adjudication question is whether the behavior behind a record (dishonesty, anger, boundary issues) bears on the role, not the legal label. A 15-year-old resolved offense and a three-year-old one tell different stories about the same charge.
  * **Expungement as reset point, not erasure.** Make the committee state the behavior pattern it is evaluating, not just the legal category; recency and demonstrated change distinguish the same charge. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Codify your expungement position with counsel.** A written "we honor expungement orders" policy protects you both from hiding disqualifying conduct and from discriminating against a legally erased record; resolve ambiguity with a local attorney. *—The Lawyer*

* **Require disclosure of arrests and charges, with non-disclosure as its own disqualifier.** A single-persona safeguard that closes the expungement gap from a different angle: ask applicants to self-report all arrests and charges (with clear "arrest is not guilt" language), because a sealed record can belong to a first-time offender whose first offense involved a minor. Undisclosed arrests later surfaced become an automatic bar.
  * **Self-report form plus merits-based committee discussion.** Expungement does not erase behavior; disclosed arrests are weighed on context and resolution, and concealment is itself the red flag. *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 9: Background check statutory floor, FCRA pre-adverse-action, and the screening workflow

**Full question**: Several states impose a mandatory screening floor for youth-serving organizations (e.g., fingerprint-based FBI checks, Pennsylvania's Act 153 requirements, California's Child Abuse and Neglect Reporting Act ecosystem) and FCRA (15 U.S.C. § 1681) requires pre-adverse-action notice, waiting period, and final adverse-action notice with dispute rights when a third-party consumer reporting agency is used. Does the policy match or exceed the state floor, and does the screening workflow build in the FCRA steps without which the organization carries direct liability?

* **Identify your state's specific statutory floor before designing anything, then exceed it.** All seven personas converge here, and several stress that the floor must be confirmed by direct contact with the state authority rather than inferred from a similar state. The named examples recur across responses: Pennsylvania's Act 153 (three-agency clearance: state police, FBI fingerprinting, child-abuse index) and California's Live Scan / DOJ / Trustline ecosystem. Where no explicit state mandate exists, a national best-practice floor is criminal (FBI plus state), sex-offender registry (national plus states lived in), and child-abuse/neglect registry checks across every state of residence in the past five years.
  * **Call the state attorney general's nonprofit division; document mandatory vs. recommended.** If the program is in Pennsylvania, skipping the three-agency check exposes the organization and staff to criminal charges, not just civil liability; exceed the floor by one tier. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Identify the floor first, then exceed it; compliance cost is negligible vs. a lawsuit.** National best-practice floor: FBI plus state criminal, sex-offender registry, and child-abuse registry in each state of residence over the past five years. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Call the state child-serving program office; build a one-page checklist.** A 30-minute call prevents running screening that is "compliant but insufficient." *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Map the floor by calling the licensing body, don't guess.** State definitions of youth-serving organizations shift, so confirm rather than assume. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Build a compliance audit table (rule per row, your implementation per column).** Layer state floor, FCRA requirements, and funder mandates so gaps become visible and documentable. *—The Analogist*

* **Build the FCRA pre-adverse-action sequence into the workflow as muscle memory, because skipping a step is direct organizational liability.** Six personas lay out the same procedural chain and warn that it is routinely violated: written disclosure and separate authorization up front; if a report may drive rejection, a pre-adverse-action notice naming the reporting agency with a 3-to-5-business-day dispute window; the waiting period; then a final adverse-action notice with reasons and dispute rights. Statutory damages run roughly $100-$1,000 per violation plus attorney fees, and a careless process also invites arbitrariness or discrimination claims.
  * **FDA adverse-event pipeline mapped to screening.** Intake, assessment, pre-adverse notice with the agency's information and a 5-day window, final notice with reasons and appeal, and 7-year retention prevents ad-hoc "we forgot to tell them" failures. *—The Analogist*
  * **FCRA is procedurally rigid and violated unknowingly.** The report is input to your decision, not the vendor's; route decisions through counsel for the first six months until the workflow is automatic. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Nine-step workflow with dates and decision-maker initials.** Application, interview, FCRA disclosure and authorization, checks, committee review, pre-adverse notice, waiting period, final decision, final notice. *—The Lawyer*
  * **One-page workflow locked into the volunteer manual.** Eight numbered steps plus a posted "4-6 weeks" timeline reduces pressure to speed up and cuts informal approval shortcuts. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Same-day pre-adverse notice via documented delivery.** Legal review within 24 hours, notice sent that day with read receipt, calendar reminder for the waiting period, final notice only after it expires. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **The organization, not the vendor, is the FCRA "employer" and carries the liability.** Four personas isolate this trap: hiring a background-check company does not transfer responsibility, because the organization controls the adverse-action decision and the vendor is only the messenger. The mitigation is to choose a vendor that automates FCRA steps, get the liability allocation in writing, and avoid using an in-house volunteer to run checks at all.
  * **Third-party reporting liability trap.** Organizations discover the violation only when a rejected applicant sues; a written protocol and paper trail are the defense. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Confirm vendor FCRA handling in writing; specify who bears liability.** Use a specialist nonprofit vendor (Checkr, Sterling, First Advantage) and never let an in-house volunteer run checks. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Hire a nonprofit-specialist company that validates FCRA compliance in writing.** Or route every decision through counsel until the workflow is muscle memory. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Vendor handles the steps, but you remain liable to the candidate.** FCRA § 1681 disclosure, authorization, pre-adverse and final notices are your obligation regardless of the contract. *—The Lawyer*

* **Treat screening as recurring, not a one-time gate.** Three personas note that a clean check in year one says nothing about year two, when a new DUI, fraud, or custody loss could change the risk picture. Re-screen active mentors every 12-24 months, and re-check whenever a mentor's role changes (for example, moving into group events).
  * **Re-screen every 24 months and at any role change.** A volunteer with no history at intake can acquire one that raises judgment or stability questions. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Annual re-check plus a confidential "anything changed?" conversation.** Run by an independent staffer, documented even when the answer is "all good," signaling that screening is ongoing. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Quarterly statutory-floor audit and annual legal review.** State requirements and registry definitions change and cascade through the whole workflow. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Apply the same screening to staff that you apply to volunteers, and let families see what you actually checked.** Two community-facing points that reframe compliance as a relationship rather than paperwork: program staff often receive lighter screening than mentors, which sends the message that staff are presumed trustworthy and volunteers suspect; and matched families deserve a plain-language statement of what "we ran a background check" really covered.
  * **Everyone undergoes the same process and the same criteria.** Holding staff to the standard demanded of others removes the implicit double standard. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Families have a right to know exactly what was verified.** "Background check" spans a name search to an FBI fingerprint check; a deliberate, documented process signals that safety outweighs speed. *—The Audience Advocate*

* **The full FCRA process is also emotional and reputational cover, not only legal protection.** A reframe from two personas: the formal steps tell a rejected volunteer "we took you seriously enough to give you a real process," and a gap that looks lawful but feels arbitrary breeds quiet resentment that damages the whole pool's trust in fairness.
  * **Compliance as confidence, not burden.** Procedural safety is relational safety; cutting corners creates suspicion and shame. *—The Empath*
  * **The workflow itself signals seriousness.** Moving fast from application to match because the pool is small tells families speed matters more than rigor. *—The Audience Advocate*

### Question 10: Documented-history misses, congregation familiarity, and counter-balancing safeguards

**Full question**: Documented criminal history catches only those who have been caught, and many people who harm minors have no prior record; meanwhile, social loyalty in a faith community where staff and volunteers worship together can quietly bend screening decisions toward leniency for known members. What structural safeguards (independent or rotating adjudication committees, blinded review, ongoing peer-observation channels) compensate for both the evidentiary limits of background checks and the social-loyalty drift that congregation familiarity creates?

* **Build ongoing peer observation into the program; it is the primary defense, because the background check is blind to behavior after approval.** All seven personas converge on this as the single most important safeguard. Screening happens once and catches only documented history, but grooming and boundary drift unfold over months of one-on-one isolation. The shared design: lightweight, normalized, and visible rather than secret. Recurring mechanisms include quarterly co-observation outings where a second mentor accompanies a pair, separate periodic check-ins where a supervisor speaks with the youth alone about whether boundaries feel safe, and a monthly mentor cohort where peers surface concerns they would never file as formal complaints.
  * **Immune-system redundancy: no single check is the whole defense.** Layer the background check, structured interview, reference checks, a community feedback window, and ongoing peer observation so one failure does not compromise the whole. *—The Analogist*
  * **Periodic check-ins with matched youth and an anonymous peer channel.** Teens need a path to raise concerns that does not require "reporting on an elder" to people who worship with that elder. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Rotating co-observation visits with structured reflection forms.** "That's just how Mike is" normalizes boundary-pushing until crisis; outside eyes catch excessive gift-giving and youth discomfort early. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Gentle attentiveness as a culture, not a checklist.** The hardest part is the emotional safety that lets people voice concern without blame. *—The Empath*
  * **Confidential channel to an external ombudsperson.** Lets colleagues flag "he's a bit too close with his mentee" without the social friction of a face-to-face confrontation. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Quarterly spot-check observations and peer flags during training.** Staff sit in on a meeting with consent and document observations; cheaper than litigation. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Every-three-months supervisor-and-youth conversation with the mentor absent.** A self-correcting mechanism: mentors who know they are observed reinforce boundary discipline. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Make adjudication structurally independent of the congregation and rotate it, because social-loyalty leniency is a systemic pattern, not a character flaw.** All seven personas treat congregation familiarity as a predictable bias to be engineered against rather than trusted away. The convergent design: at least one adjudicator with no social tie to the applicant (ideally external, such as a school counselor or partner-nonprofit director), a rule that the home-congregation relationship never adjudicates its own member, yearly rotation to prevent threshold softening, and the same independence applied to a mentor's own supervisor in approving that mentor's match.
  * **"Shifting the Burden" pattern: reliance on reputation works until it doesn't.** Build structural independence: external screener, peer-review committee with an external member, and a supervisor barred from approving their own volunteer's match. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Committee training names the relationship as a risk.** "You know this person. That is valuable context. It is also a risk." No member from the candidate's home congregation; mandatory rotation. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Rotation forces re-argument without calcified judgment.** The discomfort of fresh examination is what prevents silent collusion. *—The Empath*
  * **Make independent adjudication visible to applicants and families.** The community seeing that the approver is not the applicant's Bible-study partner is itself a signal that drift was anticipated. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Outsider-insider pairing on every borderline case.** The insider knows values and context; the outsider spots what locals normalize ("everyone knows Jane is trustworthy" is not evidence). *—The Analogist*

* **Use blind review to test whether a decision is driven by evidence or by relationship.** Three personas propose stripping names and congregation status from borderline files and presenting only documented findings, then asking whether the committee would approve a stranger from an online application. A changed answer is the bias, made visible and documentable.
  * **Blinded batch review surfaces the committee's own social-loyalty bias.** Redact affiliation, present only findings, ask the stranger question, and document the moment the answer changes. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Blind review plus a coded community-feedback channel.** Discuss each application without congregation status until a provisional decision; present concerns as "community feedback [code number]," not named accusations. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Blind review by a non-congregant reviewer for internal volunteers.** At least one reviewer enters every decision without prior familiarity; document who owned it and who provided fresh eyes. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Ask the blunt disclosure questions and call references who carry institutional liability, because abuse stays hidden longer in loyal communities.** Four personas converge that documented history catches only prosecuted cases, and that in high-loyalty settings prior harm is often handled internally or quietly forgotten by the time someone applies. The countermeasures are direct interview questions, structured reference scripts that probe conflict and boundaries rather than inviting praise, and weighting references from prior youth-serving organizations whose own liability makes them more candid.
  * **Ask directly whether they were ever asked to step back from youth work; refusal is a data point.** Call all three references and ask about handling conflict, not "is he trustworthy?"; weight youth-serving referees over social friends. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Structured reference checklist surfaces inconsistencies.** "What would you want to know if placing your own child with him?" catches the gap between "great with kids" and an application showing no childcare experience. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Behavioral-anchored questions compensate for the halo effect.** Score the same conflict question across all candidates on one rubric, because congregation references glow regardless of capacity. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Discovery will expose unasked questions.** If a youth is the first to disclose a prior incident, the lawsuit reveals the program never asked the right questions. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Triangulate risk through structured interviews and references that read for relational patterns the records cannot show.** Distinct from the disclosure framing, two personas treat the interview and reference process as an epidemiology-style correction for the records' ascertainment bias: ask how a candidate handled frustration with a young person, and ask references specifically about boundaries, anger, and reliability.
  * **Ascertainment-bias correction via relational-pattern questions.** "Tell us about a time you felt frustrated with a young person and how you handled it" reveals judgment under stress no check captures. *—The Analogist*
  * **Independent data-safety-monitoring model.** Recruit the panel from outside the congregation and have an external auditor spot-check 10% of decisions for systematic leniency toward familiar faces. *—The Analogist*

* **Watch the inverse of the loyalty bias: the unknown outsider held to an impossibly high bar.** A two-persona fairness point that the other safeguards do not address: while known members get grace, a newer volunteer with no congregation ties can face disproportionate scrutiny, and that inequity is felt immediately and poisons recruitment.
  * **The fairness gap cuts both ways.** Known volunteer gets grace, outsider gets scrutiny; the disparity will be felt and will harm recruitment. *—The Empath*
  * **Independent decision-making protects the wrongly excluded too.** A panel including lived-experience voices catches double standards applied against prior poverty, immigration status, or a candidate's own juvenile-justice history. *—The Audience Advocate*

### Question 11: Recruiting for relational capacity, not just willingness, and the 40-volunteer ceiling versus deep-design canvas

**Full question**: With ~40 interested adults sourced primarily from a single congregation, the program is volunteer-constrained from day one, yet a small, deeply known pool also enables every match to be deliberately designed rather than logistically assigned. What screening instruments or structured interview components most reliably differentiate applicants with genuine relational capacity from those motivated by service hours, religious obligation, or savior energy, and would the program rather have 10 deeply committed mentors than 40 moderately interested ones?

* **Choose roughly 10-15 deeply committed mentors over 40 moderately interested ones; the 40 are a constraint, not a resource.** All seven personas land emphatically on quality over scale. The convergent reasoning runs through both safety and outcomes: a small cohort lets staff know each mentor's style, enables deliberate matching, supports real training and high-touch supervision, and makes a struggling mentor visible before harm occurs, whereas 40 loosely vetted volunteers means a stranger pool, isolated mentors, and supervision that scales poorly. Several frame this as protecting at-risk teens from yet another unreliable adult. The recommended levers: a hard cap, a waitlist of pre-vetted overflow candidates, and a recruitment pitch reframed around commitment.
  * **10 deeply selected beats 40 loosely vetted; cap year one at 15.** Demanding screening that lets 60-70% self-select out is healthy; offer "core" and "associate" tiers so commitment levels do not mix. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Quality-over-quantity creates a resource cascade.** Ten deep mentors free time for matching, 8-week training, and monthly supervision; success generates retention and word-of-mouth that attracts more committed people. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A small pool of deeply vetted mentors is vastly preferable from the teen's view.** Pairing teens to whoever is available sets them up for another relational failure; ask the community directly whether they want quantity or sustainable patience. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Trust the hesitation; mediocre matches cost more than you predict.** Better to tell 30 people "not right for you now" and keep ten who are genuinely present. *—The Empath*
  * **Smaller cohort outperforms and is more enforceable.** If 1 of 10 fails it is visible and actionable; expand to 20-30 later as systems mature. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Launch with 15-20 intensively screened, deliberately matched pairs.** Reframe the pitch from "we need 40 volunteers" to "we're recruiting 15-20 ready for a one-year weekly commitment." *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Guild apprenticeship: invest deeply in a few.** A 3-month apprenticeship and co-mentoring before matching may yield 10-15 genuinely prepared mentors. *—The Analogist*

* **Screen for relational history and demonstrated behavior, not motivation statements, which reward aspirational answers.** All seven personas reject "Why do you want to mentor?" as predictive and converge on behavioral, scenario-based assessment. The shared instrument set: anchored questions about admitting fault, repairing relationships, setting a boundary that was unwelcome, and handling frustration or quitting under stress, scored on a consistent rubric across dimensions such as comfort with ambiguity, reciprocity, and boundary awareness. The signal is self-awareness and emotional flexibility; defensive language or impatience with feedback reads as obligation or savior energy.
  * **Relational-capacity assessment over motivation inventory.** "Describe a time you had to admit you were wrong to someone younger." High capacity shows self-awareness and reciprocity, not service energy. *—The Lawyer*
  * **30-minute interview scored on a three-point rubric.** Ambiguity tolerance, reciprocity, and boundary awareness; 2-3 hours per volunteer to administer and score, but filters for depth over enthusiasm. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Scenario questions that predict relational failure.** "The teen isn't showing up and you're frustrated, what do you do?" surfaces savior energy and low frustration tolerance better than "why do you want to mentor?" *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Assess relational history, not intention.** Enduring relationships, references known for years, repaired mistakes, and sustained prior commitments predict follow-through. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Test for curiosity vs. mission-driven certainty.** "He tells you he doesn't believe you'll help, what do you do?" Capacity says "I'd ask what would help"; savior says "I'd show him I'm different." *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Structured questions reveal what people think you want to hear.** True capacity shows as patience with not-knowing and willingness to follow someone else's lead. *—The Empath*
  * **Monastic discernment plus theater-ensemble casting.** Listen early for savior, transactional, or genuinely present motivation, and use multi-stage evaluation including a group assessment. *—The Analogist*

* **Treat savior energy as a specific, screenable, and disqualifying pattern.** All seven personas name the rescue motivation as the most dangerous failure mode precisely because it springs from good intentions. From the teen's side it is suffocating: they become a project rather than a person, and a rescuer's investment in "success" overrides the teen's agency. Screening should probe for it directly and treat a mismatch between the mentor's definition of success (grades, graduation) and the teen's (survival, stability) as a relationship-breaker to surface before matching.
  * **Savior energy is palpable and disqualifying.** A teen senses within minutes whether an adult sees them or a project; that energy suffocates authentic connection. *—The Empath*
  * **Be explicit about savior screening; it is where good intentions do the most harm.** "Tell me about a time you wanted to help someone but they chose a different path"; have a frank conversation about what success means. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Savior shows as "I want to fix their lives"; capacity as "I want to understand them."** The why matters more than the what. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Three-question intake filter eliminates 20-30% early.** A 10-minute phone screen probing motivation, realistic concerns, and hoped-for gains catches rescue fantasy before the formal interview. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Run a trial pairing, shadow, or pilot before full commitment, because watching the real work outperforms the interview.** Four personas recommend a graduated entry: a candidate shadows an active mentor or completes a short trial match, and an explicit go/no-go decision follows. The rationale is that an awkward, real one-on-one session reveals readiness better than three interviews, lets weak fits self-select out, and surfaces naturals.
  * **8-week trial pairing with an explicit go/no-go, teen consulted confidentially.** A polite-but-hesitant teen counts as a "no"; harsh-sounding but catches mismatches early. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Shadow one full outing before final approval.** Watching awkward silences, defensiveness, and small wins moves a candidate from "interested" to "informed commitment." *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Dose-escalation match intensity.** Start with shorter, lower-vulnerability matches and frequent check-ins, graduating mentors who demonstrate stability to longer, more complex pairings. *—The Analogist*
  * **8-mentor soft launch to refine screening from real experience.** Match deliberately, meet monthly, then open recruitment for 4-5 more based on what was learned; the first cohort becomes culture-setting ambassadors. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Let the teen judge relational capacity, because they are the most accurate sensor of genuine presence.** A two-persona insight that shifts the locus of assessment off the committee: have candidates spend time with youth in a group activity before matching, then ask the teen whether the person felt safe and genuinely interested.
  * **Teens are excellent judges of genuine relational capacity.** "Does this person feel safe? Do they seem interested in you as a person?" *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Debrief the teen confidentially with a youth worker present.** Treat the teen's read of the trial pairing as decisive, not advisory. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Assess through observed action and task, not declaration.** Distinct from the trial-match point, two personas propose low-stakes task assignments and community events as a screen: who follows through, how they respond when plans change, whether they center themselves or learn about others.
  * **Give a small task and a mentor-community event before the interview.** Watch follow-through and whether they center themselves or genuinely learn about others; verify long-maintained relationships by contacting those people. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Group assessment in low-stakes conversation.** Observe chemistry and reliability, not just talent on paper. *—The Analogist*
