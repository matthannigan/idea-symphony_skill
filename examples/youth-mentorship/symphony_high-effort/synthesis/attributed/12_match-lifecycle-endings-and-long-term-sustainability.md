## Synthesized Insights by Question

### Question 1: Protocol when a pairing isn't working [User Q]

**Full question**: A one-year commitment is a program strength but also a pressure point — the emphasis on commitment can create social pressure to remain in a problematic, uncomfortable, or unsafe relationship rather than end it. What is the right protocol when a mentor-mentee pairing isn't working (who decides, on what signals, with what teen voice in the decision), how does the program transition without communicating abandonment to a teen whose history may already include adults who disappeared, and what does the attrition curve from similar programs suggest about a commitment architecture that reduces early drop-off without coercing participants into staying past the point where the relationship is helping?

* **The teen needs an independent voice and an exit door that does not route through the mentor.** All seven perspectives converge on centering teen agency, but several stress that a three-way conversation alone is insufficient because the mentor holds asymmetric power and can reframe the teen's discomfort as "growing pains." The strongest version: monthly check-ins between teen and coordinator with the mentor absent, plus a teen-initiated exit that does not require the teen to articulate or justify why. Give the teen genuine veto over their own participation, since their authority over their own life outweighs the one-year commitment.
  * **Independent teen check-ins plus a no-justification exit door, paired with a reset kit.** Build monthly one-on-ones with the coordinator (not the mentor present) and a teen-initiated exit door that does not require articulating why, paired with a therapy referral, no-blame closure conversation, and a 3-month waiting period before re-matching. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Teen voice as the trigger mechanism, signaled to a non-mentor staff person.** Youth-initiated exits correlate with the teen sensing the mentor is staying out of obligation; build pathways for the teen to signal unease to a non-mentor (a peer or alumni mentor) with no requirement to confront the current mentor. *—The Futurist*
  * **Coordinator talks to the teen first, then brings the mentor in as partner not defendant.** When signals emerge, ask the teen directly "What's working? What isn't? Do you want to stay in this?" so the teen experiences adults actually listening — which reframes the ending itself as a win for their agency. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Two-voice decision gate with teen veto power.** Both meet the coordinator separately first, then jointly with structured prompts; give the teen genuine veto over their own participation, which matters more than the one-year commitment. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Decision authority stays with the teen, framed as "is this working for you."** Three explicit signals trigger a structured pause; frame as "we're checking if this is working for you" rather than "this isn't working," signaling choice rather than rejection. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Flip responsibility so the teen authors the ending.** Train mentors to treat their own thought ("maybe she doesn't want to come") as the invitation for the teen to speak truthfully; the teen who has never been asked what they want gets to author that ending, not just receive it. *—The Storyteller*

* **Reframe early exit as data and "finding the right fit," not as broken-promise failure.** Multiple perspectives independently land on the same move: the one-year frame makes early exit feel like mutual failure, so teens hide discomfort. Naming a mismatch as evidence the program is working correctly removes the psychological weight the teen carries and prevents festering.
  * **Reframe commitment as conditional.** "We're committed to finding a relationship that works; if this pairing isn't it, that's data that helps us." *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A 90-day relationship adjustment window with no documentation penalty.** Pairs can request a rematch without narrative penalty, positioning early mismatches as predictable and data-generating rather than shameful. *—The Futurist*
  * **Transition as relationship repair, not program logistics.** Name it as "this relationship isn't the right fit, and that's information, not failure," then position the next match as "we learned something, let's try again with what we know." *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Reimagine "ending" as "graduation" using navigation language.** Use "we're helping you find the mentor-relationship that fits your growth right now" instead of "we're ending this," moving the teen into an active re-match pool rather than limbo. *—The Visionary*
  * **Reframe early exits as "finding the right fit," not program failure.** Teens who exit early should be re-matched once if they want, with a different mentor profile and explicit conversation about what didn't work. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Use graduated step-down and escalation toolkits before dissolution; not every struggling match needs to end.** Several perspectives propose intermediate moves between "struggling" and "terminated": shift to lower-intensity contact, change the meeting format, or bring in a peer-mentor coach. A match often needs a different tempo, not a death.
  * **Graduated step-down rather than the cliff.** Transition a failing match to a "check-in only" model or group mentoring for two months; the teen experiences "we found a shape that fits better," and the mentor stays in some form rather than vanishing. *—The Storyteller*
  * **Escalation toolkit before dissolution.** Bring in a peer mentor who has sustained a match, try a group activity to reset dynamics, or use structured prompts to help the pair reconnect — "let's try one more approach before we decide." *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Tiered escalation ending in a teen choice.** First a three-way conversation, then a trained "mentor-match coach" for 2–3 sessions, then a choice between modified commitment or a new mentor; never present ending as "we're removing you." *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Strategic rematch instead of termination.** Change the relationship's shape — meet less often in a different setting, shift to group activities, refocus on a skill — because a match doesn't need to die, it needs a different tempo. *—The Futurist*

* **Plan the attrition curve as a budget and staffing reality, and watch for a specific failure archetype.** The attrition estimates cluster tightly (roughly 15–25% early exits in months 1–4, then stabilizing), with two distinctive cautions: at-risk teens with abandonment histories may exit at the higher end, and a struggling match "helped" by escalating mentor effort can invert into a self-reinforcing withdrawal spiral.
  * **At-risk youth may exit at 20–25%, not the 10–15% of general programs.** Budget for structured early-termination protocols from month 2–3 onward; the real test is whether the program can absorb a 20% early-exit rate without funding collapse or coordinator burnout. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Watch for the "Shifting the Burden" pattern.** When a struggling match is "helped" by the mentor doing more emotional work per meeting, the teen withdraws further; the signal for intervention is the mentor doing more, not less. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Scaffolded commitment with soft/full/optional phases.** Months 1–3 soft (either party can pause), 4–9 full with structured exit, 9–12 optional continuation; track exits by time-to-failure and reason to learn whether matches are mismatched or the structure is creating premature stress. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Three-month micro-commitments with explicit renewal points.** "Let's try this through December, then we'll both decide"; matches that clear three months and renew have dramatically higher completion rates. *—The Momentum Builder*

* **Build a "relationship autopsy" culture so endings feed a living learning database.** A single perspective elevates the ending itself into the program's most valuable strategic asset.
  * **Structured reflection on every shifted pairing, fed into a living database.** Teens see themselves as researchers, not failures, and the data becomes the program's most valuable strategic asset on what makes matches thrive. *—The Visionary*

### Question 2: The texture of a good ending versus the wound of a quiet fade

**Full question**: A well-handled ending could be one of the most powerful things the program does for a teen who has rarely experienced an adult relationship that ended with intention, care, and closure. A no-show or gradual fade-out, conversely, is the wound the program most needs to prepare for. What does a genuinely good ending look and feel like — for the formal one-year completion, for an early termination, for a match that quietly fades — what emotional safety nets exist for the teen when a match doesn't survive, and does the program name this risk honestly upfront with teens and families or quietly hope it won't happen?

* **Ritualize closure as deliberate infrastructure, designed as heavily as the first meeting.** All seven perspectives converge: the ending must be a witnessed, sensory, intentional event (letter exchange, keepsake, shared meal, public milestone) so it registers as "this ending is being held with care" rather than "I'm being left." For a teen with abandonment history this is neurologically different from a "good luck" goodbye.
  * **A non-negotiable closure ritual built into the match agreement.** A structured final meeting with letter exchange, photo, or shared meal, with staff present to ensure intention, that happens even if the match ends early. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Intentional closure as infrastructure, plus a durable relationship artifact.** Design the final session as heavily as the first; create a jointly-authored or mentor-gifted object the teen retains that says "this was real and it mattered." *—The Futurist*
  * **Ceremonial endings as developmental milestones.** A program-wide "match celebration" where pairs share a story, exchange a handwritten note read aloud, or plant something that outlasts the relationship. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **A public ritual with family and a community witness.** The mentor gives a specific reflection ("here's what I've seen in you grow"), the pair exchange written notes, and a quarterly Milestone Celebration honors completing matches publicly. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A completion ritual with a kept artifact.** A structured final month and a small ceremony — final meal, letter exchange, or a program scrapbook photo the teen keeps — transforms an ending from abandonment to milestone. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **The ceremony of completion that names what the teen became.** A "graduation" moment where the mentor says "I watched you grow, and here's specifically what I saw change" — architecturally necessary for a teen who has never had an adult witness and name their growth. *—The Storyteller*
  * **A joyful completion ceremony witnessed by peers and community.** Makes an ending feel like a beginning: "We ended something good, on purpose, together." *—The Visionary*

* **Name the fade-out risk honestly upfront with teens and families rather than hoping it won't happen.** Strong convergence: transparency at intake is the only thing that turns a fade into a navigable transition instead of a fresh wound. Several note that every teen with abandonment trauma already knows adults disappear, so candor builds trust rather than undermining it — and in a faith-based context, honest acknowledgment of limits builds trust.
  * **Transparency from day one because the teen already knows adults disappear.** Tell families at intake that mentoring is a real relationship that sometimes ends, and that the program has a protocol to ensure it is not a sudden disappearance. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A "Relationship Reality" conversation at intake.** "Real relationships sometimes change. We design endings to be clear and caring... we'll help you understand why it's not about you." *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Name the risk upfront and design for the completion ritual.** "How it ends matters enormously... a bad ending is when it just stops." Make this part of intake orientation for teens and mentors. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Lean into the hard truth with agency in how to hold it.** "Mentoring relationships are real relationships, which means they can end... here's what you can do to stay grounded" — trust-building through clarity, not doom-saying. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Address the fade-out path explicitly with escalation steps.** Programs that name the likelihood of a gradual fade and build a follow-up protocol report teens feel less blindsided when it occurs. *—The Futurist*

* **The emotional safety net must be relational and pre-built, not logistical.** Several perspectives insist that closure alone is not enough; the teen needs concrete, named-in-advance after-care so an ending does not default to "I wasn't worth staying for."
  * **Pre-built grief support: a standing therapist relationship, a "match grief protocol," and concrete after-care.** Without these, closure becomes another interaction where the teen is expected to be okay with whatever happens. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **The coordinator does not disappear after an ending.** A check-in call two weeks later, possibly a note from the mentor, an explicit re-matching offer, and a peer-support circle between matches turn the waiting period from isolation into community. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **A "closure letter" protocol for fades and early terminations.** The mentor writes a brief letter explaining the decision in terms of the mentor's situation, not the teen's shortcomings, so the story doesn't default to "I wasn't worth staying for." *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A completion note plus post-closure counselor check-ins.** Partner with the school counselor for 2–3 narrative-work conversations after closure: "What did this mean to you? What do you carry forward?" *—The Pragmatist*
  * **A letter that lives forward, opened later.** A mentor letter held in a program archive and opened when the teen is 18, 21, or 25 — evidence the relationship was real and arriving when the teen most needs it. *—The Storyteller*

* **A well-handled ending is more painful in the moment than a quiet fade — and that is exactly why it requires trained, grief-competent facilitators.** One perspective surfaces a counterintuitive load-bearing point the others assume away: intentional closure forces the teen to feel loss, whereas a quiet fade lets the teen blame themselves ("he got busy") rather than face an adult's choice to leave. This reframes closure from a feel-good ritual into emotionally skilled work.
  * **Quiet fades let the teen avoid loss; good endings require grief-competent facilitators.** Without training, well-intentioned coordinators flatten closure into bureaucratic tidiness rather than genuine farewell. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Permission to grieve and celebrate simultaneously.** "I'm grateful for what we built, and I'm sad that this shape of our relationship is ending. Both feelings are real" — radically countercultural for kids whose past relationships got retroactively deleted by their endings. *—The Storyteller*

* **Recognize the warm, infrequent fade as a legitimate outcome, not a failure to pathologize.** Two perspectives distinguish abandonment (the wound) from a relationship that simply moves into a lower-intensity holding pattern, and warn against treating the latter as failure.
  * **The quiet fade as an explicit, celebrated option.** Warm but infrequent contact after year one is something to celebrate, not pathologize; some relationships matter because they're there, not because of intensity. *—The Storyteller*
  * **Reframe the fade as a relationship's shift into the background.** Name dormancy honestly — "you know where to find each other if you need to" — to remove the shame-spiral of a teen feeling guilty for not reaching out. *—The Visionary*

### Question 3: Year-one throughput, cohort sizing, single-coordinator dependency, unit economics, and adaptations to declining high-commitment volunteerism

**Full question**: Realistic year-one throughput depends on match initiation, active match duration, and attrition; one-on-one programs rely heavily on a single coordinator whose departure or burnout is a structural failure mode; and volunteerism in structured, high-commitment programs (weekly, year-long) is declining nationally even as interest in episodic and project-based volunteering grows. Should the program launch all 40 volunteer pairs simultaneously or stage intake in cohorts, what unit cost structure looks like at 20/30/40 active matches, at what match volume does the program become financially viable without full grant dependence, what redundancy protects against single-coordinator failure, and what program structure adaptations (matching flexibility, commitment scaffolding, mentor-led peer support) protect match stability against the broader volunteer-attrition trend?

* **Target roughly 25–30 active matches in year one, not 40; one coordinator cannot hold 40 without quality collapse.** Six of seven perspectives independently converge on this number and the underlying mechanism: mentoring labor is intensive at the margins, a well-matched pair needs ~15 minutes of coordinator time monthly while a struggling one needs 60+, and the $15K seed responsibly supports about 20–25 matches. Stretching to 40 makes match quality, not volunteer count, the binding constraint.
  * **Pilot with 15–20 matches; $15K covers that responsibly, 40 stretches into a unit cost that depends entirely on declining volunteer enthusiasm.** At 40, monthly check-ins become superficial. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **The $15K seed supports ~20–25 matches at realistic quality; validate a 20-match model first.** Funders increasingly want to scale proof-of-concept rather than fund growth of undertested programs. *—The Futurist*
  * **The program becomes stable at the volume the coordinator can sustain — likely 25–30 — not at maximum volunteer count.** Build the budget and hiring plan around that number, not the 40 who expressed interest. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Aim for 25–30 active matches; the inflection where quality degrades is ~35–40.** Below it coordinator time is the constraint; above it, quality degrades and burnout accelerates. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **One coordinator can hold 30–40 only if 70% of matches are well-matched from the start.** A well-matched pair needs 15 minutes monthly; a struggling one needs 60+, so front-load match assessment to reduce back-end troubleshooting. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **About 30 quality matches is the ceiling for one coordinator.** "Beyond that, we need to either hire a second person or reduce the quality of mentoring." *—The Storyteller*

* **Unit cost falls with scale but only if peer structure absorbs load: roughly $750/match at 20, ~$500 at 30, ~$375–700 at 40.** Several perspectives produce strikingly consistent figures and the same conclusion: viability without full grant dependence requires a second-year funding source identified early, because the $15K seed cannot sustain a second coordinator.
  * **~$750 (20), ~$500 (30), ~$375 (40) — but the low end only holds with distributed workload and peer-mentor leadership.** *—The Futurist*
  * **$750/$500/$375 per match; aim for 25–30, document outcomes, then secure $40–50K in year two for a second coordinator.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **$2,500–$3,500 per active match per year all-in; at 40 the math breaks and one coordinator cannot manage it.** Financial stability arrives at the sustainable match volume, ~25–30. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **At 20, $15K covers a ~$12K coordinator plus insurance and materials; at 30 a second coordinator becomes mandatory ($25K+); at 40, $500–700/match makes grant dependence unsustainable without year-two funding.** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Coordinator salary is ~20–30% of time (~$8–12K) for 40 matches, plus insurance, training, checks.** $15K covers maybe 15–20 matches responsibly. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **De-risk the single coordinator now through a tiered peer-mentor / mentor-council layer and a documented operations guide.** Strong convergence: the coordinator is the structural single point of failure, and the fix is distributing relational and troubleshooting work to experienced mentors plus documenting processes so a temporary coordinator can step in for weeks.
  * **A "Mentor Council" of 3–4 experienced mentors that can operate 4–6 weeks if the coordinator is unavailable, plus a Program Operations Guide.** Shifts from single-person dependency to distributed expertise. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Tiered peer-mentor structure by month 6 for succession protection.** 3–4 mentors co-facilitate circles and serve as escalation points so peers can sustain the core until a replacement is hired. *—The Futurist*
  * **A mentor peer-leadership layer that holds the program's culture if the coordinator leaves.** Turns volunteer capacity into program infrastructure and makes the org structure distributed rather than hierarchical. *—The Visionary*
  * **A continuity binder, quarterly board briefings, and a "coordinator-on-call" volunteer (e.g., a retired social worker) as a pressure valve.** Not a replacement, but prevents complete paralysis. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Cross-train a senior mentor plus a part-time admin to offload the "busy work."** A $15K budget can't fund two coordinators, so build redundancy sideways. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A peer leader per cohort and a named backup from the church admin team, plus a continuity manual.** "Our program isn't fragile; we've designed it to hold." *—The Storyteller*

* **Adapt to declining high-commitment volunteerism with a commitment ladder and an honored episodic periphery.** Convergence on offering multiple entry intensities (a full year, a renewable 6-month, a 3-month trial) and a milestone/exit gateway around month 6, so the core stays one-to-one while the periphery flexes. Several note an exit-gateway at month 6 raises through-month-12 retention to ~80%.
  * **A "commitment ladder" of 52-week, 6-month renewable, and 3-month trial tracks.** Accommodates the dominant new trend while keeping meaningful minimum durations. *—The Futurist*
  * **A tiered commitment menu — intensive, steady, skill-share — protecting core matches with the highest-commitment mentors.** Fewer 52-week matches with mentors genuinely in beats 40 wavering ones. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **An exit gateway at month 6; of those who extend, retention through month 12 jumps to 80%+.** Plus a sabbatical option so a temporarily stressed mentor can pause rather than quit. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Recruit 50 to get 35–40 committed matches; honor the episodic periphery explicitly.** Assume 20–25% drop out or reduce mid-year; "some mentors shift to episodic roles after their year — that's valuable and honored, not a failure." *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A 3-month trial ramping to 6 months then a year, combined with a waterfall cohort model.** Filters out mentors likely to drop at month 2 and reduces the mid-year attrition cliff. *—The Visionary*

* **On launch timing the personas split: most favor staged cohorts, but one argues staging traps the coordinator in perpetual intake.** Five perspectives recommend staggered quarterly or twice-yearly cohorts so each wave teaches the next and load is distributed. One dissents directly: staging spreads coordinator effort thinly across 12 months and never lets the coordinator focus on struggling matches.
  * **Stage in three ~12-person cohorts; staggered intake sees 8–12% better year-one retention than front-loaded models.** Lets the coordinator ramp and apply learning to later intake. *—The Futurist*
  * **Quarterly cohorts of ~10; each teaches the coordinator what works before all 40 are active.** Also reveals whether the market truly has 40 serious volunteers. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Two cohorts (spring/fall) offset by 6 months as a natural pressure valve and a built-in model for the second coordinator.** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A "waterfall cohort" of 8–10 every 3 months, each with an embedded pioneer mentor.** The coordinator becomes a "wave manager" rather than a crisis manager. *—The Visionary*
  * **Stagger into three waves so each teen enters a "generation" of mentors with a shared language.** The narrative structure feels organic rather than bureaucratic. *—The Storyteller*
  * **Dissent: launch a full cohort at once with a 6-week intensive setup, then stabilization.** Staging keeps the coordinator perpetually in intake mode, never able to focus deeply on struggling matches; concentrate the chaos upfront for predictability after. *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 4: Match quality as a self-amplifying signal — and the downward cycle

**Full question**: A strong match builds trust, which encourages the teen to show up, which gives the mentor positive feedback, which increases mentor effort — a self-amplifying signal. A weak match runs the same dynamic in reverse: low engagement, mentor pulling back, teen disengaging further, a downward cycle. Deep mentor investment can also create its own ceiling, where ownership makes it harder for the teen to outgrow the relationship; and at scale, even a successful program can hit a tipping point where coordination demands grow faster than the volunteer base can absorb, hollowing out the relational quality that made it work. What screening and early-intervention mechanisms catch a deteriorating match before the downward cycle becomes self-reinforcing, and at what point does growth begin to hollow out relational quality?

* **Catch deterioration early with dual-channel signals: an independent teen pulse check plus a mentor health check, by month 2.** All seven perspectives converge on early-warning monitoring, and most insist the teen channel must be separate from the mentor's self-report, because a mentor pulling back will rationalize ("the teen's been busy") and mask the real signal.
  * **Independent monthly teen pulse checks ("how's the match, 1–5?") that don't rely on anyone's honest self-report.** Mentors rationalize disengagement; create early-warning signals that don't depend on the mentor's account. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A "match vitality dashboard" by month 2 tracking declining attendance, shorter meetings, topic narrowing, reduced mentor prep.** Pairs showing 2+ indicators get rapid intervention, which research shows can reverse within a single strategic intervention. *—The Futurist*
  * **A monthly "match health pulse" (three mentor questions) paired with a separate teen 1–10 check.** The dual signal catches the downward spiral before the mentor backs off and the teen disengages further. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **A monthly anonymous teen text pulse on three items, surfacing trends at week 6 not week 20.** "We're noticing your excitement is trending down. Let's talk, no pressure." *—The Visionary*
  * **A three-week micro-check-in after about three meetings.** Course-correction, not evaluation: "early intervention feels like coaching; late intervention feels like failure." *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **A single energy question after month two.** "Are meetings something you both look forward to, or does it feel like an obligation?" The honest answer is the signal. *—The Storyteller*

* **Use a behavioral, direction-of-effort metric — who initiates — as the objective early signal, not subjective judgment.** One perspective contributes a distinctive diagnostic the others gesture at but do not name: in healthy matches mentor initiation drops after week 2 as the teen takes over; in failing ones it stays high or grows. A mentor "doing all the planning" is a red flag, not a mark of excellence.
  * **Track mentor-initiation direction: it should fall sharply after week 2; if it stays high or grows, that's a week-6 red flag.** Build early-intervention signals from behavioral metrics, not subjective judgment, and use a Red/Yellow/Green Match Health Index at scale. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Design positive-feedback spirals deliberately into the first three meetings.** Plan early meetings around the teen's interests and train mentors to voice genuine appreciation, so trust builds rather than being left to chemistry. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Rediscover what connects the pair rather than adding meetings.** The intervention for a slipping match is better meetings — a shared interest or a real problem tackled together — shifting from "running out of things to say" to "working on something together." *—The Storyteller*

* **Name and design against the mentor-investment ceiling so ownership doesn't trap the teen.** Convergence: deep mentor investment can curdle into ownership where the teen feels guilty and stays in discomfort. The fix is to frame mentor effort as the mentor's chosen gift, not the teen's debt, and to position the mentor as a steady reference point whose goal is to widen the teen's web of relationships.
  * **Explicitly reframe mentor effort as the mentor's choice, not the teen's debt.** "Your investment is about what you want to give, not what we owe you," with clear boundaries on mentor intensity. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Train mentors to build the teen's relationships beyond the mentor.** "If they're only bringing problems to you, that's a relationship built on neediness, not growth"; distribute the load across teachers, coaches, peers. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Frame the mentor as consistent companion, not substitute parent.** "My job is to show up, ask questions, believe in you — not to fix your life"; surface the caretaking-instinct pattern in training. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A month-3 conversation where the teen architects the support intensity.** "I want you to challenge me on school, but I need space to figure out friend drama myself" — removing the dynamic where over-investment makes the teen feel managed. *—The Visionary*
  * **The mentor models celebrating the teen's growing independence.** "You're becoming someone who doesn't need me in this way, and that's beautiful... I'm still here, just differently." *—The Storyteller*

* **Treat the scale-quality ceiling as a deliberate design choice, not a problem to engineer away.** Strong convergence and an unresolved strategic fork: at 40+ matches with one coordinator, relational quality degrades in ways no process improvement fully recovers. The program either stays small and exceptional or grows and becomes mediocre — and several argue the honest answer is to stay small and replicate rather than scale centrally.
  * **The relational-quality ceiling at scale is real and probably baked into the model.** Stay small (15–25, high quality) or grow and become mediocre; there is no secret that makes both possible without changing the model. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Scaling beyond 40–50 with one coordinator creates structural degradation no process improvement recovers.** Decide now: 30 matches exceptionally well, or 60+ at acceptable-but-shallow quality. *—The Futurist*
  * **40 mediocre matches isn't success, it's failing differently.** "25 strong, stable matches" is the story that attracts the second round of funding; design for quality first and let scale follow. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **The growth ceiling is a feature dictating strategy, not a bug to fix by hiring.** Stay at 25–30, build extraordinary outcomes data, then license or replicate rather than scale centrally. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **A Red/Yellow/Green Match Health Index that reads systemic burnout.** When multiple matches go Yellow, it signals burnout is systemic — reduce expectations, add peer support, or stagger intake so load doesn't compound. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Reframe unit economics around social ROI: 10 strong matches may beat 40 mediocre ones.** Sell "transformation depth," not "scale" — a smaller program with legendary outcomes attracts more repeat funding. *—The Visionary*

* **Distinguish "needs a different match" from "needs skill-building" so recoverable matches are not misdiagnosed as failures.** One perspective adds a triage distinction the others don't make explicit.
  * **A 6-week "skill focus" option co-facilitated by a peer mentor for matches that struggle but want to continue.** A mentor struggling with a teen's grief response may need training, not replacement; personality clashes need rematch. *—The Systems Thinker*

### Question 5: Designing for the second grant, the funding cliff, and faith-network collective-impact positioning

**Full question**: $15K seed funding places the program in a well-understood trajectory: a 12–18 month launch window before the funding cliff, with most community youth programs failing in the gap between initial grant and sustainable funding. Regional foundation seed grants are increasingly structured as proof-of-concept investments expecting graduation toward government contracts, federal youth-services funding (chronic-absenteeism intervention streams, trauma-informed services), or earned-revenue models within 18–24 months — and collective-impact infrastructure coordinating schools, justice, health, and community organizations is crystallizing in most major urban markets. What outcome documentation architecture should the program build from day one, what design decisions made now will be costly to reverse once shaped to the first funder's reporting requirements, and what formal partnership architecture should the program pursue now to be positioned as a natural node — including faith-network replication or technical-assistance positioning — before that infrastructure crystallizes around existing anchor partners?

* **Build outcome documentation from day one, measuring what the next funder needs, not just what the first one asks.** All seven perspectives converge: the funding cliff kills programs that wait until year-end to measure, or that build measurement only around grant one's template. Track attendance, match duration, mentor retention, and narrative case stories from month one, in a lightweight tool, so a credible year-12 story exists.
  * **A "dual reporting system" tracking the current funder's numbers plus outcome proxies major funders care about (attendance, grades, police contact).** Requires school and justice data-access buy-in, which takes lead time. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Documentation architecture serving both year-one reporting and funder diversification.** Measure youth attendance, mentor retention, relationship quality, and cost/scale so grant two doesn't need entirely different data. *—The Futurist*
  * **Track five basics from month one via a simple Google Form, not a database.** Most funders believe programs that measured from the start and are skeptical of those that waited for the deadline. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **A "Learning Agenda" framework from month one that serves both foundation reporting and program development.** Track demographic reach, relationship quality, early teen outcomes, and program operation. *—The Systems Thinker*

### Question 6: What "thriving" looks like three years in — the aspirational endpoint

**Full question**: Imagine it is three years from now and this mentorship program has become something the neighborhood actively celebrates — a source of visible pride, a story the teens themselves tell others, a model peer organizations are asking to learn from. * **A visitor notices teen-driven peer recruitment and a waiting list: the program has become youth culture, not a service.** All seven perspectives converge: the defining signal of thriving is that teens recruit peers unprompted ("mine's actually helpful"), intake shifts from counselor referral to peer referral, and there's a waiting list. Mentors are selectively recruited rather than widely solicited.
  * **The teen-to-peer recruiting flywheel and a long waiting list built by the teens themselves.** Graduated teens become informal ambassadors; mentors recruit mentors. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Peer-driven adoption as the marker of authentic cultural embedding.** The program transitions from "staff-promoted" to "peer-promoted." *—The Futurist*
  * **Intake happens through peer referral, not counselor referral.** A teen overheard telling a peer "you should ask about it" — the program has organically become part of youth culture. *—The Visionary*
  * **Teens drop by to volunteer, lead tours, and recruit; a 3-month waiting list forms.** The program has become a teen asset, not a service received. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Teens recruit peers: "You should do this. Mine's actually helpful."** The most powerful signal of a visible neighborhood presence. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **The walking testimony: a teen introduces their mentor unprompted in the grocery store.** The relationship has moved from "program requirement" to "actual person in my life." *—The Storyteller*

* **A visitor sees a community of practice among mentors, not isolated pairs; mentors stay for reciprocity and meaning.** Convergence on a mentor culture (a lounge, peer circles, mentors chatting) and on the mentor testimony that the relationship changed them — "I get as much as I give" — and that the program trusts them to adapt, not just follow steps.
  * **Mentors lingering in a lounge, decompressing together — mentoring as a community, not a task.** Design a physical landing space and organic mentor gatherings. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Mentors articulate meaning, not just task satisfaction.** "I'm part of a relational practice that's changing the trajectory... the program trusts me to adapt." *—The Futurist*
  * **Mentors recruiting mentors because the relationship nourishes them.** "I keep coming back because I watch them become themselves" — the mentor as witness, not hero. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Mentor retention above 75% with mentors moving into peer-support roles.** "I thought I was helping a teen. Turns out I was helping myself find meaning." *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Reciprocity as the irreducible currency: "I get as much from him as he gets from me."** A peer community sharing strategies and celebrating wins sustains mentors past year three. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **The program heals adult loneliness too: "This changed me... we grew together."** Mentors write about it in their faith small groups and invite friends. *—The Visionary*

* **A teenager names a specific relational shift — "they stayed, I didn't have to earn it" — not a generic outcome.** Strong convergence on the teen testimony centering reliability and being chosen, which rewrites the teen's implicit narrative that adults disappear, alongside concrete outcomes (attendance, college, fewer justice contacts) as secondary evidence.
  * **"They're still here. I didn't have to earn it."** The teen has seen the mentor show up through bad days; reliability rewrites the abandonment narrative. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **"He believed in me before I believed in myself... he could've quit. But he kept showing up."** The endpoint is a relational stance, not a specific achievement. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **"My mentor didn't try to fix me. She just kept showing up... knowing she was there changed how I thought about myself."** Specific relational shifts: consistency, belief, visible interest in the teen's future. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **"An adult who believes in me who's not trying to fix me or judge me... he shows me what an adult can be."** Concrete outcomes plus the deeper win of "someone chose to be in my corner." *—The Pragmatist*
  * **"Having an adult who actually listened, who didn't have an agenda for me — that woke me up."** Said by a former teen now in a peer-leadership role. *—The Visionary*

* **Thriving means institutional muscle memory and sustainable expansion: the program is woven into schools, justice, and a diversified budget.** Several perspectives describe the program embedded as assumed infrastructure (counselors and justice officers reach for it reflexively) and operating at 40+ matches on a diversified ~$35K budget with multiple coordinators and peer mentors.
  * **Institutional muscle memory: the counselor and justice officer reflexively reach for the program.** "One of the things that actually works here" — institutional permanence is what thriving feels like at scale. *—The Storyteller*
  * **Outcomes embedded in institutions; the program no longer feels like an external intervention.** Schools reference mentors on attendance; justice sees mentorship as reentry planning. *—The Futurist*
  * **40+ matches, 2 coordinators, 4–5 peer mentors, ~$35K diversified across grants, contracts, in-kind, and donors.** Selective mentor recruitment and a 3-month teen waiting list. *—The Systems Thinker*

### Question 7: What if a one-year commitment is the wrong unit entirely

**Full question**: The program asks for a one-year commitment from mentors, but what if the meaningful relational unit for a 15-year-old is a decade — the period from now until they are 25 and have established adult footing? What would program design look like if it was explicitly trying to create relationships that survive the program's end rather than relationships that fulfill the program's duration? What gets built differently when "program success" means the mentor and teen are still in contact in 2036?

* **Reframe the program as a launch of a potentially decade-long relationship, not a one-year container.** All seven perspectives converge: hold the structured year intact but change the framing and success metric to relationship continuity, with variable intensity matched to the teen's developmental stage (intensive at 15, transitional at 18–21, peer-like at 22+).
  * **A "bridge into adulthood" over a critical 1–3 year window, with success meaning the teen is ready for the next relationship type.** A literal decade is kinship, not mentoring, and most volunteers can't commit it; design the window, not the decade. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Shift the role from "provider of mentorship" to "initiator of lifelong connection," with transition points every 2–3 years.** Weekly to monthly to text-with-annual-visits, modeling a relational maturity many trauma-affected youth have never witnessed. *—The Futurist*
  * **A "relationship launchpad" rather than a "commitment container."** Celebrate matches moving to sustained informal contact as successes, not "completion"; ask mentors to "build a real relationship and see where it goes." *—The Futurist*
  * **Decade-thinking as framing, not formal restructuring: "in this person's corner from now until they're 25."** Year one stays structured; the framing makes mentors understand they're building a relationship, not doing a job. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **"Lifespan mentorship" with adaptive roles: 14–17 intensive, 18–21 transition, 22+ peer.** "At least 10 years of contact in whatever form makes sense," de-emphasizing the program's role after years 1–3. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A "relationship trajectory" model: intensive year one, sustained year two, emeritus thereafter.** "We're building something that probably lasts longer than the program itself." *—The Pragmatist*
  * **The "anti-program program": lasting relationships that started through institutional introduction.** The infrastructure is just matching and initial training; the program gets out of the way. *—The Storyteller*

* **Redefine success and outcome metrics as relationship survival, which forces new tracking infrastructure.** Several perspectives converge that measuring "still in active contact at 24 months post-program" rather than "completed 12 months" is more meaningful but harder, requiring alumni-tracking and contact-info protocols.
  * **Measure "% still in active contact at 24 months post-program-end" and persistence into college/job support.** Harder to track but it measures what actually matters during the critical developmental window. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Count "success" as a stable long-term relationship rather than program completion.** Year one becomes trust and identity work; by year three the mentorship role dissolves but the relationship persists. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A "Decade Map" sketched at orientation as a non-binding north star.** Mentors who can't imagine a relationship persisting past year one shouldn't be matched. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Decade design changes who you screen for, the funding story, and the program's identity as a network.** Convergence that longevity-oriented matching demands more selective screening (stable mentors, capacity for the messy middle years) and opens new funding (workforce, college-access), with the program evolving into a tended network of relationships and an alumni-mentor pipeline.
  * **Longevity screening: stable mentors who aren't in crisis and aren't expecting the relationship to complete them.** More selective, fewer matches, but matches with staying power. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **A new funding story: "community leadership development and employment-pathway creation."** Opens workforce-development and college-access funding and an alumni-mentor model; cost per teen drops when measured across a decade. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **The program evolves into a network, not an intervention — reunions, a mentor channel, storytelling nights.** The outcome measured is "how many relationships are still active five years later." *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **A "mentor ambassador" role and a mentor-matching network introducing teens to professionals.** The program weaves a community of trusted adults around young people across a decade. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **"Connection continuity" rather than "commitment continuity": a mentoring constellation at multiple intensities.** A mentor who can't meet weekly joins quarterly gatherings — "I'm still here, just in a different way." *—The Visionary*

* **The teen's emotional architecture shifts when the relationship is framed as having seasons but not an expiration date.** Several perspectives note that telling the teen the mentor is "a real adult in your life... they might step back, but they're not leaving" changes how deeply the teen invests, and uniquely positions the long-present mentor to mark life milestones from 15 to 25.
  * **A "mentor continuity expectation" in the teen's orientation.** Permission for the relationship to have seasons but not to end makes the teen invest more deeply, knowing it's not conditional on the program's timeline. *—The Visionary*
  * **The milestone architecture for 15 to 25, with the mentor as the thread running through the teen's story.** Uniquely positioned to mark high school completion, first job, first adult relationship. *—The Storyteller*
  * **"Intentional presence across life chapters" where the mentor's role deepens rather than ends.** Still there at 22 navigating college, at 25 facing "what does it mean to be an adult." *—The Visionary*

* **A direct caution: the decade frame risks unmeasurable outcomes and codependency, and teen readiness is not constant.** One perspective dissents from the aspirational consensus, surfacing buried costs: defining success as "still in contact in 2036" makes the formal structure irrelevant and the outcome unmeasurable, and some teens need functional support before relational deepening.
  * **"Still in contact in 2036" makes the program structure irrelevant and the outcome unmeasurable; build two explicit tracks instead.** A formal reporting year plus an informal "alumni connection" option, so pairs choose their arc without confusing the program's promise. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Teen development doesn't follow program timelines.** Some 15-year-olds are still in survival mode and need functional support (rides, food, safe space) before relational deepening; build flexibility into relationship depth and duration. *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 8: The end of the commitment — the final meeting

**Full question**: Imagine the final meeting of a one-year match — both participants know the formal commitment ends today — and consider what your program does to ensure that conclusion feels like a milestone rather than an abandonment, especially for a teen whose history may already include adults who disappeared.

* **Mandate the final meeting as a structured milestone ritual with a kept artifact, not a passive close.** All seven perspectives converge: the final meeting is potentially the highest-stakes moment in the program, so it must be designed (reflection, an exchanged letter or keepsake, naming what the relationship gave each person) and the closing ritual should be non-negotiable, signed at the start, so no mentor can ghost.
  * **A mandated closing ritual: letters read aloud, a keepsake exchanged, the mentor naming what the relationship gave.** Make it non-negotiable in the match agreement so mentors uncomfortable with goodbyes cannot exit quietly. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A ceremonial milestone, not a release date, with the pair leaving knowing exactly what happens next.** Reflection on growth, exchange of tokens, introduction to next-phase supports. *—The Futurist*
  * **A structured four-week completion cycle: separate reflections, shared reading, a co-designed celebration, a program-wide gathering.** Plus a signed completion certificate and a mentor letter as a "relic." *—The Pragmatist*
  * **A structured-ritual final meeting: regular activity first, then reflection, then a kept letter; the teen gives back or not, no obligation.** "This program's requirement ends, but our relationship doesn't have to." *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **The pair co-designs the final meeting — a special outing, a meal, a meaningful place — with a reflection exchange.** "One moment that stayed with me," a tangible memento; naming what it meant is neurologically different from silence. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **The mentor brings something — a photo, a year-long letter — and the pair makes a concrete plan for what's next.** "We've reached the milestone; here's what we're honoring today." *—The Storyteller*
  * **A "continuation ceremony": letters opened in five years, planting something, a time capsule, a forward-looking question.** "We're not ending; we're marking a milestone." *—The Visionary*

* **Tell the teen directly, in words, that the ending is not their failure.** Strong convergence on an explicit, separate anti-abandonment message — because a teen primed to expect abandonment will fill silence with "I messed it up again." Several pair this with a dedicated final-month conversation for teens with abandonment histories.
  * **"You didn't do anything wrong. You weren't too much or not enough... you absolutely were worth a year of this person's time."** Without this naming, the teen's brain fills the silence with old stories. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **A separate final-month conversation naming the risk: "You're not abandoned when this program ends."** Anticipatory naming beats letting the teen assume the silence means something went wrong. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Frame the ending as proof of the teen's own achievement: "You've shown up for a whole year... I'm proud of you."** The program's end is incidental to what the teen accomplished by showing up. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **The teen as the expert in their own transformation, witnessed by peers and coordinator.** Reverses the power dynamic — "they did the work; the mentor was a mirror." *—The Visionary*
  * **Position the moment as proof that adults don't always disappear without notice.** "We're ending the formal year together, on purpose, with care. This is different from the adults who left without explanation." *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Make the post-program relationship an explicit, documented choice — continue, pause, or clean break — rather than an ambiguous "we'll stay in touch."** Convergence that the vague promise tends to fade, so the program should facilitate an explicit conversation (with guardian consent for minors) and offer a structured vessel for those who want to persist.
  * **An optional "Stay-In-Touch Agreement" naming frequency and channel, revisited in six months.** Removes the ambiguity of "we'll stay in touch," which often fades; many will drift, and that's okay. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Explicitly validate continuation and help exchange contact info with guardian consent.** "The program is completing, but your relationship doesn't have to" — removing the guilt of staying in touch. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **A facilitated conversation: "Are we saying goodbye for now, or is this the beginning of something different?"** Talking about it beats pretending and then ghosting. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **The ending sketches the teen's "next chapter architecture" so they don't free-fall.** A planned handoff — staying connected, a peer-mentor role, a community group — is what prevents the fade that feels like abandonment. *—The Visionary*

* **Recognize the mentor's year and hold space for grief alongside celebration.** Several perspectives add that the mentor needs their commitment honored (so they'll mentor again or leave whole), and that a good ending names both pride and sadness at once.
  * **Honor the mentor's 52 meetings so they feel the year was worth it.** A certificate or community gathering: "You showed a young person that an adult can stay, can be honest, can care without strings." *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **The grief permission slip: "I'm so proud of who you've become, and I'm going to miss seeing you every week."** A teen learns humans can hold both joy and sadness, completion and loss. *—The Storyteller*
  * **A handoff ritual to a next-phase mentor or a peer mentor so the teen isn't suddenly alone.** "You're graduating from one phase of this community to another," not "you're exiting." *—The Storyteller*

### Question 9: Long commitment as self-selection mechanism

**Full question**: A one-year minimum commitment will deter some potential volunteers. What if this is exactly the point — and how might the program use the commitment requirement as a deliberate filter that surfaces only the mentors most likely to stay, thereby reducing match disruption for teens who have already experienced abandonment?

* **The one-year ask is a deliberate, protective filter, not a barrier — and roughly 30% who self-select in are the ones you want.** All seven perspectives converge: a substantial commitment screens out episodic, unreliable, or unreflective volunteers, and the ~30% who remain are deliberate and far more likely to follow through. Several cite a stark retention contrast (programs with low barriers see 40–50% dissolution; high-barrier programs see 70–80% completion).
  * **A documented filter: 30–40% of prospects drop at the application stage with a 12+ month ask, which is correct filtering.** The mentors who stay are more likely to follow through; frame it as a filter, not a burden. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A documented filter that measurably improves match stability.** Lower barriers see higher enrollment but significantly higher attrition; 52-week minimums see higher completion and relationship quality. *—The Futurist*
  * **About 70% self-select out, "and that's beautiful" — the 30% have thought it through.** They won't vanish in month three when life gets busy. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Low-barrier programs see 40–50% dissolution; serious-commitment programs see 70–80% completion.** Remaining mentors interpret the hard stretch as part of a real relationship, not a mistake. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **The one-year weekly ask filters out episodic seekers and the unreflective; "this is exactly the point."** "Yes, I can commit to a year" on reflection predicts follow-through; don't soft-pedal it in recruitment. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Mentors who agree to a year are self-selecting for seriousness, building a specific culture.** "Everyone in the room has said I'm doing this because I believe it matters" — the thing that holds the program together when support is thin. *—The Storyteller*
  * **Only mentors who believe in sustained presence become one-on-one pairs; others contribute differently.** Abandonment becomes almost impossible because only people with that capacity self-select in. *—The Visionary*

* **Make the commitment explicit to the teen as a protective signal: "this adult chose to stay for you."** Strong convergence on reframing the requirement from a burden on volunteers to a gift to the teen — telling the teen their mentor made a binding, deliberate choice measurably deepens trust and safety for a teen exquisitely sensitive to mentor commitment level.
  * **The commitment as protective for the teen: "your mentor signed up for a year because they wanted to be in your life for real."** Frames the commitment as protection, not restriction. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Reframe the requirement as "a gift to the teens" in recruitment.** "Teens here have often experienced adults who disappeared. We ask for a year because your teen needs to know you're staying." *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A binding commitment, presented as such, measurably increases teen trust depth.** Youth with abandonment history are exquisitely sensitive to mentor commitment level; non-negotiable signals "this adult made a real choice." *—The Futurist*
  * **A stability signal: "this adult isn't auditioning me; this adult chose to show up."** Teens may still test the commitment, but from a position of greater safety. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **The abandonment-proof design: a publicly, deliberately committed mentor offers something different from the pattern.** Not a guarantee, but a signal the teen can relax into. *—The Storyteller*

* **Use screening and visible commitment rituals to convert the filter into self-reinforcing accountability and identity.** Several perspectives go beyond the filter to make the commitment psychologically and socially load-bearing: screen for motivation and follow-through patterns, make the commitment a visible public identity, and build renewal checkpoints so the "yes" is chosen repeatedly.
  * **Screen for the deepest motivation, not the shallowest availability.** "Why a full year?" — mentors answering "because you need people who stay" differ from those answering "I have time." *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Screen for follow-through patterns and honest motivation, not just available time.** A one-year commitment is a proxy for life-slack, not for good mentoring; ask "what will keep you showing up in month 9?" *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Make the commitment a visible community marker — a pin, a public list, social recognition.** A private contract becomes a public identity that strengthens the commitment. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Commitment as a source of mentor pride and self-concept that becomes protective when things get hard.** "I'm the kind of person who shows up" — accountability to oneself, not coercion. *—The Momentum Builder*
  * **Visible renewal checkpoints at months 6 and 12 where mentors choose again, relationally.** An ongoing yes deepens the commitment beyond the one-time yes at the start. *—The Visionary*
  * **Strict commitment messaging is a recruitment amplifier, not a deterrent.** Serious people step forward, are more reliable, and recruit their friends; a "come whenever" program attracts churn. *—The Pragmatist*

* **A caution: the filter risks excluding good mentors with messy-but-reliable lives, so define "commitment" as reliability, not resourced stability.** A couple of perspectives flag the blind spot in the self-selection enthusiasm: a year-long ask can screen out experienced mentors whose lives are chaotic but who are still reliable, so the program must specify what stability means.
  * **The filter may attract only the most resourced (those who can afford a year of free time); specify that stability means reliability, not perfection.** "If your life is chaotic right now, you're probably not the right fit — come back when it stabilizes." *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Be specific about stability to avoid filtering out experienced mentors with messy lives.** Not perfection, but reliability and the capacity to show up. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Position commitment as a hedge that works in your favor amid the volunteer-attrition trend.** Market to the shrinking cohort who want depth — often older mentors, people who've been mentored, people with strong community ties. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Regularly test whether one year is actually the right unit, using the program's own attrition data.** One perspective adds the discipline of treating the commitment length as a hypothesis to validate, not an article of faith.
  * **Track whether early exits cluster in months 3–5; if so, the timeline may be misaligned with relationship development.** Every year ask "is our commitment structure working?" and consider shorter initial commitments with an extension option. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Track the correlation between voluntary long-arc commitment and retention/match quality to inform future messaging.** The hypothesis: voluntary commitment to a longer arc selects for stronger relational capacity. *—The Systems Thinker*
