## Synthesized Insights by Question

### Question 12: Matching variables, weighting, and what actually predicts realized match quality [User Q]

**Full question** [User Q]: Compatibility likely spans demographics, shared interests, geographic proximity, personality fit, and referral context — but realized match quality (where a relationship actually thrives, not where it could theoretically survive) hinges on factors beyond surface similarity. What structured weighting system should govern matching, which variables carry the highest risk of harm if prioritized incorrectly, and how does intake elicit honest preference data from teens who may distrust the institutions making the referral?

* **Treat demographics and shared interests as necessary-but-insufficient screening, and weight relational disposition above surface similarity.** All seven personas converge here: the variables that predict a thriving relationship are not the ones that look good on a compatibility matrix. The strongest predictors named are the mentor's curiosity about difference rather than sameness, the capacity to witness rather than fix, proven follow-through, and the teen's own felt sense of being seen. Shared hobbies are noise that can mask incompatibility. Build a weighting system that ranks relational intention and reliability at the top, treats demographic and interest overlap as a tiebreaker, and explicitly downweights assumed common ground.
  * **Downweight assumed shared interests; weight teen's stated openness and mentor's curiosity about difference.** Tech recruiting learned that weighting explicit categories (degree, prior titles) underpredicts who thrives; treat demographics as necessary-but-insufficient. *—The Analogist*
  * **Weight proven stability and follow-through in mentors above shared interests.** The people we serve have experienced adults who promise and disappear; a track record of showing up when inconvenient predicts trust better than shared taste in music. *—Audience Advocate*
  * **The most predictive signal is whether a teen feels this adult sees them, not their file.** Weighting variables mathematically already loses the thing that makes mentorship work. *—The Empath*
  * **The match looks compatible on paper but fails on relational intention.** A mentor who sees the role as coaching mismatches a teen who needs a witness in the bleachers; intake should ask "What does it mean to not solve a problem?" *—The Storyteller*
  * **Match on what people are becoming, not who they are.** "Heterogeneous complementarity with aligned growth vectors" may predict realized quality better than similarity. *—The Visionary*

* **Build a tiered weighting system with explicit harm thresholds, because the highest-risk error is over-weighting the wrong variable.** Multiple personas warn the danger is not just a weak match but an actively harmful one. Over-weighting demographic similarity can invisibly sort vulnerable teens by neighborhood circumstance rather than connection. Over-weighting "mentor comfort" silently filters out cross-difference matches that could work if the mentor grew first. Over-weighting institutional records imports a deficit frame. A tiered model separates non-negotiable safety filters from high-weight predictors from nice-to-have alignment, and assigns harm weight inversely so mismatching on safety is disqualifying while mismatching on hobbies is trivial.
  * **Three tiers with inverse harm weights: safety filters, high-weight predictors, nice-to-have alignment.** Gives intake staff clear rules rather than discretionary judgment that varies by coordinator mood. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Over-weighting proximity or demographic similarity steers teens toward mentors who "look like" their circumstances rather than genuine connection.** This replicates the lived experience of being sorted by institution. *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Weighting systems trained on past matches replicate bias about which matches "succeed" (measured by mentor retention, not teen growth).** Test whether demographic similarity actually correlates with durability in your own context and adjust weights quarterly. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Separate tracks for teens with trauma histories, asking mentors concrete behavioral questions.** "How do you respond when someone won't tell you why they're upset?" surfaces capacity to not invoke authority. *—The Storyteller*

* **Elicit honest preference data through trusted, non-institutional channels and oblique conversation, because direct institutional intake produces systematically false answers.** Strong convergence: a teen referred by a system that failed them will give safe, performative answers to a clerk or a form. The shared remedy is to separate preference elicitation from the matching machinery, route it through someone the teen trusts (a peer mentor or community broker who is not program staff), and ask indirectly through story rather than survey.
  * **People answer the way they think responders want when power imbalances exist; co-design intake questions and let teens answer in groups first.** Peer validation surfaces real preferences. *—The Analogist*
  * **Preference data will be systematically false if collected through institutional channels.** Separate elicitation from matching; have a trusted community member outside the program run a low-stakes chat. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Conduct a 30-minute semi-structured interview, not a Likert scale.** "Tell me about an adult who's helped you" and transcribe with consent as the primary matching signal. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Ask indirect questions that reveal preference through description.** "Tell me about a time you felt understood by an adult" reveals what they're actually looking for. *—The Empath*
  * **A peer mentor who shares the teen's experience interprets what the teen means, not just what they said.** A justice-system peer reported the real preference: someone who won't ask too many questions but notices when she's upset. *—The Storyteller*

* **Surface the teen's testing behavior as a predictable signal and match for mentors who can absorb it.** A single-persona point worth preserving: the teens whose guards are highest will test the match with small provocations, and matching should predict which mentors can feel that test coming without taking it personally.
  * **The teens whose guards are highest test with rudeness, lateness, skepticism — needing proof the adult won't abandon them.** This requires emotional maturity more than demographic alignment. *—The Empath*

### Question 13: Matching as relationship-making, not logistics — what predicts a relationship that lasts past the one-year minimum

**Full question**: Given that the most powerful mentorships hinge on genuine connection, what matching process would reliably surface the conditions for that connection rather than just minimizing mismatch risk, and what information about a volunteer's life experience or a teen's interests and hopes is most predictive of a relationship that lasts and deepens past the one-year minimum?

* **The most predictive information is stories of vulnerability and past sustained relationships, not credentials or stated interests.** Strong convergence across five personas: durability is rooted in mutual humanity, and the way to surface it is to ask both parties to tell stories about real relationships and real failures rather than list experience. Ask mentors about a time they failed or a relationship that deepened over time; ask teens about a moment they felt understood or someone who stuck around. These narratives reveal relational style and capacity for sustained care far better than any inventory.
  * **Ask for stories of vulnerability, not credentials.** "Tell me about a time you failed at something you cared about," not "list your volunteer experience." *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Weight mentor profiles that include honest reflection on failure, not just achievement.** The turning point came when the mentor admitted dropping out and feeling like a failure. *—The Storyteller*
  * **Make "past relationships that lasted" the primary variable.** Ask what made you keep showing up; find a mentor whose relational DNA matches what the teen already knows. *—The Visionary*
  * **The mentor's willingness to be vulnerable in intake predicts durability.** Track whether sharing their own struggles correlates with month-12 retention. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Durability comes from shared respect, not shared identity; select for absence of savior energy.** Ask "What would a young person teach you?" and watch who lights up. *—The Empath*

* **Design the match as a low-stakes trial interaction that surfaces connection, rather than a one-time assignment.** Five personas converge on replacing one-shot matching with a "second date," "trial jam," or staggered relationship-first process. Let mentor and teen have one or two real, low-pressure interactions (a meal, a walk, a shared activity) before formalizing, then have each privately reflect on whether they felt they could be honest with the other. This adds friction to the timeline but catches the many paper-good matches that dissolve because the first meeting was rushed or performed.
  * **Design a "second date" conversation, not an algorithm.** Each privately answers "Did this feel like someone I could be honest with?" *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Design a "trial jam" of one or two low-stakes interactions and observe who asks questions of whom.** Jazz musicians reveal genuine fit in the first tune. *—The Analogist*
  * **Reverse the assignment direction and let teens choose from profiles.** Surfaces genuine attraction early rather than revealing mismatch after three awkward meetings. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Replace one-time intake with a staggered process; the formal match becomes official only in month three.** Based on demonstrated mutual interest, not theoretical compatibility. *—The Visionary*
  * **Some great matches begin with awkwardness and low similarity; front-load a no-shame testing period.** The assumption that good matching prevents bad relationships is partly false. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Honor the teen's right to opt out early, because psychological permission to leave is what makes them stay.** A pointed insight from the Audience Advocate: a mentee who feels they can leave without shame stays longer, because they are there by choice rather than obligation. Build explicit permission to end early into the matching frame itself.
  * **A mentee who can leave stays; one who feels trapped leaves anyway but with less hope.** Create psychological permission to end early ("We're still learning if this pairing works"). *—Audience Advocate*

* **Surface the teen's existing relational ecosystem and design the mentor to amplify it, not replace it.** A distinctive reframe from the Storyteller: rather than treating the dyad as floating free, map who already matters in the teen's life and source a mentor whose presence strengthens those bonds.
  * **Map the teen's relational ecology and find a mentor who amplifies existing supports.** For some teens the mentor helps them communicate better with their grandmother; for others, validates interests their family doesn't understand. *—The Storyteller*

### Question 14: Referral source data asymmetry and cultural-experiential distance across difference

**Full question**: Teens referred through juvenile justice come with a richer institutional record than those referred by school counselors, and congregation volunteers paired with juvenile-justice-referred teens bring assumptions about family structure, communication norms, and authority that — even when well-intentioned — can register to teens as judgment rather than curiosity. How does matching account for this referral-source asymmetry without over-relying on justice-system framing or under-informing mentors about safety context, and how does the process surface and act on teen feedback about a match that isn't working before the relationship quietly collapses?

* **Limit the institutional record shared with mentors to safety context only, and let the teen curate their own narrative.** All seven personas engage the asymmetry, and a clear majority converge on a concrete remedy: the thick juvenile-justice file primes problem-watching and registers as surveillance, while the thin school file makes a teen look like a blank slate. Give mentors trauma-informed safety context without the diagnostic record, and let the teen decide what to disclose about justice involvement and when.
  * **A court file tells you where the system noticed the teen, not who they are; source understanding from the teen.** European maps of Africa confidently mislabeled regions from fragmentary institutional knowledge. *—The Analogist*
  * **Provide safety context without the diagnostic file; let the teen narrate their own story.** The people we serve have a right to decide what to disclose. *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Tell the mentor nothing about arrest history unless the teen voluntarily discloses; let the relationship build on fresh ground.** A justice file primes problem-watching the teen feels viscerally. *—The Empath*
  * **Give the teen a sealed envelope with their file summary and let them decide whether, when, and how to share it.** The teen becomes curator of their own narrative. *—The Visionary*
  * **Create parallel intake streams that mark which information came from which source.** Ask JJ case managers about safety context and previous positive adult relationships; ask counselors about strengths. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Name the referral-source asymmetry and the mentor's cultural assumptions explicitly in preparation, framed as reality-check rather than guilt or disqualifier.** Strong convergence: the cultural gap does the most damage in silence, through a mentor's surprise or discomfort that the teen reads as "you're wrong for how you live." The shared remedy is to make assumptions discussable, asking mentors directly what they might bring and whether they can stay curious instead of defensive. A mentor who can acknowledge the gap is more ready than one who claims to "treat everyone the same."
  * **Name the asymmetry in volunteer training: treat the teen as a peer with full humanity, not validate the institution's framing.** Staff and mentors fall into the deficit pattern because it feels responsible. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Surface mentors' implicit cultural narratives about family, authority, and success; pair for flexibility.** Assumptions register not as intellectual difference but as judgment. *—The Storyteller*
  * **Ask mentors "What assumptions might you bring? How might those feel to them?" — reality, not guilt.** "I treat everyone the same" has already lost connection. *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Ask "What assumptions might you bring, and are you willing to let them be challenged?"** The ones who squirm with genuine self-awareness are ready. *—The Empath*
  * **Tell the mentor directly which referral source and what it means for how the teen experiences being matched.** Curiosity about the teen's life beats assumptions about their family. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Build a proactive teen feedback loop that treats silence as data, with check-ins run by someone outside the dyad at weeks 3-4, 6-8, and 12.** Near-universal convergence (six of seven): a struggling teen will not volunteer that the match is failing; they go quiet, attend inconsistently, then ghost, and staff mistake silence for contentment. The shared design is a private, recurring check-in conducted by a peer or trusted third party (not the coordinator and not with the mentor present) that explicitly invites the teen to say it isn't working and routes that answer to a rematch conversation, not a fix-the-volunteer conversation.
  * **A third-party check-in at weeks 4, 8, 12 asking privately "Is this working?"; treat teen silence as data requiring investigation.** Damage often goes unnoticed until collapse. *—The Analogist*
  * **A private channel for micro-discomfort before it becomes exit.** The people we serve absorb discomfort quietly; early signals matter more than formal outcome measurement. *—Audience Advocate*
  * **A week-3 check-in with a peer, not staff, that directly triggers a rematch conversation.** "Is this person getting it, or do you want to try someone else?" *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A 4-week check-in with the teen alone; normalize switching early.** Teens are conflict-avoidant with authority and show up checked-out to avoid disappointing the mentor. *—The Empath*
  * **A week-2 and week-6 check-in offering an easy exit.** Catches misalignment before it hardens into quiet resentment. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **A four-week conversation allowing rematch without stigma or explanation.** Treat silence as data; some teens disappear without drama because that is how they learned to stay safe. *—The Storyteller*

* **Reframe justice-system involvement as demonstrated institutional resilience rather than liability.** A distinctive single-persona reframe from the Visionary: these teens have navigated courts, social workers, and probation officers, and a mentor can be trained to respect that learned armor rather than try to dissolve it.
  * **Frame it as "this teen has learned to show up in formal relationships and protect themselves," not "this teen has been through the system."** Match mentors who work alongside the armor. *—The Visionary*

* **Assign a cultural broker to prepare both parties to read each other charitably.** A complementary structural idea: a coach who understands both worlds, distinct from the mentor, prepares each side for the other's communication norms and offers real-time feedback on cultural safety.
  * **Appoint a cultural broker who helps each side read the other charitably.** "Your mentor may ask direct questions; that's how her family talks, not interrogation." *—The Analogist*
  * **Connect mentors with a broker from the teen's neighborhood for real-time feedback on cultural safety.** A mentor trained in cultural humility notices withdrawal and asks "Did something shift?" *—The Storyteller*

### Question 15: What "being chosen" feels like to a teen who has rarely been chosen

**Full question**: When a mentee is matched with a volunteer, there is a moment of being singled out as worth someone's time. That moment will feel enormous to some of these kids — maybe uncomfortably so. How do we prepare mentors to hold that weight without letting the relationship become defined by pity or savior energy, which these teens will detect instantly and resent?

* **Replace savior language with specific curiosity, because teens read "I chose you because you need me" as pity.** All seven personas converge: the antidote to savior energy is to ground the mentor's interest in something specific and real about the teen rather than in the teen's deficits. Coach mentors to say "I noticed you mentioned X and I have experience with that" instead of "I want to help you reach your potential." The reframe positions the teen as expert and interesting, not broken.
  * **"I'm curious about what matters to you" positions the teen as expert; "I want to help you achieve your potential" positions them as broken.** Pity is often invisible to the mentor until the teen withdraws. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Shift from "I will rescue you" to "I saw something real about you and want to explore it."** The people we serve hear "I chose you because you need me" as pity. *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Frame mentorship as mutual benefit: "I'm interested in your thinking because you've navigated things I haven't."** "Matched" (mutual) beats "assigned" (unilateral). *—The Analogist*
  * **Have the mentor name a specific reason: "I wanted to work with you because of X" — something the teen shared in intake.** Position the mentor as someone who actually chose them. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Screen for mentors who have examined their own savior narratives.** "Tell me about a time you received help that felt genuine rather than humiliating." *—The Storyteller*

* **Prepare mentors for testing behavior, idealization, and withdrawal as evidence the relationship is becoming real, not as rejection.** Five personas converge: a teen who feels chosen may put the mentor on a pedestal, then test whether the choice is real by acting difficult, going quiet, or pushing boundaries. Mentors must be told explicitly this is a loyalty test ("Will you stay when I'm not grateful?") and that steady, non-defensive presence is how it is passed.
  * **The test is "Are you going to leave when it gets hard?"; your steady presence proves you meant it.** Prepare mentors for silence, withdrawal, and testing behavior. *—Audience Advocate*
  * **The moment the kid starts testing you is the moment you're becoming real; don't flinch.** Idealization is grief and hope meeting for the first time. *—The Empath*
  * **The teen may test whether your interest is real by pushing back; this is self-protection, not rejection.** Give mentors language: "I don't need anything from you. I'm here because I want to know you." *—The Storyteller*
  * **The teen owes nothing — not growth, gratitude, or transformation; the mentor chose to show up, that's the whole contract.** The "chosen" moment can flip into pressure to perform gratitude. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Demonstrate reliability over enthusiasm; don't show up with gifts and effusiveness.** Ask "What does someone have to do to prove they won't disappear on you?" *—The Empath*

* **Make the moment of pairing a ceremonial but genuine ritual that signals intrinsic worth, not deficit.** Several personas converge on marking the match with intentional ritual that communicates "you matter because you're worth knowing." The caution is to keep it simple and real rather than performative.
  * **A shared meal with a simple acknowledgment, not casual small talk in a church office.** Intentional enough to feel significant, simple enough to be genuine. *—Audience Advocate*
  * **A "mattering ritual" — a special dinner or field trip — before any "let's talk about your challenges" conversation.** Your mattering isn't conditioned on your deficits. *—The Visionary*

* **Watch for the moment the teen realizes they are not a project — when the mentor enjoys them without needing them to change.** A distinctive Storyteller insight: the deeper shift is the teen discovering "she likes me without needing me to be different," which is what lets them stop waiting for the catch.
  * **Look for mentors who can enjoy someone as they are, not mentors with a theory about how to develop them.** "Describe someone who likes you without trying to change you." *—The Storyteller*

### Question 16: What if the match were the program

**Full question**: Imagine a world where all the training curricula, safety protocols, and outcome metrics dissolve into a single obsessive focus: the design of the pairing itself. What would it look like to treat the matching moment as a sacred act of relational architecture — something more akin to a long-term friendship curation than an administrative intake process? How might this reframing change every downstream decision?

* **Invert the resource allocation so the pairing is the core work and everything downstream serves match durability.** Strong convergence (six personas embrace the reframe): most programs spend roughly 20% of effort on matching and 80% on curricula, training, and metrics; flip it. Once the match is solid, even loose training and protocols work; once it is weak, perfect training cannot save it. Make match strength the north-star metric and let training become relationship-specific rather than generic.
  * **Dedicate 60% of program energy to pairing; write a "match success statement" as the north star.** "90% report genuine connection by month 6; 85% continue past year one." *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Everything else serves the pairing; training focuses on this specific dyad, not generic curricula.** "How will you offer structure without replicating the authority that already failed them?" *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Matching as the core curriculum; the relationship teaches everything else.** Mentors and teens learn each other's actual language, not instruction-speak. *—The Storyteller*
  * **Design matching as three-phase relational architecture; everything flows from match quality.** Investing 80% in training and 20% in matching is a beautiful auditorium in a fragmented floor plan. *—The Analogist*
  * **Strip away modules and metrics; provide only periodic relational check-ins with a facilitator.** The mentor and teen design what their year looks like. *—The Visionary*

* **Treat matching as continuous relational care with a built-in repair protocol, not a one-time administrative event.** Several personas converge: if the match is the program, then a rough patch at month four gets intensive support (mediation, a relational-repair specialist, a brief recalibration pause) rather than being left to coast or quietly fail.
  * **Design an ongoing relational audit and a "match repair" protocol for relationships in trouble.** "We invested in you because we believed in this pairing. We don't give up at the first crack." *—Audience Advocate*
  * **A mentor and teen co-author a relational charter that all downstream training and escalation flows from.** People who design their own agreements are more likely to honor them. *—The Storyteller*

* **Adopt this reframe with deliberate guardrails, because match-obsession can mask boundary risks, neglect engagement structure, and ignore mentor burnout.** A load-bearing dissent from the Devil's Advocate that productively qualifies the convergence: a perfect match does not eliminate the need for safety protocols, conflict resolution, and burnout support; it just makes those scenarios quieter. This must be held alongside the enthusiasm.
  * **A great match can mask boundary issues for months; treat matching as one pillar and non-negotiable safeguards as another.** Perfect matches make safety scenarios quieter, not absent. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Obsessive matching can neglect engagement structure and mentor burnout.** Design the minimum structure needed for durability and an off-ramp that doesn't shame mentors who step back. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Acknowledge that this model depends on the relational sophistication of coordinators, which is its real constraint.** A candid Empath observation that names the cost of the vision: it only works if coordinators can sense fit and have the courage to say "no match for now," and that capacity is unmeasurable and hard to staff.
  * **The vision depends on emotionally sophisticated coordinators who can trust intuition and refuse forced pairings.** You can't graph sacred curation, but the relationships it produces are more durable. *—The Empath*

### Question 17: The turning-point moment

**Full question**: Every sustained mentoring relationship has a moment — maybe six weeks in, maybe six months — when the mentee decides the mentor is genuinely trustworthy; what conditions, rituals, or touchpoints does your program design specifically to help that moment arrive, and how will you know when it has?

* **Design for a mentor vulnerability moment around weeks 4-8, because trust most often turns when the teen sees the adult as fallibly human.** Strong convergence: the turning point recurs when a mentor admits a real failure or uncertainty and the teen realizes the adult is "not magical, just human and still trying." Coach mentors to share a genuine struggle (not an "I overcame it" story) in the early weeks, and to do so without centering their own need for sympathy.
  * **Design a deliberate vulnerability moment around week 4-6; it's your proxy for trustworthiness.** Can the mentor be vulnerable without centering their own need for sympathy? *—The Pragmatist*
  * **The turn comes when the teen realizes "this person is not magical, they're just human and they keep trying."** Coach a real struggle, not a success story, in weeks four to six. *—The Storyteller*
  * **A moment where the mentor admits a mistake accelerates the crossing.** The hero's-journey threshold, usually weeks 4-8. *—The Analogist*
  * **Trust arrives through unscripted moments — the mentor admits a mistake, shows up when doubted.** Design conditions that don't prevent it rather than scripting the moment itself. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **The mentor admitting they don't know something shows the relationship isn't about being expert.** Trust emerges in the small and specific, not the grand gesture. *—The Empath*

* **Treat consistent, unremarkable reliability as the primary trust-builder and watch for the teen to stop performing.** Several personas converge that the turn is less a dramatic event than the accumulation of predictable presence, and the visible marker is the teen dropping politeness for realness — disagreeing, admitting they didn't do something, initiating contact.
  * **Monotonous reliability builds trust; the moment arrives when the teen stops performing.** Watch the shift from "good mentee" to expressing anger or disagreement; that's arrival, not breakdown. *—The Empath*
  * **The turning point shows when the teen starts initiating, suggesting, or asking the mentor about their life.** When a teen asks you a real question, answer honestly: "I trust you to know me too." *—Audience Advocate*
  * **Embed opportunities for the teen to initiate — to suggest, pay, choose — and gently mark it when they do.** Buying the coffee marked "I'm investing in this too." *—The Storyteller*

* **Resist codifying a universal timeline; the turning point is idiosyncratic, and false urgency blocks it.** A load-bearing caution from the Devil's Advocate (echoed by the Empath): if staff treat "trust by month two" as a rule, they intervene when a pair is behind schedule, and that intervention reads as surveillance. Train mentors that there is no universal timeline and the job is reliable presence.
  * **The six-week timeline is an average, not a promise; codifying it creates false urgency that blocks trust.** Staff noticing a pair isn't at trust by month four start intervening, which feels like surveillance. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **The turning point is different for every teen; notice it emotionally, not administratively.** Mentors really paying attention will feel the shift in how the teen greets them. *—The Empath*

* **Use the turning point as a collaborative achievement — a shared problem solved together — rather than only a disclosure.** A distinctive Storyteller pattern: trust can turn through pragmatic agency, when mentor and teen navigate a real fear or conflict together and the teen survives it as an ally relationship.
  * **The turn comes faster through collaborative agency on a shared problem.** Walking the teen through a feared teacher conversation as an ally in difficulty, not a dispenser of wisdom. *—The Storyteller*

* **Mark the turn with a tangible ritual or artifact when it arrives, and surface it through reflective check-ins rather than progress metrics.** Convergence on naming the shift: a week-6 reflection asking "Has anything surprised you about this person?" detects approach, and a small concrete ritual (a meal, a planted tree, a recorded message) anchors the moment.
  * **A week-6 "fidelity checkpoint" asking "Has anything surprised you about this person?"** Real-time relational care, not progress-on-goals metrics. *—The Visionary*
  * **A ritual at week 8-10 surfaces whether the turn has arrived.** "I like how this is going. What about you?" *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Design a "turning point ritual" for week 6 or 8 that names the shift.** "We're past getting-to-know-you; now we're figuring out how to think together." *—The Analogist*

### Question 18: Matching architecture from labor markets

**Full question**: Two-sided matching markets (residency match programs, school choice lotteries, organ transplant registries) have been extensively studied. The core insight is that stable matches require surfacing preferences from both sides and designing an algorithm that accounts for mutual fit — not just one-sided assignment. Most mentorship programs assign based on coordinator judgment or simple demographic similarity. What structured information — about mentor interests, availability, communication style, and life experience, and about teen goals, interests, and counselor observations — would allow a more systematic match process? And critically, how do you elicit honest preference data from teens who may distrust the institutions making the referral?

* **Adopt the two-sided mutual-preference structure from the residency match: collect ranked preferences from both sides and prioritize pairings where both ranked each other highly, leaving gaps rather than forcing mismatches.** Strong convergence: the load-bearing insight from labor-market matching is mutual preference, not algorithmic complexity. At a 40-volunteer, 15-30-teen scale, this means a simplified ranking where both sides express top choices, raising buy-in and quality even if the matching logic is basic.
  * **Both sides submit ranked lists; propose pairings where both ranked each other highly.** Limits flexibility but dramatically improves match quality and buy-in. *—The Analogist*
  * **Ask each mentor to rank top 3 teens and each teen top 3 mentors; leave gaps if mutual preference is low rather than forcing pairs.** You don't need the computational complexity, but you need the information. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Both teen and mentor submit ranked choices so no pair both prefers a different match.** Creates stability so neither is quietly wishing they'd been paired differently. *—The Storyteller*
  * **A human curator reads both narratives and recommends; both parties can accept, propose an alternative, or pass.** Honors mutual preference without reducing humans to data points. *—The Visionary*
  * **Even a basic algorithm improves outcomes because expressed preference creates investment.** 70% from preference alignment, 30% from coordinator judgment likely beats either alone. *—The Analogist*

* **Elicit teen preference data obliquely and through trusted intermediaries, because economic models assume stable, honest preferences that justice-distrusting teens will not give institutional channels.** Strong convergence reinforcing Question 12: the two-sided model's stated weakness is that teen preferences are volatile, strategic, and shaped by what feels safe to say. Layer formal surveys with informal relationship-building, route elicitation through a peer or community member, and ask about other people rather than about the self.
  * **Combine formal surveys with informal conversations run by a non-staff peer or community member.** You won't get full honesty, but layering methods helps; preferences are volatile, so build in rematch. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Elicit preference through a trusted interviewer, conversationally.** "Who do you admire? If you could pick an adult to hang out with, what would they be like?" *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Have teens talk about other people first.** "What qualities do you notice in adults you respect?" reveals values without self-exposure. *—The Visionary*
  * **A teen says "someone who understands growing up poor" but means "someone who won't patronize me."** Elicited preferences need to go deeper than the obvious. *—The Empath*
  * **Preference data emerges in informal conversations with a peer mentor who interprets meaning.** Build in a translation layer between what the teen said and what they mean. *—The Storyteller*

* **Collect mentor preference data that explicitly probes openness to difference and reliability, and audit for the bias baked into stated preferences.** Convergence on a structural caution: mentors asked what they want will name comfort and similarity, and unaudited preference matching reproduces demographic sorting (white mentors with white teens, degreed mentors with high-achievers). Ask mentors to describe appreciating someone very different, probe follow-through, and run a diversity-gap check on the resulting matches.
  * **Mentor preference data reflects unconscious bias toward comfort; run a "diversity gap analysis" and recalibrate toward cross-difference pairings.** They won't spontaneously say "I need to be challenged by someone I don't understand." *—The Devil's Advocate*
