# Brainstorming Questions: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Vision, Purpose & Strengths-Based Foundations

**Cluster focus**: What this program is fundamentally for, what excellence looks like, and which existing community assets it can mobilize before any policy is written.

1. **Peak-moment trajectory shift**: Think about the most powerful youth mentorship story you've encountered — what was it about that single relationship that made the difference, and what conditions in the program made that relationship possible? Which conditions can be deliberately reproduced and which were accidents of fit?
2. **The congregation as irreplaceable lifeline and asset base**: This organization has sustained a 20-year presence in the neighborhood — what relational practices, cultural knowledge, and community trust already exist here that no outside program could replicate, and which of those assets (existing relationships, congregation networks, school-counselor knowledge, the foundation grant itself) can the program mobilize immediately, before a single formal policy is written?
3. **Start with five pairs as discovery data**: Rather than designing the whole program in advance, what would it look like to begin with a small cohort of three to five carefully supported pairs, treat their experience as discovery data, and let what works in those relationships shape the program design for everyone who follows?
4. **The 2029 model-program vision**: Imagine it's three years from now and delegations from other cities are visiting to learn from this program. What do they notice first when they walk in the door, what do they hear teens and mentors saying to each other, what metrics are on the wall, and what stories are being shared?
5. **Co-inquiry instead of guidance**: What if we completely reimagined the mentor-mentee relationship so that it isn't a structured adult-guiding-youth dynamic at all — but rather a genuine co-inquiry where both parties explore meaning together, and the power gradient simply doesn't exist in the way we currently assume it must?
6. **A congregation as a new kind of civic institution**: Imagine a world where a faith-based organization with a 20-year neighborhood presence becomes the prototype for an entirely new category of institution — not a church, not a nonprofit, not a social service agency, but something that doesn't have a name yet. What capabilities does this organization already have that no traditional youth-serving institution possesses, and how could a mentorship program be the seed of that new category?

## Topic Cluster 02: Volunteer Recruitment, Screening & Background Checks

**Cluster focus**: Designing a defensible volunteer pipeline that meets statutory background-check floors, screens for value alignment, and survives predictable attrition.

1. **Disqualification tiering and borderline cases**: What framework should govern which background-check findings are automatic disqualifiers, which require case-by-case review, and which are irrelevant to working with at-risk teens — incorporating any state-mandated absolute bars (e.g., registered-sex-offender status) and discretionary review categories so the screening policy meets the legal floor without exposing the organization to discrimination claims, and how should borderline cases like old non-violent offenses or expunged records be handled, with whom holding decision authority at each tier? [User Q]
2. **Screening-funnel attrition and the launch gap**: Breaking down the volunteer intake process into its component stages — expression of interest, application, background check, interview, training, matching — at what rate do you expect candidates to drop out at each stage, and if half or more of the 40 "interested adults" don't complete the formal commitment process, how does the program avoid launching with mismatched pair counts that strand enrolled teens?
3. **Renewal cadence, off-cycle re-screening, and value-alignment screening**: What is the right interval for re-running background checks on active volunteers, and what events (arrests, address changes, role changes) should trigger an off-cycle review — and beyond criminal-record screening, what process surfaces the misalignment between a congregation member's worldview and a teen's lived reality before it becomes a failed match, supporting the standard of care needed to manage negligent-selection-and-retention exposure?
4. **Volunteer rhythm problem across institutional cycles**: Faith congregations, schools, and juvenile justice systems each operate on different annual rhythms (liturgical calendar, academic year, fiscal/court cycles) — how might the program design its volunteer onboarding and renewal cadence to account for the fact that mentor availability and organizational attention will peak and trough on different schedules depending on which institution a volunteer comes from?

## Topic Cluster 03: Mentor Training, Mandatory-Reporter Competency & Ongoing Support

**Cluster focus**: Building mentor competency that is internalized rather than checked-off, and sustaining mentors so they don't burn out, drop out, or cause harm.

1. **Mandatory-reporter competency, internalized vs. box-checked**: Mandatory reporter obligations vary in specificity — what are the distinct knowledge components (recognition of abuse indicators, reporting thresholds, chain of notification, documentation requirements) that a mentor must demonstrate competency in before being cleared, how is that competency verified rather than assumed from attendance, and how do we structure the training so mentors actually internalize and act on it rather than just checking a box during onboarding? [User Q]
2. **Concern escalation when below the mandatory-report line**: How should a mentor raise a concern about a mentee when they're unsure whether it rises to the level of a mandatory report — and what formal incident-documentation protocol (initial disclosure, chain of report notification, regulatory filing timelines, record retention) ensures mandatory reporting obligations are met without creating conflicting instructions between program staff, church leadership, and external agency mandates? [User Q]
3. **Training as a gift, not a compliance burden**: When volunteer training programs work brilliantly — when people leave more energized and capable than when they arrived — what is happening in the room, and how might we design mandatory-reporter training, boundary-setting, and matching orientation so that mentors experience it as a gift rather than a compliance burden?
4. **Reciprocal protection and burnout prevention in the first six months**: What ongoing support and check-in cadence do mentors need to prevent burnout, especially during the first six months of a match — and what does a reciprocal protection framework look like that protects the mentor from secondary trauma and ambiguous situations, not just the organization and the teen? [User Q]
5. **Mandatory reporting as a shared protocol layer**: Air traffic control works across dozens of airlines and airports through a single shared protocol layer that all parties implement independently — what would it look like to design mandatory reporting obligations and escalation procedures as a shared protocol that the faith org, school, and juvenile justice system each implement in their own language, rather than training mentors to navigate each institution's separate reporting chain?
6. **Assume the 40 volunteers are the problem**: What if you started from the premise that well-meaning adult volunteers in high-poverty communities cause more harm than good — through pity, savior dynamics, inconsistency, and premature dropout — and designed the entire mentor training program to actively counteract the damage motivated volunteers naturally want to do? What would "don't be helpful in the ways you want to be helpful" look like as a curriculum?
7. **The test of a hard week**: Walk through the moment six months in when a teen ghosts two scheduled meetings after a crisis at home — what structures, relationships, and rituals does the program have in place so the mentor knows what to do, feels supported rather than abandoned, and the teen has a clear path back without shame?
8. **The moment trust breaks**: Imagine a mentor who, trying to be helpful, shares something a teen told her in confidence with the teen's parent — and the teen finds out — what policies, training moments, and repair conversations does the program have ready so this breach doesn't end the relationship and teaches both people something true about trust?

## Topic Cluster 04: Matching Logic & Relationship Dynamics

**Cluster focus**: How pairs are formed, what predicts durable bonds, and how to detect — and intervene in — weakening matches before they collapse.

1. **Matching variable weighting, the relationship-as-outcome assumption, and one-size-fit**: What are the three to five variables — demographic similarity, shared interests, personality fit, geographic proximity, gender, cultural background, referral source, or something less obvious — that carry the most predictive weight for relationship longevity, and behind that question: why do we assume the relationship itself is the primary outcome that moves the needle (rather than evidence-based mechanisms like skill transfer, advocacy, or network access), and that a single one-on-one weekly meeting model is right for every teen — rather than a flexible model that adapts to each teen's stability, school schedule, and family situation? [User Q]
2. **Match quality as a compounding factor with early-warning signals**: When a match is strong, the teen shows up, the mentor stays engaged, and both sides invest more — a self-amplifying pattern; when a match is weak, disengagement feeds more disengagement. What measurable intermediate checkpoints — at 30, 60, 90 days — distinguish a relationship on track from one at risk of early termination, and what intervention protocol activates at each checkpoint before the dynamic becomes irreversible?
3. **Congregation homogeneity and cross-cultural preparation**: A faith-based volunteer pool may skew toward a particular demographic (age, race, class, worldview) that doesn't align with teens referred through juvenile justice — how will the program recruit beyond the congregation, and when mentors and mentees come from different racial, class, or cultural backgrounds, how does the program prepare both parties to navigate that difference with care rather than papering over it?
4. **Matching as ecological fit, not resume alignment**: Conservation biologists match reintroduced species to habitats based on environmental conditions, not just genetic similarity — there's a structural parallel to mentor-mentee matching here: what ecological conditions (neighborhood proximity, shared commute, availability patterns, cultural context) might predict relationship durability better than surface-level interest alignment alone?
5. **Flip the referral**: What if the teens chose and vetted the mentors — not the other way around? If a 15-year-old from the juvenile justice system had full veto power over who entered their life, what would the selection criteria actually look like, and what does that reveal about whose comfort the current process is really designed to protect?
6. **The quiet Tuesday that changes everything**: What kinds of ordinary, low-stakes moments — a shared meal, a car ride, watching someone fix something — does the program design space for, and how do we build a program structure that trusts these unscripted interactions rather than filling every hour with agenda?

## Topic Cluster 05: Consent, Referral Integrity & Juvenile-Justice Privacy

**Cluster focus**: Lawful, informed entry pathways into the program — and protecting minors' records, especially those carrying juvenile-justice histories.

1. **Layered consent with contested guardianship and juvenile-justice power dynamics**: Given that participants are minors ages 13–17, what layered consent framework — distinguishing between initial program enrollment consent, photo/media release, data collection authorization, and ongoing participation acknowledgment — must the organization obtain from parents or legal guardians, what happens when guardianship is contested, shared, or held by the state through juvenile justice involvement, and how do we obtain genuinely informed consent from teens referred through juvenile justice given the power dynamics and the privacy stakes of their records (recognizing that requiring parental consent may inadvertently exclude the most at-risk youth from households with the least parental capacity)? [User Q]
2. **Juvenile-record confidentiality and data minimization**: When teens are referred through juvenile justice channels, what restrictions do state juvenile-record confidentiality statutes place on what information school counselors and juvenile justice officers may legally share with program staff, what is the minimum data the program must collect to operate effectively, and how should retention policies be structured so that sensitive information doesn't migrate outside its lawful disclosure boundary or create a private record that outlasts the legal protections governing the official one?
3. **Referral source diversity, institutional load-shifting, counselor incentives, and a shared "at-risk" standard**: If the program relies primarily on school counselors and juvenile justice referrals, what feedback loops emerge — does early success with one channel make staff increasingly dependent on it, gradually narrowing who the program reaches, and as the program proves effective, what prevents referring institutions from increasing volume faster than capacity can absorb (institutional load-shifting that quietly degrades quality)? Will counselors and probation officers refer the teens they most want to help, the teens easiest to manage, or the teens they most want to pass off — and given the two referring systems apply different thresholds of risk (schools' chronic absenteeism vs. juvenile justice's prior involvement), how will the program define and document a unified eligibility standard with clear authority over its application?
4. **Cross-system intake as a warm handoff**: School counselors and juvenile justice coordinators each have distinct relational authority with a teen — how might the program design its intake process to borrow from the "warm handoff" model used in healthcare transitions, where the referring professional personally introduces the mentee to the program rather than passing paperwork alone?
5. **Referral as a trust-transfer mechanism**: In franchise models, brand trust transfers from the franchisor to a new location the moment a customer walks in; there's a structural parallel here with referral — how might the program design the mentor-matching step so that the trust a teen has in their school counselor or parole officer actually transfers forward into their first interaction with their mentor?

## Topic Cluster 06: Safety Architecture, Meeting-Location Policy & Insurance

**Cluster focus**: Designing legally defensible safety infrastructure for one-on-one work with minors without making mentorship feel like supervised visitation.

1. **One-on-one meeting-location risk profile and the public-vs-private question**: The one-on-one model with minors creates a distinct liability surface — what is the matrix of acceptable meeting settings (public vs. private, observed vs. unobserved), which combinations are categorically prohibited regardless of mentor tenure or relationship quality, and are there specific circumstances where private or home settings are appropriate and defensible, recognizing that overly prescriptive rules may prevent the natural trust-building interactions that make mentorship effective? [User Q]
2. **Insurance coverage architecture for a small program**: What level of liability insurance is appropriate for a program of our size — what combination of general liability, directors-and-officers, volunteer accident, and sexual-abuse-and-molestation (SAM) coverage does a one-on-one youth mentorship program require, is the church's existing liability policy likely to extend to program volunteers and off-site mentoring activities, and should we carry a separate sexual misconduct policy in addition to general liability before the first mentor-mentee meeting occurs? [User Q]
3. **Safety-as-care and the threshold where safeguards become burdens**: Rather than safety protocols that feel like surveillance, what would a program look like where every safeguard — background checks, meeting-location policies, mandatory-reporter training — was designed so that teens, mentors, and families all felt genuinely held and trusted? Where is the threshold at which safeguards shift from reassuring to burdensome, and how does the program design training so that mandatory reporting obligations don't suppress the very openness the program is trying to build?
4. **Imagine the safeguards cause the harm**: What if the mandatory reporting obligations, background checks, and liability waivers — designed to protect teens — are the primary reason at-risk teens distrust adults and disengage from the program? What if the program was redesigned from the assumption that bureaucratic safety infrastructure is the thing making the relationships feel unsafe? Which protection mechanisms signal institutional distrust to the people they're meant to protect?
5. **Inventing a new kind of safety**: What if "safety" in a youth mentorship program didn't mean protocols, waivers, and rules — but was instead designed from scratch as an emergent property of the relationships themselves? What would a program look like where safety arises from trust architecture rather than legal architecture?

## Topic Cluster 07: Teen Voice, Family Engagement & Cultural Inclusion

**Cluster focus**: Designing the program from the teen's and family's lived experience — cultural fit, dignity, logistical reality, and the program's faith identity.

1. **The arrival moment and the dignity of being matched**: What does a teen experience in the first 15 minutes of their first mentor meeting — what are they thinking, feeling, and silently assessing — and what does the matching process feel like from the teen's perspective: are they an object being assigned, or a person being heard? What would it mean to design intake as an experience that itself communicates respect?
2. **Parent partnership when home is complicated and communication that doesn't backfire**: Beyond obtaining consent signatures, how might parents and guardians be meaningfully included as participants in the program's success, given barriers like work schedules, language, and distrust of institutions — and for teens whose home environment is part of what makes them "at-risk" (unstable housing, incarcerated parents, family conflict), how does program design account for the reality that parental involvement may be fraught, while structuring guardian communication so it builds trust rather than creating a surveillance dynamic that causes teens to shut down?
3. **Cultural-linguistic fit, the stigma of "at-risk," and faith neutrality**: From the perspective of teens from communities that may historically distrust faith-based institutions, authority figures, or the juvenile justice system — what signals in the program's design, language, and environment either invite them in or quietly tell them this isn't for them; how do teens experience the label that got them into this program; and without explicit policy clarity on religious neutrality in program delivery, does the organization's faith identity (an asset for congregation trust) function as a deterrent for secular families, LGBTQ+ teens, or families from different religious traditions, potentially raising civil rights questions about publicly-referred participants placed in a faith-directed program?
4. **The invisible logistics problem**: What practical barriers — bus routes, after-school jobs, childcare for younger siblings, lack of a phone — might cause a teen to miss meetings without ever explaining why, and how does the program design catch and address these before they read as "disengagement"?
5. **The crossing-the-threshold moment**: Imagine a 15-year-old, referred by her school counselor, walking into her first meeting with a mentor she has never met — what does that room look like, who is in it, and what happens in the first ten minutes that makes her decide whether this is worth her time?
6. **Strip the church entirely**: Imagine the program existed with exactly the same teens, mentors, and funding — but with no faith-based organizational home. What would change? What would the teens who were referred through juvenile justice gain or lose? Which elements of the faith-based context are genuine assets versus implicit barriers to entry for specific youth?

## Topic Cluster 08: Outcome Measurement & Learning Loops

**Cluster focus**: Measuring what matters — to teens, mentors, funders, and the program itself — without turning measurement into surveillance or optimizing for the wrong proxies.

1. **Outcome variable selection, baseline acquisition, and the teens' own definitions**: Given the program's goals, what are the two or three quantifiable proxies — school attendance delta, disciplinary incident rate, mentor-reported relationship quality score — that are both meaningful and obtainable without burdening participants or requiring data-sharing agreements the organization cannot easily execute, how do we measure outcomes without making mentors feel surveilled or teens feel over-surveyed, what is the realistic pre-program baseline-collection window (with which indicators sourced from school counselors or juvenile justice referrers rather than self-report), and how does the measurement framework include a 15-year-old's definition of "a good year with a mentor" alongside the funder's metrics — treating data collection as something done with participants rather than to them? [User Q]
2. **Attrition exit data as a feedback signal**: When a match ends before the one-year commitment, what structured exit data — reason category, duration, initiating party, antecedent events — should be collected, and how does that data feed back into refining the matching process and identifying systemic program weaknesses?
3. **Short-cycle versus long-cycle feedback and the measurement-burden chilling effect**: Many meaningful outcomes — educational attainment, justice system involvement, economic stability — manifest over years, while surveys, check-ins, and reporting requirements add friction for both mentors and teens. At what point does the measurement apparatus start degrading the participation it's trying to study, and what shorter-cycle signals function as reliable proxies for long-term outcomes without optimizing for proxies in ways that miss the deeper changes the program actually cares about?
4. **Measure failure, not success**: What if instead of tracking program outcomes — attendance, graduation rates, "relationship quality" — you tracked every moment a mentor or teen wanted to quit but didn't? What would that data tell you that success metrics never could? Resilience and durability in relationships are invisible to outcome measurement but may be the actual product being built.
5. **What if outcomes were designed to be felt, not counted**: What if we completely abandoned the standard outcome measurement frameworks — attendance, grades, recidivism — and designed a program whose primary evidence of success was qualitative transformation visible to the teens themselves and the community around them? What entirely new forms of witnessing and documentation would we need to invent?

## Topic Cluster 09: Sustainability, Funding & Institutional Continuity

**Cluster focus**: Whether the financial and operational architecture can survive the first year, scale predictably, and absorb the loss of any single staff member or grant.

1. **Cost-per-match unit economics, the $15K gap, and behavioral lock-in**: With $15K in seed funding plus in-kind support, what does the estimated cost per active mentor-mentee pair look like across the first year — broken down into screening, training, coordination, and overhead — at what funding level does the program become self-sustaining versus dependent on grant renewal, and which organizational behaviors become load-bearing on that grant such that they collapse if it isn't renewed (behavioral lock-in), versus which the organization has built capacity to sustain independently? If $15K falls short at month six, what contingency keeps already-matched pairs intact rather than abruptly terminating relationships?
2. **Single-coordinator single-point-of-failure and compliance-by-design**: If the administrative coordinator who manages consent records, match files, and mandatory-report logs becomes unavailable, does the program have documented procedures and backup access to continue without compliance exposure — and rather than treating legal compliance as a checklist to complete before launch, what standing governance mechanisms (designated compliance officer, periodic mandatory-reporter refreshers tied to re-enrollment, formal policy review cycle, board-level reporting) should be built into the operating structure from inception so legal obligations evolve with the program?
3. **Caseload supervisor ratio and the mentor-attrition reputation feedback**: How many active mentor-mentee pairs can a single staff coordinator effectively supervise while maintaining quality check-ins, responding to escalations, and supporting struggling mentors — what is the staffing implication if the program scales beyond 40 pairs, and if mentor burnout causes visible departures in the first cohort, how does word-of-mouth from burned-out mentors reshape the volunteer pipeline and reduce future recruitment?

## Topic Cluster 10: Endings, Continuity & What Comes Next

**Cluster focus**: How matches end well — at the one-year mark, when a pairing isn't working, or when a teen ages out — and what carries forward into the community.

1. **The one-year commitment cliff and phased commitment alternatives**: What happens at the one-year mark when teens and mentors who've built real relationships face an abrupt structural ending — what does the off-ramp look like, what signals tell us a relationship should continue versus conclude, and is there a phased commitment structure (rather than the binary one-year minimum) that preserves quality without filtering out volunteers who might thrive with a shorter initial trial? Relatedly, when a mentor must withdraw at month four, is the program prepared to absorb that rupture without re-traumatizing youth who may already have unstable adult relationships?
2. **Match-termination protocol when a pairing isn't working**: What's the right protocol when a mentor-mentee pairing isn't working, and how do we transition without causing harm to the teen — particularly given that from the teen's perspective, requesting a change can feel like another failure on a long list, so does the program make it genuinely safe and easy for a teen to say so? [User Q]
3. **Cross-system coherence when the adults disagree**: What if a teen's school counselor, juvenile justice case manager, parent or guardian, and mentor are all working from different frameworks and giving conflicting guidance — who resolves that, and is the program designed to handle it?
4. **The graduation that doesn't feel like abandonment**: Picture a teen who has just turned 18, aged out of the program, and whose mentor is the most consistent adult relationship in her life — what does the program's closing chapter look like so that this ending feels like a launching rather than another loss, and what, if anything, carries forward?
5. **The mentorship program as a community memory project**: What if the program was designed not just to support individual teens but to create a living archive of neighborhood wisdom, story, and identity — where each mentor-mentee pair becomes a vessel for intergenerational knowledge transfer that the community has never had a structure for before? How would that purpose reshape every element, from matching to meetings to what happens after the formal year ends?
