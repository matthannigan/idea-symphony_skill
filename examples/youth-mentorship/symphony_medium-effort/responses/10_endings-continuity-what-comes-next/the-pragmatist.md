# Endings, Continuity & What Comes Next - The Pragmatist

---

## The One-Year Commitment Cliff And Phased Commitment Alternatives

* **Tiered renewal with clear milestones.** Rather than binary one-year or nothing, build in three-month check-ins at months 3, 6, and 9. At each point, both mentor and mentee rate the relationship (comfort, progress, obstacles). Those hitting strong markers at month 9 renew for another six months; others complete at month 12 with dignity. This reduces volunteer burnout from open-ended commitment while signaling to teens that relationships can end *well*. Cost: one structured review form and mentor training on having hard conversations. Sustainability: pairs that renew twice are likely anchored for years without feeling trapped.

* **Trial-period entry point for cautious mentors.** Offer a three-month pilot with explicit framing: "Try it, learn the role, then commit to the year or part ways." This pulls in working professionals and skeptical volunteers who wouldn't risk 12 months cold. You'll lose some at month 3, but you'll also keep people who became confident and invested. Requirement: matching process must be equally rigorous during the trial to protect the teen from false starts. Buildable with existing volunteer pool; no new infrastructure needed.

* **Structured alumni pathway post-12 months.** At month 12, don't say goodbye. Offer low-touch options: monthly coffee meetups (mentee can attend or skip, no pressure), annual "graduation ceremony" honoring the relationship, and a simple contact mechanism (mentee keeps mentor's number, can reach out on birthdays or crises). Document outcomes so the mentee sees the tangible impact of that year. This respects the firm boundary while acknowledging that the relationship doesn't disappear. Requires one staff person to coordinate alumni events; minimal budget for refreshments and venue.

* **Emergency mentor-withdrawal protocol to minimize rupture.** When a mentor must leave mid-stream (job loss, relocation, crisis), have a *prepared* process: (1) mentor gives 2-4 week notice; (2) staff mentors the mentee through the transition, not the original mentor; (3) program offers "closure session" with departing mentor, overseen by staff, so the teen has agency; (4) quick re-match option for youth who want continuity, or sabbatical for those who need breathing room. Document the mentee's reflections from the year so nothing is lost. This takes planning time upfront but saves traumatic exits later.

## Match-Termination Protocol When A Pairing Isn't Working

* **Anonymous concerns hotline for teens.** Create a dedicated phone line or text number mentees can contact without naming themselves. They report concerns ("My mentor keeps canceling," "We don't connect,") and a staff member calls back to listen and explore options. Framing: "This line helps us make mentoring work better for you." Removes the fear of "saying no" to a trusted adult. Follow-up: staff member never forces a switch but offers genuine choice and explains what a successful pivot looks like (new mentor, program exit, reduced frequency). Cost: one part-time staff person to manage the line and train mentors on receiving feedback.

* **Mentor-side check-in at month 3 and 6.** Flip the script: ask mentors directly, "Is this working? Do you feel connected?" Normalize that not every pairing clicks, and that's *data*, not failure. Create a confidential space for mentors to flag struggles without fear of being labeled bad mentors. Offer re-matching as a straightforward option: "We'll pair you with someone whose style aligns better." This catches problems early, before the teen internalizes rejection. Reduces the likelihood of a mentee having to ask for a change because the mentor exits quietly instead.

* **Soft re-match option, not a hard end.** When a pairing isn't working, don't frame it as termination. Say: "We're going to introduce you to a mentor who specializes in [interest/background the mentee has]." If the new pairing doesn't work either, *then* have a conversation about whether program participation is right now, but give two genuine chances. Requires flexible matching criteria and willingness to have multiple mentors "dating" a mentee, but it preserves dignity and hope. Operationally: keep a waitlist of mentors available for immediate re-match.

## Cross-System Coherence When The Adults Disagree

* **Designated program coordinator as single point of contact.** Establish one staff role whose job is to *synthesize* guidance from school, juvenile justice, parents, and mentors. This person attends or calls case conferences, listens to conflicting guidance, and clarifies: "Here's what we heard, here's what the mentorship program will prioritize." They own the decision, not the mentor. The mentor knows they have a backstop; the other systems know someone is listening. Prevents mentors from being caught between incompatible directives. Budget: one full-time case manager. Payoff: fewer mentors quit, fewer teens get whiplash from conflicting adult advice.

* **Written care plan signed by all stakeholders.** At enrollment, gather school counselor, JJ case manager (if applicable), parent, and mentor for a 45-minute meeting. Agree on three to five concrete goals for the mentee and how each adult will reinforce them. Write it down, sign it, distribute copies. Revisit every six months. Resolves disagreements *at the front end* rather than when the teen is caught in the crossfire. Creates accountability: if a counselor or case manager isn't present at the planning meeting, they don't get a vote on the teen's path mid-program. Requires skilled facilitation but prevents paralysis.

* **Clear escalation chain for real-time conflict.** If a mentor and parent disagree on what the teen should do (e.g., mentor says "take the summer job offer"; parent says "focus on school first"), the mentor does *not* decide. Instead, they report the conflict to the program coordinator, who calls a quick (30-minute) three-way with the parent and mentor to decide. Document the decision and why. This feels slower but moves faster than the teen receiving mixed signals for weeks. Requires trained coordinators and a willingness to sometimes side with the system *outside* the program, which is hard but necessary.

## The Graduation That Doesn't Feel Like Abandonment

* **18-birthday celebration and structured closure ritual.** When a mentee ages out, don't let it slide into silence. Host a small ceremony (with mentor, family, program staff) where the mentee is publicly recognized for the year they completed together. Mentor gives a written letter to the mentee reflecting on specific growth they witnessed. Program presents a "completion certificate" or small token (nothing expensive—symbolic). Frames the exit as *achievement* rather than rejection. Cost: staff time and refreshments; payoff: mentee carries a tangible sense of completion, not abandonment. This ritual takes two hours but anchors the experience in dignity.

* **Transitional mentoring role for mentee-mentors.** At 18, offer a voluntary "peer mentor" track: the young adult can mentor a younger teen in the program (usually age 13-15), with structured training and oversight from staff. Doesn't obligate them, but offers a role in the community that honors the relationship they built. Some will jump at it; others will politely decline and move on—both are fine. Keeps the program alive in their life without dependency. Requires training curriculum, but it solves two problems: you get trusted peer mentors, and mentees don't feel discarded.

* **"Open-door" alumni status with clear boundaries.** Tell aging-out mentees: "You can reach out to your mentor on your birthday, to ask for advice on a big decision, or to let us know how you're doing—but not weekly or for ongoing support. Your mentor moves on to new mentees, and you're building your own adult life." Document a few *specific* milestones to re-connect around (high school graduation, 21st birthday, college applications). This isn't closure; it's a gentler reconfiguration. Requires mentors to be comfortable with this, which means naming it explicitly in training: "The goal is for this teen to outgrow their need for you, and that's success."

* **One-year post-program check-in with staff.** Three months after aging out, a program staffer (not the mentor) reaches out: "How are you landing? What's different from when you started? Do you want to stay in touch with [mentee's name]?" This acknowledges the transition, gathers outcome data, and gives the mentee a chance to ask permission to contact their mentor. Some mentors and mentees will stay loosely connected; others will drift. Neither is failure. Builds longitudinal data on program impact without burdening individual relationships.

## The Mentorship Program As A Community Memory Project

* **Structured story-capture protocol as program infrastructure.** Build in a simple practice: each mentor-mentee pair records one short audio or video reflection per quarter (five to ten minutes) on something the mentee learned, a challenge they overcame, or an adult skill they observed. The program keeps this archive and, at program completion, gives the mentee a digital or physical copy. Over five years, you'll have a community archive of 40+ teens' voices and growth stories—becomes a resource for recruiting future mentors ("Listen to what happened for past participants"). Cost: one person to manage storage and handoff; one hour per pair per year in facilitation. Payoff: mentees feel their story was *worth recording*, mentors get perspective on their impact, organization has evidence of community change.

* **Intergenerational asset-mapping built into matching.** When recruiting mentors, capture their specific skills and wisdom: "Are you a parent, a tradesperson, a therapist, an immigrant with a story about resilience?" Use this to match with teens who need exactly that perspective. Frame the program internally as "connecting neighborhood expertise with teen growth." At year-end, compile a simple "who taught what" reflection with the mentee. Over time, this makes visible that the neighborhood itself is the curriculum, and the program is just the structure that makes the transfer intentional. Requires thoughtful matching and reflection; shifts the narrative from "mentors rescue teens" to "mentors share what they know."

* **Closing-year project that mentees lead.** In month 11-12, instead of just winding down, give the mentee a project: create something with their mentor that reflects what they learned (a photo essay, a list of advice for next year's mentees, a short video on a skill they mastered, a letter to their younger self). Display these in the church or community center. This turns the ending into a *contribution*; the mentee is now a resource for the program and the community. Also gives mentors and mentees a shared creative task in the final months, deepening the bond rather than letting it fade. Requires light facilitation but gives the mentee agency in how their story ends.
