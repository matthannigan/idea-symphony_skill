# Topic Cluster 08: Outcome Measurement & Learning Loops - The Pragmatist

---

## Outcome Variable Selection, Baseline Acquisition, And Teens' Own Definitions

* **Start with school data, not surveys.** Ask the schools to pull baseline attendance, disciplinary incidents, and course grades for your 30-40 incoming teens before enrollment—this costs nothing, creates no new burden, and gives you clean comparison points. Pair this with a single, 10-minute structured conversation with each teen at month 2: "What would a good year with your mentor look like?" Record themes (someone to call when stressed, help with a specific class, just showing up consistently), and use those same themes as your outcome proxy. Funder gets metrics; teens get heard.

* **Minimize mentor reporting burden.** A pragmatic approach might be: mentors complete one 5-minute check-in at months 3, 6, and 12 that asks only "On a scale of 1–10, how solid is your relationship?" and "Any major shifts for your teen (academic, legal, family)?" This avoids surveillance-feeling daily logs and gives you a relationship trend line without drowning mentors in paperwork.

* **Baseline from referral partners, not the participant.** Juvenile justice and school counselors already track the teens they refer. Request permission to access historical attendance and incident reports directly from partners—this is faster, more accurate, and doesn't ask teenagers to fill out background forms they'll resent. Set a cutoff (e.g., data from 6 months pre-match) so you're measuring meaningful change, not snapshot noise.

* **Build a "measurement with them" protocol.** At the 6-month mark, convene a teen advisory group (4–6 of your most engaged participants) for a 90-minute session where they review your current metrics and ask: "Is this telling the story of what's actually happening? What are we missing?" This isn't overhead—it's co-design. Teens often identify patterns mentors and funders miss (e.g., "we actually talk about college applications all the time, but you're not measuring that").

* **Prototype a lightweight teen survey.** Instead of a long questionnaire, try a photo-elicitation or visual scales approach: "Show a picture of what a good week with your mentor looks like" or "Point to where you are on this scale of feeling supported." Reduces literacy barriers, increases honesty, and takes 5 minutes. Pilot with 5 teens first; if it works, scale.

## Attrition Exit Data As A Feedback Signal

* **Create a structured exit form for both parties.** When a match ends, the mentor and teen each independently answer: reason (schedule change, personal reasons, mismatch, mentor relocation, etc.), how long the match lasted, who initiated the conversation, and what worked or didn't work. Keep it to 5–7 fields, 10 minutes max. This data is gold—you'll quickly spot patterns (e.g., matches breaking after month 4 with mentors aged 55+, or geographic distance being the culprit in 60% of closures).

* **Monthly exit trend review.** Dedicate 30 minutes of your program coordinator's monthly work to analyzing the previous month's exits: Why did they leave? How long did they last? Was it preventable? This isn't bureaucracy—it's real-time feedback. If you see 3 matches ending because mentors didn't know how to handle conflict, that tells you your training is missing a module.

* **Use attrition as a leading indicator, not a failure metric.** A practical reframe: If 3 months in you're seeing 40% attrition, that's telling you something about matching, training, or mentor expectations that you can fix fast. High early attrition is actually useful data—it keeps you from sinking 12 months into a flawed process. Treat exits as learning, not shame.

* **Feed exit data directly into rematch criteria.** Once you've seen 6 months of exits, look at the data: Do mentors with no kids match better? Do pairs with shared neighborhoods stay together longer? Do teens in juvenile justice track exit faster? Use these insights to tweak your matching algorithm for next cohort. It's iterative, concrete, and evidence-driven.

* **Create a "match rescue protocol."** If exit data shows that 50% of breaks happen between months 2–4, that's your window. Before month 4, check in with each pair—mentor and teen separately. Ask directly: "How's it going? Any obstacles we can help with?" Sometimes a 15-minute coordinator call prevents an exit that otherwise feels inevitable.

## Short-Cycle Versus Long-Cycle Feedback And The Measurement-Burden Chilling Effect

* **Adopt a tiered feedback approach.** For **immediate signals** (month 1–3), track only attendance (mentor reports "we met 4 times" or "we met 0 times") and participant satisfaction ("Would you like to keep meeting?"). These cost nothing and tell you if the match is viable. For **medium-term signals** (months 4–12), add school attendance and a 2-minute relationship check. Save the heavy metrics (graduation rate, justice involvement) for the final report, where they're actualized, not speculative.

* **Proxy the long-term with short-term discipline.** Instead of waiting 5 years to measure justice system involvement, track school disciplinary referrals every semester—this is a behavioral proxy that moves faster and still costs the school almost nothing. Similarly, don't wait on graduation; measure course completion and GPA by semester. You'll spot trajectory shifts years before they land in the long-term metrics.

* **Set a measurement freeze at month 9.** After 9 months of data collection, stop adding forms and surveys. Let mentors and teens just be together for the final 3 months. The chilling effect is real—people start gaming metrics or dropping out because they're tired of reporting. One cohort with light load for the last quarter will often show **better** long-term outcomes because the relationship had space to deepen.

* **Automate the metrics you can.** Work with the school district to set up a one-time data-sharing agreement that pulls your cohort's attendance and grades directly into a simple dashboard every semester. Zero burden to mentors, zero burden to teens. Same for juvenile justice partners if you're tracking recidivism. Automate; don't survey.

* **Design for sampling, not census.** You don't need data from every mentor-mentee pair at every timepoint. A practical approach might be: collect full data from 8 pairs per cohort (every one if your program is under 15 pairs, or a representative sample), and lighter check-ins from the rest. This cuts burden by 60% while keeping signal intact.

## Measure Failure, Not Success

* **Track the "stay despite friction" moments.** At your monthly team check-ins, ask mentors directly: "Any moment this month when you or the teen wanted to quit but stuck it out? What happened?" Collect these stories in a simple spreadsheet: date, what triggered the breaking point, how it got resolved. Over a year, these resilience stories become your actual program data. Maybe the real outcome is: "Mentors learned to show up even when teenagers are hostile." That's invisible to surveys but it's the core skill you're building.

* **Create a "obstacle overcome" metric.** Instead of measuring attendance (success), measure "scheduled meetings that faced obstacles but happened anyway." Did the teen have family crisis? Did the mentor have transportation trouble? Did they meet anyway, or did they reschedule and follow through? This captures grit and commitment in ways standard metrics never do, and it's what actually changes people.

* **Log the near-breaks.** When a coordinator intervenes to save a match, or when a mentor calls an emergency meeting, or when a teen discloses suicidal thoughts and the mentor helps connect them to crisis support—log it. Don't count these as "failures"—count them as the moments the program actually worked. Six months in, you'll have a portfolio of these moments that tells you far more about impact than attendance rates.

* **Build in exit-intent interviews.** When a match is on the verge of ending, conduct a structured conversation: "Help me understand—what would it have taken for this to have worked? What did we miss?" The answers often reveal systemic insight (e.g., "I needed more guidance on how to talk about his mom" or "We live too far apart and public transit doesn't work"). Reframe exits as data, not loss.

* **Measure adaptability and repair.** The real skill isn't preventing conflict—it's navigating it. Document: When conflict or misalignment happened, did the mentor and teen stay connected? Did they problem-solve together? Did they adjust expectations? These moments are where transformation happens, and they're invisible to traditional metrics. Create a narrative log of 3–4 critical conflicts per cohort and how they resolved.

## What If Outcomes Were Designed To Be Felt, Not Counted

* **Shift to community witness as primary evidence.** Instead of surveys, build in structured moments where teens share their story with the program community: a quarterly "open gathering" where each teen can talk about their mentorship (or not), and community members—teachers, parents, other teens, mentors—witness and respond. Recording these (audio or written summary) creates a qualitative evidence archive that's far richer than any checklist. Teens are the experts; your job is creating the platform for their voice.

* **Use the teen's own voice as the metric.** At 6 months and 12 months, ask each teen to record a 2–3 minute voice memo: "Tell me what's different about you this year. What do you notice? What would your friends say?" Keep these recordings. They're not scored or analyzed—they're just collected. Over years, you'll have a library of unfiltered, real transformation that no evaluation consultancy could capture. This is evidence that feels true because it is.

* **Anchor success in specific behaviors and shifts that matter locally.** Instead of "graduation rates," measure: "How many teens are talking about their futures with conviction?" Instead of "recidivism," measure: "Which teens are now the ones helping younger kids make different choices?" These are felt, visible, observable in the community—and they're what actually signals deep change. Interview the principal, the counselors, the families: "What do you notice about this teenager compared to a year ago?"

* **Create a "ritual of recognition."** At 12 months, hold a celebration where each teen is recognized for one specific, concrete way they showed up or changed—not in generic "mentor of the year" awards, but in real specificity: "You kept showing up even when your family fell apart." "You asked for help for the first time." "You made a choice that surprised all of us." These moments are felt, remembered, and far more meaningful than a metric. Document them as part of the program narrative.

* **Design feedback loops around mutual transformation.** After 12 months, ask mentors: "What did you learn about yourself? How are you different because of this teenager?" The program's deepest outcome might be that mentors become more patient, more resourced, more connected to the community. Capture that mutual shift as evidence. Interview mentors about how this year changed their perspective on young people, on struggle, on hope. That's the felt outcome.
