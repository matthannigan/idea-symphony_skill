# Sustainability, Funding & Institutional Continuity - The Systems Thinker

---

## Cost-Per-Match Unit Economics, The $15K Gap, And Behavioral Lock-In

* **Direct cost baseline.** Break first-year expenses into screening ($200/mentor × 40 = $8K), 40 hours group training ($3K), weekly supervisor check-ins (60 hours @ $25/hr = $1.5K), and administrative overhead ($2.5K). Result: ~$15K covers 40 matched pairs, or $375 per pair. If second-year costs are similar but grant depletes, the program has sunk relationship-building but lost operational runway—watch for "Shifting the Burden" where relationships become dependent on grant timing rather than sustainable practice.

* **Self-amplifying recruitment burnout.** Early success brings parental demand exceeding current capacity; coordinator works unsustainable hours to maintain wait-list relationships; mentor peers see burnout and drop recruitment efforts; growth stalls, then reverses. This reinforcing cycle compounds if the organization hasn't built capacity *before* demand arrives. Capacity buildout (hiring a part-time assistant) must precede success, not follow it.

* **Contingency ladder for pair continuity.** If funding gaps at month six, establish a tiered preservation protocol: (1) maintain all current matches with volunteer-only support (coordinator moves to 50% paid, mentors handle light admin), (2) pause new recruitment to avoid commitment-taking you can't staff, (3) transfer responsibility for logistics to a mentor leadership circle (paid stipends from remaining budget). This distributes risk across roles rather than collapsing at the coordinator role.

* **Behavioral commitment lock-in mapping.** Document which organizational behaviors are grant-dependent: Are mentors expecting paid coordinator response 24/7? Have youth started expecting weekly outings funded by program? Is the congregation expecting the coordinator to manage everything? Each of these represents behavioral momentum that works *against* you when funding tightens. Build in framing (e.g., "monthly not weekly check-ins," "mentor-led problem-solving," "co-responsibility for logistics") that assumes scarcity from the start.

* **Grant dependency as a system archetype.** This is "Success to the Successful"—the program succeeds, demand grows, the organization scales operations to meet demand, success becomes dependent on grant timing, and the inability to sustain causes a sharp collapse rather than graceful scaling down. The intervention isn't more fundraising; it's deliberate capacity constraint: set a target (e.g., 20 matches max) and grow slowly enough to build sustainable operations *before* scaling.

## Single-Coordinator Single-Point-Of-Failure And Compliance-By-Design

* **Documented delegation framework.** Create a "continuity binder" (digital or physical) containing: consent records with backup access protocols, match files indexed by youth and mentor, mandatory-reporter log with escalation history, and policy references. Assign one designated staff member (e.g., executive director or board secretary) as "compliance lieutenant" with read-only access and monthly audit responsibility. If the coordinator becomes unavailable, the lieutenant can locate records and notify relevant parties without reconstructing knowledge from memory.

* **Compliance as structural rhythm.** Rather than front-loading legal compliance before launch, treat it as an annual governance cycle: Q1 board review of policy effectiveness (e.g., "Did consent forms prevent any breach? Did escalation protocols work?"), Q2 refresher training for all staff and mentors on mandatory-reporter obligations and documentation, Q3 audit of match files for completeness, Q4 policy updates based on learning. This keeps compliance visible and evolving rather than static and forgotten.

* **Embedded oversight without bureaucracy.** Establish a standing "safeguarding committee" (rotating board member, coordinator, one mentor representative) that meets quarterly, reviews de-identified incident reports, identifies patterns, and recommends policy adjustments. This distributes compliance knowledge across roles and creates peer accountability—if the coordinator misses a step, the mentor rep or board member catches it in the next meeting.

* **Backup access architecture.** Build redundancy into critical systems now: Two staff members sign off on consent forms (coordinator + director). Match files live in a shared drive with versioning (not email). Mandatory-reporter logs are monthly-summarized and filed with the executive director. If the coordinator leaves unexpectedly, nothing is stranded in personal files or undocumented memory.

* **Knowledge transfer as prevention.** Don't wait for turnover—conduct a "compliance walkthrough" with the designated lieutenant twice yearly, going through records, reviewing procedures, testing backup access. This surfaces documentation gaps early (e.g., "Wait, where's the copy of the liability policy?") and ensures the lieutenant actually knows how to execute continuity if needed, rather than discovering it in crisis.

## Caseload Supervisor Ratio And The Mentor-Attrition Reputation Feedback

* **Realistic supervision ceiling.** A single coordinator can maintain quality check-ins and escalation response for 20–25 active matches if mentors are trained and proactive. Beyond that, response times to struggling mentors lengthen, escalations stack up, and the coordinator becomes a bottleneck. At 40 pairs, you need a part-time coordinator assistant (10–15 hours/week, ~$8K annually) to handle scheduling, basic welfare checks, and administrative triage, freeing the lead coordinator for complex cases and mentor coaching. This scales predictably: 40 pairs → 2 FTE, 80 pairs → 3 FTE.

* **Burnout cascade and word-of-mouth decay.** If three mentors burn out visibly in months 4–6 (withdrawing from program, sharing frustration in congregation), word spreads: "The program is demanding," "The coordinator never responds," "The kids are harder than expected." This reputation feedback creates a recruitment cliff—future volunteer recruitment becomes *harder* even if you fix the underlying burnout cause, because early cohort departures have shaped perceptions. Prevention: proactive mentor coaching in month 2, before burnout accumulates.

* **Caseload intensity stratification.** Not all matches are equal. Youth in crisis escalations require weekly coordinator check-ins; stable matches need monthly contact. Create three tiers: intensive (weekly coordinator), standard (bi-weekly), and low-touch (monthly). This allows one coordinator to supervise 40+ total matches if they're distributed across tiers, and scales capacity without linear hiring—80 pairs might still be manageable with 1.5 FTE if staffing is intelligence-driven rather than pure headcount.

* **Mentor circle as load-distribution leverage.** Instead of all mentor problems routing to the coordinator, establish a "mentor huddle" (monthly peer circle, 8–10 mentors rotating attendance) where mentors share struggles, offer peer coaching, and collectively problem-solve. The coordinator facilitates and handles escalations, but peer support *before* escalation reduces burnout intensity. This is a leverage point: small investment in peer structure prevents high-cost coordinator interventions later.

* **Early burnout as system warning.** When mentors depart in months 4–6, treat it as a system diagnostic, not individual failure. Exit conversations should map specific pressure points: Was the youth more challenging than expected? Did the mentor feel isolated in problem-solving? Was the coordinator unresponsive? Each answer points to operational redesign (better matching, peer support, staffing capacity). If you don't diagnose burnout causes, reputation decay spreads even after you add staff.
