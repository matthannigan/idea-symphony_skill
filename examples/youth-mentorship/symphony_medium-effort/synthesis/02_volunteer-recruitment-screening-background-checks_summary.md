# Summary: Volunteer Recruitment, Screening & Background Checks

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension in this cluster is that the screening bar must be strict enough to satisfy negligent-selection-and-retention standards yet open enough to keep a viable, well-matched volunteer pool. Set the bar at the legal floor alone and the insurer and a guardian's lawyer can still reach the organization; set it reflexively high and the program rejects exactly the second-chance and lived-experience volunteers who model resilience to teens, while a heavy intake funnel quietly thins the pool further. Every perspective lands on the same resolution: a defensible policy is not a stricter policy but a documented one. A written three-tier framework (automatic bars, discretionary review, irrelevant findings) applied consistently by a standing panel and logged with reasoning protects against negligent-retention and discrimination claims at once.

Funnel attrition is the second hard constraint. All four perspectives expect 50–70% attrition between expression of interest and an active match, which means the 40 interested adults realistically yield around 20 mentors. The defenses converge: recruit a 50–70 person top of funnel, launch in rolling cohorts rather than all at once, start background checks the moment an application is complete to preserve momentum, and negotiate teen-enrollment cadence with schools and courts so demand tracks mentor readiness. A teen who enrolls and sits unmatched past 45–60 days is both a broken promise and a liability.

A recurring and important caution is that a clean background check is necessary but not sufficient. Boundary violations and value misalignment do not appear in a criminal database. The standard of care depends on behavior-based monitoring between checks and on a values screen built entirely from behavioral scenarios rather than belief inquiry. In a faith-based program this firewall is not optional: a "values alignment" screen that strays into beliefs risks religious-discrimination and protected-class claims. The most actionable strategic implication is to invest as much in the structured pre-match interview, the confidential reporting channel, and the seasonal support calendar as in the criminal check itself, because that is where both the real risk and the real match quality reside.

## Key Themes

### Documentation is the actual safeguard
Across every question, the defense against both negligent-retention and discrimination liability is the same: a written framework, applied consistently, with each decision logged and reasoned. The decision log cuts both ways—it defeats a "you missed a risk" claim and a "you treated me inconsistently" claim. After twelve months it also doubles as a dataset for refining the framework.

### Heavy attrition is predictable, so design for it rather than against it
A 50–70% funnel loss is the baseline expectation, not a failure. Recruiting past target, launching in rolling cohorts, front-loading background checks, and maintaining a standing bench converts that loss from a launch-day crisis into managed reserve capacity.

### The criminal check is the floor, not the safeguard
Clean re-checks can mask deteriorating behavior and criminal history predicts match success poorly. Frequent wellness check-ins, a low-barrier confidential reporting channel, and a behavior-based pre-match interview do the work the background check cannot.

### Values screening must be behavior-based and firewalled from belief inquiry
Surfacing worldview misalignment before a failed match is a standard-of-care issue. In a congregation-based program, a direct belief screen invites civil-rights exposure. The shared method is scenario-based respect screening that infers capacity from past behavior, never doctrinal agreement.

### Institutional rhythms collide, so the program needs its own cadence
Congregation, school, and court calendars peak and trough on different schedules. Staggered source-aligned recruitment, multiple training cohorts, check-ins timed before each crunch, and pre-agreed seasonal step-downs let the program ride the misalignment rather than be caught off-guard by it.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Draft the written three-tier disqualification framework (automatic / discretionary / irrelevant), anchored to the state statutory floor and the insurer's stated expectations, and have a volunteer-law attorney review it ($500–$1,500) before running any check.
- Resolve the expunged/sealed-records question against state public-records law, and build a non-punitive voluntary-disclosure invitation into the application.
- Stand up a 3-person borderline-review panel with one written rubric, documented reasoning, rotating membership, and a two-week turnaround target.
- Size recruitment to 50–70 expressions of interest and add an intent-confirmation call within two weeks of initial interest.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Launch in rolling monthly cohorts of 8–10, starting background checks immediately on application completion and scheduling interviews to coincide with results.
- Build the behavior-based, scenario-driven pre-match values interview and document it for every candidate; route weak-alignment volunteers to non-mentoring roles.
- Stand up a low-barrier confidential reporting channel for teens and families, plus a monthly-to-quarterly wellness check-in cadence for active mentors.
- Negotiate rolling teen-enrollment windows and a hard 45–60 day match deadline with school and juvenile-justice referral partners, with a holding-list communication plan.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Set the re-screening cadence (annual or biennial) plus a narrow written list of off-cycle triggers, name the staff member who monitors arrest notifications, and define role-tier re-screening on escalation.
- Establish staggered source-aligned recruitment windows, two-plus training cohorts, and a partnership calendar from quarterly liaison meetings with each institution.
- Adopt seasonal mentoring agreements, sabbaticals, and pre-mapped reduced-contact windows, with check-ins timed before each source's predictable crunch.
- Write a data-retention and secure-disposal schedule for screening records into the volunteer manual, accounting for any applicable state privacy laws.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- A transparent second-chance posture can attract reformed and lived-experience volunteers who model resilience and credibility to teens.
- Selectivity, when communicated honestly, becomes a trust signal that families value over mass enrollment.
- The borderline-decision log becomes a year-two dataset for refining the framework and forecasting attrition.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- Meeting only the legal minimum can still leave the org exposed to negligent-retention suits from guardians.
- A clean background check can give false confidence while a volunteer's behavior deteriorates invisibly between checks.
- Slow background-check processing disproportionately loses lower-income candidates with less schedule flexibility.
- A teen who enrolls and sits unmatched past 45–60 days becomes both a broken promise and an escalating liability.

**Trade-offs**:
- Annual re-screening gives the clearest legal posture. Biennial is cheaper and arguably no less effective given database lag.
- Strict screening reduces risk but shrinks an already-thinning funnel and may exclude high-value second-chance volunteers.
- Distributing borderline authority to a panel curbs single-point bias but without one rubric can itself produce inconsistent and litigable outcomes.
- Inviting voluntary disclosure of expunged records builds defensibility and trust but risks chilling otherwise-qualified applicants.
