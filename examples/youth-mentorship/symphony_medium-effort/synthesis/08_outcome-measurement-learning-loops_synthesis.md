# Brainstorming Synthesis: Outcome Measurement & Learning Loops

---

## Synthesized Insights by Question

### Question 1: Outcome variable selection, baseline acquisition, and teens' own definitions

**Full question**: Given the program's goals, what are the two or three quantifiable proxies (school attendance delta, disciplinary incident rate, mentor-reported relationship quality score) that are both meaningful and obtainable without burdening participants or requiring data-sharing agreements the organization cannot easily execute, how do we measure outcomes without making mentors feel surveilled or teens feel over-surveyed, what is the realistic pre-program baseline-collection window (with which indicators sourced from school counselors or juvenile justice referrers rather than self-report), and how does the measurement framework include a 15-year-old's definition of "a good year with a mentor" alongside the funder's metrics, treating data collection as something done with participants rather than to them? [User Q]

* **Pull baselines from schools and referral partners, never from the teens themselves.** Counselors and case managers already track attendance, disciplinary incidents, and grades. Request that historical data directly (a 6-month pre-match window is reasonable) rather than asking a guarded 15-year-old to self-assess. This approach is cheaper, more accurate, and avoids the dignity cost of asking teens to document their own dysfunction. One caution: different referrers apply different "at-risk" thresholds, so read baselines as within-cohort trends, not cross-cohort benchmarks.

* **Build teen definitions of "a good year" into the framework through co-design.** Ask teens what success means early (a youth advisory group or a short structured conversation at intake or month 2), capture goals verbatim, and fold them into the mentorship agreement. Translate soft language like "feeling heard" into observable proxies so it survives funder scrutiny, and treat any gap between teen definitions and funder metrics as actionable information.

* **Replace mentor surveys with low-burden reflection that mentors keep.** Use a monthly one-page guide or three-sentence journal that lives in the mentor's hands, plus a periodic 1-10 relationship-solidity rating, to show the relationship trend without surveillance. Self-report inflates as emotional investment grows, so don't let it stand alone. A periodic third-party observation provides a ground-truth check.

* **Offer a measurement-light track so deeper data collection never costs the relationship.** Let mentors choose between a standard track (quarterly check-in plus baseline/midpoint data pull) and a lightweight track (annual reflection plus year-end data only). Over-measurement undermines the relationships you are trying to measure.

* **Prototype a literacy-light teen instrument before scaling anything.** If you collect anything from teens directly, pilot a photo-elicitation or visual-scale tool with five teens first. It lowers literacy barriers, increases honesty, and takes five minutes.

### Question 2: Attrition exit data as a feedback signal

**Full question**: When a match ends before the one-year commitment, what structured exit data (reason category, duration, initiating party, antecedent events) should be collected, and how does that data feed back into refining the matching process and identifying systemic program weaknesses?

* **Collect structured, coded exit data from both parties: reason category, duration, initiating party, and a short narrative.** A compact 5-7 field form completed independently by mentor and teen, with coded reason categories and a one-sentence narrative, fed to leadership monthly, surfaces patterns within six months. Keep the framing explicitly non-punitive—tracking who ended the match can make mentors defensive about reporting early warning signs. Use only aggregate patterns.

* **Run the teen's exit conversation separately from the mentor, framed as learning rather than blame.** Have program staff (not the mentor) hold a low-stakes conversation with the teen ("What would have made this work? What did we miss?"). Teens name micro-issues mentors never perceive. Frame it as relationship repair and program improvement, not the teen explaining a failure.

* **Treat attrition as a leading indicator that feeds matching and triggers fast intervention.** High month-3 attrition tells you about matching, training, or expectations while you can still fix it. Exit themes (geography, age, justice-track) feed the next cohort's matching criteria. A match-rescue protocol helps: if breaks cluster in months 2-4, check in with each pair before month 4, since a 15-minute call can prevent an exit.

* **Hand-code themes and act on the top two; don't wait for statistical significance you'll never reach.** With low, variable volume you cannot statistically separate "mentors who leave early" from random noise. Code qualitative narratives by hand and act on the top one or two themes. Flag systemic weakness only after the third or fourth similar case. Favor front-loaded rapid-cycle feedback (pause, adjust training, resume) over slow endpoint analysis.

### Question 3: Short-cycle versus long-cycle feedback and the measurement-burden chilling effect

**Full question**: Many meaningful outcomes (educational attainment, justice system involvement, economic stability) manifest over years, while surveys, check-ins, and reporting requirements add friction for both mentors and teens. At what point does the measurement apparatus start degrading the participation it's trying to study, and what shorter-cycle signals function as reliable proxies for long-term outcomes without optimizing for proxies in ways that miss the deeper changes the program actually cares about?

* **Adopt a tiered measurement cadence and reuse data schools already collect.** Track only viability signals in months 1-3 (did they meet, do they want to continue). Add a brief relationship check and reused school data in months 4-12, and save heavy long-term metrics for the final report. Automate by reusing existing school discipline and attendance records rather than surveying. This puts near-zero burden on mentors or teens.

* **Proxy long-term outcomes with faster-moving behavioral signals, but write down how each proxy could mislead.** Track semester disciplinary referrals, course completion, and GPA, which shift years before recidivism or graduation at near-zero cost. For each proxy, document in one sentence how it could be gamed or diverge from the goal (attendance can signal compliance, not re-engagement). Revisit annually so the easy metric doesn't quietly replace the goal.

* **Freeze measurement in the final quarter so the relationship has room to deepen.** Stop adding forms and surveys around month 9 and let mentors and teens simply be together for the last three months. A cohort with a light final quarter often shows better long-term outcomes because people stop gaming the metrics.

* **Meta-measure the apparatus: set a friction floor and kill any metric whose completion rate falls.** Declare an acceptable burden ceiling before rollout (e.g., 10 min/month for mentors, one question/month for teens). Track completion and response rates, and retire any metric missing completion by more than about 20%. Dial frequency back when teen-touchpoint response rates drop below about 60%.

* **The deeper cost of measurement burden is selection bias, not just fatigue.** Heavy reporting is survived disproportionately by already-organized mentors and already-connected youth. The hardest-to-reach participants drop out under load, the remaining sample becomes unrepresentative, and apparent success is inflated. Reduce friction most for the hardest-to-reach (auto-capture through mentors, no youth surveys) and accept noisier data to preserve representativeness.

* **Let mentor and teen review a shared one-page dashboard together instead of reporting upward.** Shared visibility ("we've met 8 times, you mentioned school 6 times, you got a B last month") replaces top-down reporting and lowers burden. It creates two-way accountability.

### Question 4: Measure failure, not success

**Full question**: What if instead of tracking program outcomes (attendance, graduation rates, "relationship quality") you tracked every moment a mentor or teen wanted to quit but didn't? What would that data tell you that success metrics never could? Resilience and durability in relationships are invisible to outcome measurement but may be the actual product being built.

* **Track "wanted to quit but stayed" moments as core program data, captured in the natural rhythm of mentor check-ins.** Ask mentors at monthly or quarterly check-ins whether there was a moment they or the teen nearly quit and what kept them in. Log these as resilience data. Over a year these accumulate into a portfolio that says more about impact than attendance rates. Choosing to stay despite difficulty is the durable relationship being built.

* **Distinguish genuine resilience from resignation, or the metric lies to you.** A teen who stayed might have built grit or might have lost hope and seen no point in leaving. Ask "what changed your mind?" "My mentor helped me see a way forward" signals resilience, while "I was afraid to tell my mentor" signals resignation wearing resilience as a mask. Without this filter, near-quit counts can mistake quiet despair for durability.

* **Read durability in continuous behavior, because not every fraying is a dramatic near-quit.** Some relationships quietly drift without a visible crisis. Track no-shows, reschedule patterns, conversation length, and whether the teen progressively shares more, drawn from mentor observation logs rather than self-report. Durability lives in consistency, not only in surviving acute stress.

* **Log repair moves and obstacles-overcome, not just survival.** The observable signal is what people do under strain: texting the coordinator instead of quitting, meeting despite a family crisis, navigating a conflict together. Build a narrative log of a few critical conflicts per cohort and how they resolved. Navigating friction (not avoiding it) is where transformation happens.

* **Pair durability inside the match with small risk-taking outside it, so you don't reward a low-demand match.** You can preserve a match by lowering expectations, but that isn't building resilience for the wider world. Ask whether the teen tried something new (raised a hand in class, joined a club, was vulnerable with a peer). In-match durability bleeds into real life change through those moments.

* **Listen for what the teen sacrifices to keep showing up.** A teen who chooses the weekly meeting over something they wanted to do is exercising the exact resilience being built. Asking "what was hard about coming today, and why did you anyway?" surfaces it.

### Question 5: What if outcomes were designed to be felt, not counted

**Full question**: What if we completely abandoned the standard outcome measurement frameworks (attendance, grades, recidivism) and designed a program whose primary evidence of success was qualitative transformation visible to the teens themselves and the community around them? What entirely new forms of witnessing and documentation would we need to invent?

* **Make structured community witnessing the primary qualitative evidence.** Recruit the people already around the teen (mentor, family, school counselor, peers, community elders) to notice and document specific observed shifts. Triangulate across witnesses. Make this rigorous rather than anecdotal: assign a set of anchors per teen, ask the same question quarterly ("What change have you noticed?"), and look for consensus to capture witnessed transformation instead of cherry-picked stories.

* **Center the teen's own voice as evidence through first-person narrative capture.** Collect teens' own accounts of their change (a written, voice-memo, or video answer to "tell the story of a moment you felt different this year"), kept unscored as a library of real transformation. This produces richer evidence than any external measure and treats the teen as the authority on their own growth.

* **Document observable shifts in mindset and presence rather than abstract transformation.** Capture concrete, witnessed micro-changes ("he used to say 'I'm just like my dad,' now he says 'I'm trying something different'"; from silent to laughing; from defensive to curious) as the actual unit of qualitative evidence. These presence and mindset shifts often precede measurable outcomes and are visible to the community.

* **Do not abandon the quantitative floor; dual-track instead.** Foundations report to boards in numbers and cannot tell donors a program works because teens "feel" different. Abandoning quantification entirely costs future funding and the comparability needed to convince a skeptical superintendent. Keep standard metrics (attendance, grades, a brief monthly dyad-quality score) as a floor and layer felt outcomes on top. Show funders the numbers, show communities the stories.

* **Guard against optimizing for the poetic and missing the quiet, vulnerable teens.** Purely qualitative evidence is easiest to capture for articulate, already-engaged, culturally-aligned teens. Quiet or trauma-affected youth may transform deeply in ways the program never narrates. Explicitly train witnesses to notice quiet transformation (persistence, small risk-taking, protective behaviors) and create witnessing that does not require eloquence.

* **Create durable artifacts and rituals that make transformation visible and shared.** Build tangible records of change: a physical or digital "community gallery" of mentorship moments (a photo, a quote, a reflection, with consent) and a year-end "ritual of recognition" naming each teen's specific, concrete growth. These make success a felt, witnessed reality that works with funders, recruits, and the community.

* **Treat the mentor's own transformation as program evidence too.** Ask mentors at year-end what they learned about themselves and how this teenager changed them. The program's deepest outcome may be mentors becoming more patient and more connected to the community.
