# Summary: Outcome Measurement & Learning Loops

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension is between measurement light enough to protect relationships and measurement rigorous enough to keep the funder satisfied and distinguish genuine change from its convincing imitations. Every perspective agrees the program must measure outcomes with participants rather than to them, and that the heaviest-handed instruments (teen self-assessment baselines, frequent surveys) would damage exactly the trust the program is trying to create. But the responses do not allow the program to drift into pure qualitative storytelling, because a funder reports to its board in numbers and a quiet, guarded teen can look identical whether they are growing or giving up.

A strong practical consensus resolves most of the friction. Pull baselines and ongoing behavioral data from records schools and referral partners already keep, so attendance, disciplinary incidents, and grades arrive at near-zero burden. Replace mentor surveys with reflection the mentor keeps, plus an occasional 1-10 relationship rating. Co-design success with each teen at intake and treat the gap between their definition and the funder's as useful signal. Sequence the burden by scheduling viability checks early, reused school data mid-program, and heavy metrics only in the final report, with a deliberate measurement freeze in the last quarter so the relationship can deepen.

Two adversarial reframes sharpen the rest. First, the deepest cost of measurement burden is selection bias rather than fatigue. Heavy reporting is survived by the already-organized and already-connected, so the hardest-to-reach participants drop out and apparent success inflates. This inverts the usual instinct, arguing for the least friction on the most fragile participants even at the cost of noisier data. Second, resilience metrics only work if you can separate grit from resignation. A teen who "stayed" may have built durability or simply lost hope, and only the question "what changed your mind?" tells them apart.

The strategic implication is a dual-track design. Keep a thin quantitative floor (attendance, grades, a brief monthly dyad-quality score) for comparability and funding, and layer structured qualitative witnessing on top: triangulated community observation, teen-authored narratives, and documented shifts in mindset and presence. Treat attrition as the program's fastest learning loop rather than as failure, and track the moments people nearly quit but stayed as evidence of the actual product being built.

## Key Themes

### Measure with participants, not on them
The single most convergent commitment across the cluster is that data collection is a relationship act. Source baselines from school counselors and justice referrers instead of asking teens to document their own dysfunction, co-design success goals with each teen at intake, and make the teen a sense-maker who sees and shapes findings rather than a data subject. The dignity principle is also a data-quality principle: instruments that feel like surveillance produce both worse relationships and worse data.

### Reuse and sequence to keep friction near zero
Rather than build new measurement, reuse what schools already collect and stage the burden across the year: viability signals first, reused behavioral data in the middle, heavy long-term metrics only at the end, with a final-quarter freeze. Several responses push further into meta-measurement by setting an explicit friction ceiling (minutes per month, questions per teen) and retiring any metric whose completion rate falls below threshold. This way the measurement system itself has to survive contact with reality.

### Attrition as the fastest feedback loop
Exits are reframed from shame to diagnostic. Collect compact, coded, non-punitive exit data from both parties, run the teen's exit conversation separately from the mentor, and feed themes into the next cohort's matching plus a mid-program rescue protocol. Because a 40-volunteer program will never reach statistical significance, the discipline is to hand-code narratives, act on the top one or two themes, and flag systemic weakness only after a few similar cases rather than the first.

### Resilience versus resignation (load-bearing reframe)
The "measure failure, not success" frame is productive, but only with one guard that changes which data is trustworthy: a teen who nearly quit but stayed may have built grit or may have quietly given up, and these look identical in a near-quit count. Asking "what changed your mind?" is what separates durability from despair wearing its mask. This single distinction determines whether the cluster's signature metric measures the actual product or a comforting illusion.

### Felt evidence, but on a quantitative floor
The aspiration to make success felt and witnessed (community observation, teen narratives, mindset shifts, recognition rituals) appears across responses, but they refuse to abandon counting. Foundations need numbers, comparability convinces skeptical referrers, and a monthly dyad-quality score prevents qualitative mystique from hiding a failing match. Purely qualitative measurement also risks optimizing for the eloquent and missing the quiet, trauma-affected teens whose transformation is least narratable.

## Recommended Next Steps

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Negotiate data-sharing with school counselors and justice referrers to pull a ~6-month pre-match baseline (attendance, disciplinary incidents, grades) so no teen is asked to self-assess.
- Define the thin quantitative floor: 2-3 reused behavioral proxies plus a brief monthly mentor dyad-quality rating (1-10), and write one sentence per proxy on how it could mislead.
- Replace any planned mentor survey with a mentor-kept monthly reflection (three sentences) and set an explicit friction ceiling (e.g., 10 min/month for mentors, one question/month for teens).
- Build the coded, non-punitive exit form (5-7 fields, both parties, separate teen conversation with staff) before any match can end.

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Co-design success with each teen at intake or month 2, fold 2-3 teen-defined goals into the mentorship agreement, and start logging the teen-vs-funder definition gap.
- Stand up a mid-program rescue protocol: if breaks cluster in months 2-4, check in with each pair separately before month 4.
- Begin structured community witnessing: assign a small set of anchors per teen and ask the same "what change have you noticed?" question quarterly, training witnesses to notice quiet, non-eloquent transformation.
- Start tracking near-quit moments at monthly check-ins, paired with the "what changed your mind?" resilience-versus-resignation filter.

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Run a measurement freeze in the final quarter and compare long-term outcomes against the friction-ceiling completion data to confirm the apparatus isn't degrading participation.
- Hand-code accumulated exit narratives and feed the top themes into the next cohort's matching criteria and mentor training.
- Build the dual-track evidence product: numbers for funders, a curated archive of teen-authored narratives, community-gallery artifacts, and a year-end recognition ritual for communities and recruits.
- Add one new baseline indicator per year only as trust and data-sharing capacity deepen, rather than front-loading ambitious agreements.

## Key Considerations

**Opportunities**:
- School and justice partners already hold reliable baseline data, so meaningful metrics cost almost nothing and burden no one.
- Attrition can become the program's fastest learning loop, surfacing matching and training fixes within a single cohort.
- Teen-authored narratives and community witnessing produce evidence that is both more honest than surveys and more persuasive to communities.
- Tracking near-quit-but-stayed moments captures resilience, the program's likely actual product, that standard outcomes never see.

**Risks & Challenges**:
- Heavy measurement creates selection bias that drives out the hardest-to-reach participants and silently inflates apparent success.
- A guarded teen's resignation can be misread as resilience, corrupting the signature "measure failure" metric.
- Abandoning quantification entirely would cost future funding and all comparability with other programs.
- Purely qualitative evidence optimizes for the articulate and misses quiet, trauma-affected teens. Mentor self-report also inflates with emotional investment.

**Trade-offs**:
- Lower friction protects relationships and representativeness but yields noisier, less complete data.
- Co-designed and felt outcomes honor teen voice but are harder to defend against grant reporting requirements than hard numbers.
- Short-cycle proxies move fast and cheap but can be gamed and can quietly displace the long-term goals the program cares about.
- More frequent measurement gives earlier warning but accelerates the chilling effect that drives the most fragile pairs out.
