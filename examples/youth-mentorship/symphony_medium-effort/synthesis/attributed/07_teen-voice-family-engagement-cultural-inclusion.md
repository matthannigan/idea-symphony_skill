### Question 1: The arrival moment and the dignity of being matched

**Full question**: What does a teen experience in the first 15 minutes of their first mentor meeting — what are they thinking, feeling, and silently assessing — and what does the matching process feel like from the teen's perspective: are they an object being assigned, or a person being heard? What would it mean to design intake as an experience that itself communicates respect?

* **Give the teen a real voice in the match before it happens, not a form to fill out.** All four perspectives converge here: the difference between dignity and processing is whether the teen's stated preferences visibly shape the match. Let the teen describe what they want in a mentor through a recorded or written reflection, or a real conversation the mentor witnesses and references within minutes. The test is whether the teen leaves feeling "they listened" rather than "they collected my answers and ignored them."
  * **Teen completes a reflection (video/audio/written) as a frame, not a screening tool, so the mentor meets a person with preferences.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Matching intake structured so the teen expresses what they actually want; being heard is dignity, being collected-then-ignored reads as performance.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Flip the dynamic: staff meets the teen first to hear goals, the prospective mentor joins as observer, signaling the teen directs their own match.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Ask "What matters most to you in hanging out with someone?" as real conversation; matching becomes the first proof someone listened.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **The physical space is the first message, before anyone speaks.** Three perspectives independently flag that an institutional office, a church office with religious symbols, or a sterile room decorated with stock posters tells certain teens "this isn't for you" before a word is exchanged. A warm, informal, lived-in space (couch over desk, natural light, snacks, chairs at an angle) communicates connection over compliance.
  * **A church office or boardroom signals institutional, watchful space; consider neutral ground or let the teen choose the first location.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **The room should feel like a real gathering place, not an intake facility; is there tea, a couch, the feeling of being invited rather than processed?** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Two chairs at an angle, not across a desk; the space itself says "this is about connection, not compliance."** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Name the relationship's exit honestly so the teen is not trapped guessing.** Two perspectives stress that uncertainty about "what does matched mean, and what if it doesn't work" is its own anxiety. A no-questions-asked rematch window, stated plainly, communicates that the relationship is built on genuine connection rather than obligation.
  * **Build a no-questions-asked rematch window (first 3 weeks) where either party can request a different pairing without guilt.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Spell out "we commit to finding someone, and if it isn't working by month three we'll adjust or rematch," which removes the trapped-guessing anxiety.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*

* **A 48-hour post-meeting check-in catches the silence you would otherwise misread.** Single-persona insight worth acting on: a teen can feel respected in the moment and still decide "that wasn't for me," and you will attribute non-engagement to resistance unless you build a low-pressure feedback channel.
  * **Send a brief non-judgmental check-in 48 hours after the first meeting ("How are you feeling about this?") to catch early misalignment.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Build accountability into staff behavior with an observable "teen voice" checklist.** Single-persona operational insight: document five observable respect behaviors (teen speaks unprompted, teen asks questions, mentor mirrors the teen's energy, no "we selected you" phrasing, staff acknowledges hesitations) so staff audit their own intake meetings.
  * **A one-page checklist of five observable behaviors turns "respect" into something staff can self-audit; no budget required.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

* **A nervous, honest mentor is an asset, not a liability.** Single-persona reframe: a mentor admitting "I've never done this and I want to do right by you" gives the teen permission to be imperfect too, shifting matching from finding the flawless adult to finding one brave enough to learn alongside them.
  * **The mentor's visible nervousness, named aloud, becomes mutual permission to be imperfect.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

### Question 2: Parent partnership when home is complicated and communication that doesn't backfire

**Full question**: Beyond obtaining consent signatures, how might parents and guardians be meaningfully included as participants in the program's success, given barriers like work schedules, language, and distrust of institutions — and for teens whose home environment is part of what makes them "at-risk" (unstable housing, incarcerated parents, family conflict), how does program design account for the reality that parental involvement may be fraught, while structuring guardian communication so it builds trust rather than creating a surveillance dynamic that causes teens to shut down?

* **The surveillance dynamic is the central risk, and it is predictable; separate serious-safety reporting from day-to-day confidentiality.** All four perspectives converge: regular mentor reports to parents make the teen experience the mentor as institutional monitoring, and the teen shuts down. The fix is an explicit, stated boundary that mentors escalate genuine safety concerns (abuse, trafficking, imminent harm) to supervisors, while ordinary conversation stays between mentor and teen. Separating one-time legal consent from an opt-in for ongoing updates lets distrustful parents consent without committing to surveillance-feeling contact.
  * **Be explicit: serious safety concerns go to supervisors and parents, but day-to-day conversation stays between mentor and teen, so teens choose what to share.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Parents fear mentor contact equals child protective services; design trust first with clear boundaries on what gets shared, why, and how it protects rather than exposes.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Separate consent from ongoing communication via a two-form system; teen and staff decide when parent contact is appropriate, not automatic.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **"We'll never surprise you with information; you'll always hear things from us first" becomes a protective fence for the teen.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Position the parent as the expert on their own child, which flips the power dynamic.** Three perspectives independently land on inviting the parent to brief the mentor ("Tell me what I should know to show up well for your teen") rather than receiving reports. For families used to being blamed or surveilled, being asked for their wisdom is transformative and builds investment.
  * **Invite parents to help the mentor understand the teen's cultural and family context as a real conversation, positioning them as the expert.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Hire bilingual family liaisons from the community who understand local distrust patterns, so engagement is relational not transactional.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **"What does your kid love? What's one thing they're proud of?" shifts a parent from defensive to invested.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Make participation asynchronous and accommodating, because work schedules and poverty are structural, not motivational.** Three perspectives converge: a parent working two jobs cannot attend evening events or answer email promptly, and reading that as disengagement is a design error. Offer voice messages, flexible windows, interpreters offered without fanfare, and small incentives (gift cards, bus passes) to offset the cost of participation.
  * **Make participation asynchronous; offer small incentives to offset the time and cost burden, treating work schedules as structural barriers.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Offer interpreters without making it a big deal; flexible text/email/scheduled calls on the parent's schedule; accommodation over requirement.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Childcare reimbursement so a parent can attend an occasional event; letters in the home language; meet families where their constraints actually are.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Build credibility with a "positive news only" period before any hard conversation.** Single-persona operational insight: train mentors to send one positive observation weekly for the first three weeks, so that when a real concern surfaces it is framed as partnership ("let's problem-solve together") on a foundation of trust rather than landing cold.
  * **Positive-news-only contact for three weeks before any concern-escalation call delays hard conversations until credibility exists.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Design a family advisory board with honoraria to veto surveillance-feeling language.** Single-persona insight: recruit 3-4 parents from the cohort, paid $50 per quarterly meeting, to review communication templates and flag punitive or surveillance-like phrasing, catching blind spots the staff cannot see.
  * **A paid family advisory board reviews templates and vetoes language that feels punitive, improving legitimacy with families.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Define guardianship broadly so no teen is forced to choose between the program and family safety.** Single-persona reframe worth surfacing: for teens with no safe parent to involve, ask "is there someone you trust — an aunt, an older sibling, a neighbor?" and design for complex family ecosystems rather than recreating the institutional demand for parental sign-off that some families cannot provide.
  * **Offer a "trusted-adult" alternative to parental notification, honoring that teens often have complex family ecosystems and some have no safe adult to notify.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*
  * **Train mentors to ask the teen first ("Would you like me to check in with your guardian?") and consider the mentor as the trusted adult who hears concerns before family contact.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 3: Cultural-linguistic fit, the stigma of "at-risk," and faith neutrality

**Full question**: From the perspective of teens from communities that may historically distrust faith-based institutions, authority figures, or the juvenile justice system — what signals in the program's design, language, and environment either invite them in or quietly tell them this isn't for them; how do teens experience the label that got them into this program; and without explicit policy clarity on religious neutrality in program delivery, does the organization's faith identity (an asset for congregation trust) function as a deterrent for secular families, LGBTQ+ teens, or families from different religious traditions, potentially raising civil rights questions about publicly-referred participants placed in a faith-directed program?

* **Drop the "at-risk" label from anything a teen sees; it deepens shame and reads as a verdict.** All four perspectives converge: the deficit framing precedes the teen into the room and tells them "I'm broken and they're fixing me." Replace it with aspirational, neutral naming ("Youth Connection Program," "a space where an adult cares about your actual life") and ask teens what they would call it.
  * **Avoid "at-risk" in any teen-facing material; reframe to "teens navigating complex circumstances" or ask teens what they'd call themselves.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **"A program for teens who want adult mentorship" shifts the emotional register from deficit to aspiration; small wording, massive difference.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Replace "at-risk" everywhere; let teens supply the language ("a space where an adult cares about your actual life").** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Rename to "The Youth Connection Program"; start intake with "tell me what you didn't put on paper," centering agency not the referral reason.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Write and publish a plain faith-neutrality statement, then make the welcome real, because the feeling precedes the policy.** All four perspectives converge: a one-page statement ("This program is hosted by a church and welcomes teens of all faiths and none; religious practice is not required") is necessary but insufficient. The teen feels "is this for me?" before reading anything, so the welcome must be visible: secular framing, diverse mentors, no religious materials in the meeting space, no proselytizing.
  * **State transparently in recruitment and referral agreements that all faiths and none are welcome and religion is not required; get legal review of referral agreements.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Write a one-page statement, post it, read it at orientation, and mean it; transparency alone reduces anxiety, but visible signals must back the words.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **A faith-lens clarity document with real scenarios (teen asks about religion, mentor's faith vs. teen's atheism) removes legal ambiguity for secular families and referrers.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **"Faith is our home; it's not your requirement" said on the website, in intake, and again, becomes the permission to trust.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Define "faith-neutral delivery" operationally, or mentors will improvise and some teens will experience proselytizing.** Two perspectives flag that without explicit rules, "neutral" means nothing in practice. Write the boundary: mentors do not initiate religious conversation; if asked, they share briefly and redirect to the teen; no religious materials or activities are part of the program; review this in training.
  * **Without an operational definition, mentors improvise and some teens experience proselytizing; write explicit guidelines and review them in training.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A one-page faith-lens document with concrete scenarios trains the boundary so it survives contact with real situations.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Recruit mentors who mirror the community, and notice who is invisibly filtered out.** Three perspectives converge: a congregation homogeneous in race and class recruits mentors who reflect that homogeneity, and the program ends up serving the at-risk teens whose families can navigate intake rather than the most at-risk. Intentionally recruit for diversity and partner with secular, immigrant, and LGBTQ+ organizations for referrals.
  * **Ask which families didn't show up to orientation; mirrored identity (a justice-involved mentor, an LGBTQ+ adult) tells the teen "people like you stayed."** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Partner with immigrant centers, LGBTQ+ youth groups, and secular nonprofits for referrals so the cohort isn't faith-gatekept or skewed to system-involved youth.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Diverse leadership and genuine partnership with community organizations turn the faith context into stability rather than a barrier.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **The civil rights exposure is concrete: a publicly-referred teen placed in a faith program without informed consent is a lawsuit and trust failure waiting to happen.** Two perspectives independently surface this as a live legal and reputational risk, not an abstraction. Prevention is transparency before placement: counselors tell families upfront, consent forms are clear, and a secular opt-out exists.
  * **Publicly-referred youth in a faith-directed program raises a civil rights question; get legal review of referral agreements to test exposure.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A non-Christian family complying because the school recommended it, then a teen feeling alienated, is the setup for a lawsuit; prevent with transparency before placement and a secular-mentor opt-out.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Train mentors to recognize microaggressions, because well-meaning comments repeatedly tell a teen they don't belong.** Single-persona insight: "You're so articulate" to a Black teen or "Where are you really from?" to an immigrant teen accumulate into exclusion; include bias awareness with specific examples and a safe channel for teens to flag unwelcoming moments.
  * **Include microaggression recognition in training with specific examples and a reporting mechanism for teens who felt stereotyped.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Audit how teens are portrayed in program imagery, not just whether they appear.** Single-persona reframe: if every photo shows mentees looking grateful or struggling, the message is "only broken people are here"; showing teens proud, defiant, or joyful changes who decides the program is for them.
  * **Photos showing mentees proud and joyful, not just grateful or struggling, shift the message from "for broken kids" to "for young people who deserve more."** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

### Question 4: The invisible logistics problem

**Full question**: What practical barriers — bus routes, after-school jobs, childcare for younger siblings, lack of a phone — might cause a teen to miss meetings without ever explaining why, and how does the program design catch and address these before they read as "disengagement"?

* **Treat the first missed meeting as a signal to investigate, never as evidence of disengagement.** All four perspectives converge on a tiered, curious response: after one missed meeting the mentor reaches out with warmth ("We missed you, everything okay, want to adjust anything?") rather than judgment. The program, as the powerful institution, absorbs the flexibility cost rather than placing accountability on the teen.
  * **Tiered response: first miss, reach out with curiosity; second, problem-solve; third, decide together whether to pause and restart rather than terminate.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A warm text from a neutral person after one missed meeting signals "absence is a problem to solve, not a judgment," which the teen feels as care.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **A missed-meeting protocol that investigates: mentor texts the teen (not parent) after one miss, staff calls after two, so the real barrier surfaces.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **The mentor thinks disengagement; the teen is thinking logistics; design the system to ask "what days and times actually work with your life?"** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Screen for transportation, jobs, sibling care, and phone access at intake, then flex the schedule and location to the teen's real geography.** All four perspectives converge: a teen who needs two buses for a 7pm meeting, works after school, watches younger siblings, or has no reliable phone faces logistics problems, not motivation problems. Map these openly at intake and co-design solutions (meet near home or a bus stop, Saturday breakfast instead of weeknight, occasional sibling inclusion, multiple contact pathways).
  * **Ask "how do we reach you?" and accept multiple answers; work through trusted intermediaries for teens without stable housing or a phone.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Map transportation reality into matching; offer bus passes, meet halfway, name sibling care directly so shame becomes collaboration.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Build a transportation screening into intake; co-design solutions (pickup, meet near a bus stop, virtual check-ins, Saturday mornings) so no teen guesses alone.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **"You work Wednesday nights — what about Saturday breakfast?"; build meetings into the teen's existing geography rather than the other way around.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Budget a small teen-controlled stipend to dissolve micro-barriers.** Single-persona operational insight: $5-10 per teen per month (roughly $600-1,200 annually for a cohort of 10-15) for a transit card, phone top-up, or food before a meeting removes friction and signals the program is responsive to real life.
  * **A teen-controlled gift card "for coffee, a bus pass, or whatever you need to make meetings work" removes one micro-barrier per month.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Design contact around the resource the teen actually has, not the one you assume.** Single-persona reframe: rather than pathologizing a teen whose phone fails, ask "how do you usually know when to meet?" — a fixed same-day-same-time ritual, a call to the home phone, a paper calendar — so the system adapts instead of reading absence as unreliability.
  * **Ask "how do you usually know when to meet?" and build a reliable ritual (same day, same time, or a Monday home-phone call) instead of assuming texting works.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Reframe a responsible teen's absences as life, not failure.** Single-persona insight worth preserving: a teen showing up less because they took a job to help the family or are caring for siblings is "a teen living their actual life," and training mentors to see it that way prevents the program from punishing responsibility.
  * **"This teen is showing up less because they're being responsible — that's not a failure of mentorship," reframes the mentor's read of missed meetings.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

### Question 5: The crossing-the-threshold moment

**Full question**: Imagine a 15-year-old, referred by her school counselor, walking into her first meeting with a mentor she has never met — what does that room look like, who is in it, and what happens in the first ten minutes that makes her decide whether this is worth her time?

* **Her nervous system is scanning for safety; the first ten minutes must hand her control through small choices.** All four perspectives converge: she is assessing exits, the mentor's body language, and whether this is a trap while the program is thinking about agenda. The antidote is micro-agency — where to sit, what to drink, whether to stand — delivered by a calm mentor who sits at her level, names the awkwardness, and follows her lead on greeting.
  * **The mentor makes themselves small and accessible, sits at her level, says "I know you might not want to be here, that's okay."** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A human greeting and a micro-choice ("water? sit here or stand?") signals her agency matters; couch says conversation, desk says evaluation.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **A teen-centered entry ritual: greeted by name, one low-pressure question, the teen chooses the seat, the mentor arrives a beat late so she isn't waiting.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **The mentor stands when she enters, offers a fist-bump or handshake following her lead, and opens with "I know this might feel weird, should we sit?"** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **State explicitly that she can say no or leave, because being asked if she wants to stay is often the moment she decides to.** Three perspectives converge: a referred teen believes she has to be there, and naming her actual choice ("you can tap out, we can find a different mentor, no pressure to stay the full hour") dissolves the trapped feeling and paradoxically increases engagement.
  * **"We'll try this, but if it doesn't feel right you can tell me; you're not locked in" gives emotional permission to be honest.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Tell the teen "we have an hour, but if you're done earlier we can end"; agency over duration often leads to longer engagement.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **"Do you want to be here? That's fair. But if you want to give it one more shot, I'm in" — agency, not recruitment.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **Let the teen tell the first story; the mentor listens for strengths, not problems.** Three perspectives converge: the first real question should invite the teen to choose what she shares ("What do you like to do?" or "What's something nobody knows about you?") rather than interrogating her circumstances, and the mentor's genuine listening is the proof of concept that she is worth attention.
  * **The mentor learns what she cares about, what she's good at, one thing that made her smile — genuine curiosity, not therapy.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **"What do you like to do?" not "tell me about your family problems"; the teen decides what to share, and being really heard is what makes it feel like possibility.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **After small talk, one question that matters ("what would you do next year if you could do anything?"); the mentor actually listens instead of planning a response.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **The room's sensory details quietly answer "am I an inconvenience?"** Two perspectives converge: a hypervigilant teen reads natural light, a snack, a lived-in (not sterile) space, and a prepared, waiting mentor as evidence that her comfort was considered before she arrived.
  * **A space free of stock "believe in yourself" posters, with natural light and an easy exit, communicates this is for her.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Tea or a snack, natural light, a book or game the mentor brought; a space thought through answers "does the adult care about my comfort?" without words.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **First meetings should be one-on-one, with the mentor calm and okay with silence.** Single-persona insight: a supervisor in the room creates a two-adults-against-one power imbalance, and a mentor trying too hard to be "cool" signals inauthenticity; the only agenda is getting to know her.
  * **First meetings are one-on-one only; the mentor is trained to be calm, curious, and comfortable with silence rather than performing.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **The mentor sharing something real and slightly vulnerable invites the teen to risk being real too.** Single-persona operational insight: 2-3 minutes of the mentor naming an actual hobby or current struggle ("I'm learning Spanish, kind of badly") breaks the fake-interest detection that makes teens shut down, and a tangible takeaway card reinforces that something real happened.
  * **The mentor talks 2-3 minutes about a real hobby or struggle; a takeaway card with contact info and one sentence about what they discussed signals follow-through.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

### Question 6: Strip the church entirely

**Full question**: Imagine the program existed with exactly the same teens, mentors, and funding — but with no faith-based organizational home. What would change? What would the teens who were referred through juvenile justice gain or lose? Which elements of the faith-based context are genuine assets versus implicit barriers to entry for specific youth?

* **Stripping the church widens access for the teens you most want to reach, at a real cost to volunteers and funding; the resolution is structural separation, not dissolution.** All four perspectives converge on the same trade-off and the same recommended path: a secular framing removes the perception barrier for secular families, LGBTQ+ teens, and justice-referred youth (one estimate: a 30-40% larger cohort) and eliminates the civil rights exposure, but it costs the congregational volunteer base, in-kind support, and a built-in funding story. The synthesis across all four is to keep the church as host, fiscal sponsor, and space provider while making the program legally and operationally distinct and secular in presentation.
  * **Don't dissolve the church; separate the program organizationally into an independent nonprofit hosted at the church but legally distinct, protecting secular reputation while keeping resources.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Stripping faith removes ambient anxiety for marginalized-religion and LGBTQ+ families; the trade-off is congregation members feeling the program is no longer "theirs."** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **Pilot one cohort in secular space, measure outcomes and referral volume, then decide if the loss of congregational support is offset by reach.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Redesign visibility (less faith messaging, more secular framing) to keep stability while widening welcome, or accept the trade-off and build secular from scratch.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **A secular home would diversify the mentor pool and deepen justice-system trust, the two assets the congregation cannot fully provide.** Three perspectives converge: faith-based recruiting yields reliable, vetted, demographically homogeneous volunteers, while neighborhood recruiting (teachers, "made it out and came back" adults, formerly incarcerated mentors) brings cultural resonance and street credibility that justice-referred teens read as independence from the system that referred them.
  * **A secular program recruits intentionally from the neighborhoods served — community centers, immigrant organizations, LGBTQ+ groups — making mentor diversity a goal, not an accident.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **The volunteer pool becomes more diverse and reflective of the actual community; teens feel "mentors come from all kinds of backgrounds, not just church people."** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*
  * **An advisory board including formerly incarcerated people and public defenders signals "we're independent, here for you, not the system" to justice-referred teens.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*

* **The faith context shapes mentor culture toward a "serving the needy" power imbalance you may not notice; audit the training language regardless of the institutional home.** Two perspectives converge: church-based programs often carry redemption and service framing that positions mentorship as "helping someone in need" rather than mutual growth, and that subtle imbalance survives unless the curriculum is deliberately rewritten.
  * **Audit and remove "serving the needy" and "making a difference in a life" language; replace with "building mutual relationships," which changes outcomes.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Without faith, mentor experience shifts from "serving" to "showing up"; neither good nor bad, but some mentors thrive and others struggle without the faith-based purpose.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*

* **Build teen-safety reporting independent of organizational leadership, because a church may prioritize reputation over transparency.** Single-persona insight that reframes the safety question: churches often resolve misconduct privately to protect harmony, so teens need an external party to report to, and this matters more than whether the home is a church or a nonprofit.
  * **Establish a misconduct-reporting structure independent of leadership so teens can report to an external party; this matters more than church-vs-nonprofit.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **A secular program loses the redemptive "you are more than your mistakes" framework that some justice-referred teens genuinely need.** Single-persona reframe worth preserving: the faith context's redemption narrative is an asset for a teen who needs to believe they can start over, and a secular program must intentionally rebuild and communicate that message rather than assuming it transfers.
  * **Justice-referred teens may worry less about coercion in a secular program but lose the forgiving, redemptive framework; a secular program must rebuild "you can start over" deliberately.** Original detail from The Empath. *—The Empath*

* **Consider holding both faith and secular rather than choosing, with clear opt-in for those who want faith elements.** Single-persona reframe: instead of a binary, design different mentors for different teens and a transparent opt-in to faith elements, accepting more management complexity in exchange for a more genuine welcome across the community's actual religious landscape.
  * **Don't choose between faith and secular; design the program to hold both with clear opt-in, more complex to manage but more genuinely welcoming.** Original detail from The Storyteller. *—The Storyteller*
