## Synthesized Insights by Question

### Question 1: Layered consent with contested guardianship and juvenile-justice power dynamics

**Full question** [User Q]: Given that participants are minors ages 13–17, what layered consent framework — distinguishing between initial program enrollment consent, photo/media release, data collection authorization, and ongoing participation acknowledgment — must the organization obtain from parents or legal guardians, what happens when guardianship is contested, shared, or held by the state through juvenile justice involvement, and how do we obtain genuinely informed consent from teens referred through juvenile justice given the power dynamics and the privacy stakes of their records (recognizing that requiring parental consent may inadvertently exclude the most at-risk youth from households with the least parental capacity)?

* **Build consent as separate, granular tracks rather than a single signature, with media and data-sharing authorizations kept distinct and optional.** All four personas converge here: enrollment consent should be the minimum viable agreement, with photo/media release and data-collection authorization broken out as separate, opt-in forms that the program can run without. The strong shared recommendation is to default justice-referred teens to zero public media use unless they proactively opt in, and to store the authorizations separately so a refusal on one does not block participation.
  * **Media/data separation prevents surveillance creep; refusal on photos is non-negotiable.** Create separate authorizations for participation, photos (optional), and evaluator data-sharing (name who, what fields, how long); store separately from program files. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Media release is a separate vulnerability being underestimated.** A single photo on a website or annual report can expose a teen's participation to peers, employers, or untrustworthy household members; require granular media consent distinct from program consent and default to zero public media for justice-referred teens. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Layered framework with reassent milestones.** Initial enrollment form + annual reassent + fresh consent triggered by major activity changes (overnight trips, data sharing outside program). *—The Lawyer*
  * **Legally-distinct media/data authorization, deferrable to week two.** Each optional; program continues without them; enrollment consent is minimum viable, with media and research forms deferred until relationship trust forms. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Create an explicit alternative-consent pathway so unreachable guardians do not silently exclude the most isolated youth.** All four personas treat the exclusion risk named in the question as real and solvable through a documented proxy mechanism rather than by chasing absent guardians. The convergent design: where a guardian is incarcerated, homeless, or unreachable despite documented good-faith effort, a known adult (school counselor, caseworker, named standby guardian, or state-agency representative) can authorize enrollment, with the decision-maker and rationale recorded in every case.
  * **Establish proxy authority; the teen's agreement matters more than a signature.** Work with districts and juvenile justice so a counselor or caseworker who knows the teen acts as informed-consent witness, with clear documentation. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Tiered model: teen assent + counselor confirmation of legal authority rather than chasing absent guardians.** For state-involved teens, work directly with the juvenile justice system's legal representative to pre-authorize standard program activities. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Written exclusion-risk policy with executive sign-off.** State-agency rep, court-appointed advocate, or guardian ad litem can consent; for unreachable parents of 16–17-year-olds, document good-faith effort and have the executive director or board chair authorize an alternative pathway. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Waiver pathway for genuinely incapacitated guardians (15+).** After documented reasonable effort, empower the executive director to seek consent from a teen-named standby guardian; document every waiver for board oversight and year-one legal review. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Separate the referral conversation from the enrollment conversation, and state in writing that participation is not a condition of probation, to keep coerced "consent" from being legally void.** Three personas treat the power dynamic as something that must be engineered around, not papered over. A neutral adult (not the referring authority) should conduct enrollment; the consent document should explicitly disclaim that participation is mandated, because a teen who believes refusal signals non-compliance has not genuinely consented.
  * **Power-neutral language and a neutral-adult script break the coercive frame.** A neutral adult, not law enforcement or the referring official, reads the offer ("You choose whether to join"); record that the teen chose. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Temporal and emotional distance signals consent is optional.** Separate the referral conversation (counselor/PO) from the enrollment conversation (neutral program staff, referring authority absent). *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A "Probation Officer Notification" form, not a consent form, keeps the power dynamic transparent.** State explicitly that participation is voluntary and not a probation condition; if a teen believes it is mandated, any consent is coerced and legally void. *—The Lawyer*

* **Make consent revocation reversible and teen-initiated, with ongoing assent checkpoints rather than a one-time signature.** Two personas reframe consent as a living process: a teen who cannot withdraw without convincing a guardian is back inside the original power imbalance. Allow teens (roughly 15+) to pause or end the match independently with guardian notification but not veto, and structure low-friction reassent checkpoints (initial, ~90 days, exit).
  * **Consent revocation must be reversible and teen-initiated.** Allow teens 15+ to pause or end the relationship independently, with guardian notification but not veto. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Ongoing assent checkpoints (initial, 90-day, exit) double as a retention filter.** Framing as checkpoints rather than re-consent reduces bureaucracy and refreshes permission if custody changes mid-year. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Establish guardianship status as a documented intake field before collecting any consent.** Two personas stress that you cannot route consent correctly until you know who legally holds it. Build the intake form as a decision tree that determines guardianship (parent, grandparent, state agency, shared custody) and routes to the correct track, getting written clarity from the counselor or officer where guardianship is contested or in transition.
  * **Guardianship determination protocol with a "guardianship status" field.** Get written clarity from the counselor or officer where contested; state-agency custody requires the agency's designated representative, not a biological parent. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Tiered consent structure encoded as a routing decision tree.** Standard dual-parent, single-custodial, and state-custody tracks built into one intake form; measure enrollment drops by track to find barriers. *—The Pragmatist*

### Question 2: Juvenile-record confidentiality and data minimization

**Full question**: When teens are referred through juvenile justice channels, what restrictions do state juvenile-record confidentiality statutes place on what information school counselors and juvenile justice officers may legally share with program staff, what is the minimum data the program must collect to operate effectively, and how should retention policies be structured so that sensitive information doesn't migrate outside its lawful disclosure boundary or create a private record that outlasts the legal protections governing the official one?

* **Get a written legal opinion on your state's specific juvenile-confidentiality statute before accepting a single referral, including your program's legal classification.** All four personas lead with this; convergence is total. Statutes vary radically by state, counselors and officers frequently over-share out of uncertainty, and whether you are a "treatment provider," "community partner," or something else determines what you may legally receive. The practical version is a paid attorney memo distributed to referral partners alongside your intake paperwork.
  * **Learn the boundaries first, build policy second.** Have a lawyer review what can legally be shared and what flows are illegal without the minor's consent before accepting any referral. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Obtain a written legal opinion on your organization's statutory classification.** Whether you are a "treatment provider," "community-service partner," or other determines everything you can legally handle. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **These records are state-governed, not FERPA/HIPAA/COPPA.** Consult your state Public Defender Association or a youth-advocacy firm; counselors can share that a teen "meets eligibility criteria" but generally not why. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Map the statute in writing for 5–8 billable hours; attach the memo to policies.** A one-page memo clarifies boundaries for both program and referral source, who often over-share out of uncertainty. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Collect only mentor-critical operational data; explicitly refuse offense history, case numbers, and family detail.** All four converge on a tight whitelist (name/nickname, age, contact, interests, match-relevant safety flags) and an explicit blacklist (arrest record, charges, probation officer name, psychological diagnoses, family criminal history). The shared mechanism for unavoidable sensitive context, such as a court-ordered curfew, is to receive it verbally from the referrer and never write it into program records.
  * **A mentor doesn't need the charge; it doesn't change mentoring.** Referral forms with explicit checkboxes; skip fields that don't inform mentoring. Fewer details means less to leak or subpoena. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Information migration is the silent killer.** Useful context ("lives with grandparent") should be stored without the explanation; teach mentors to ask teens directly rather than rely on counselor notes. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Hard do-not-collect list; safety facts received verbally only.** Collect interests, goals, disclosed safety issues; train mentors to receive things like a curfew verbally and not record them. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Referrer voices sensitive context by phone; record only the mentor-critical summary.** The teen's file contains only referral source, consent docs, and mentor submissions. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Structure retention to mirror the legal lifecycle so your file never becomes a shadow record outlasting the sealed official one, and enforce it technically.** All four name the "shadow/shadow-record" risk explicitly and prescribe time-bounded purging (commonly match duration plus six months to two years). The Devil's Advocate adds the enforcement teeth the others imply: a written policy without auto-deletion is fantasy, and spreadsheets or paper are a red flag because retention cannot be enforced at scale by hand.
  * **Destroy records ~3 years after the relationship ends; document only program-goal progress, not behavioral observations.** This respects the legal presumption the teen has moved forward. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Retention policies without technical enforcement are fantasy.** Cap retention to match plus 6 months; auto-delete; backups not restorable without executive approval; paper/spreadsheets are a red flag. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Mirror the statute (active + ~2 years), then delete with a one-line log.** Prevents your records from becoming a shadow history that outlasts protections on the official record. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Purge after exit + two years; document the purge.** A thin auto-purged file is also a legitimate basis to decline records requests ("we no longer hold that file"). *—The Pragmatist*

* **Pre-negotiate disclosure boundaries with referral sources in writing, and have a subpoena protocol that distinguishes a subpoena from a court order.** The Lawyer supplies two distinctive mechanisms the others gesture at: a one-page data-governance memo telling referral sources exactly what you will and will not accept, and a subpoena protocol noting that many states require a court order (not merely a subpoena) to reach juvenile records, so you should route requests to counsel and produce only basic participation data while redacting sensitive observations.
  * **Do not auto-comply with a subpoena; many states require a court order.** Route to counsel, produce dates/mentor assigned while redacting observations, document every disclosure. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Written data-governance agreement with referral sources.** A one-page memo states what you request, how you use and store it, retention, and what you will not accept (arrest records, case summaries, psych files). *—The Lawyer*

* **Separate sensitive data from general files and audit access.** Two personas add storage-architecture discipline beyond the retention clock. Keep pairing notes separate from any background context, split physical and digital storage with tightly limited access, and log who touched a justice-linked file, when, and why, so a later leak can be shown to be accidental rather than negligent.
  * **Access audit trail makes confidentiality monitored, not hoped-for.** Log who accessed a justice-linked file, when, and why; teens trust a program that monitors confidentiality. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Separate physical and digital storage with limited access.** Locked cabinet for originals; password-protected folder for notes; mentors submit via a form, not email chains. *—The Pragmatist*

### Question 3: Referral source diversity, institutional load-shifting, counselor incentives, and a shared "at-risk" standard

**Full question**: If the program relies primarily on school counselors and juvenile justice referrals, what feedback loops emerge — does early success with one channel make staff increasingly dependent on it, gradually narrowing who the program reaches, and as the program proves effective, what prevents referring institutions from increasing volume faster than capacity can absorb (institutional load-shifting that quietly degrades quality)? Will counselors and probation officers refer the teens they most want to help, the teens easiest to manage, or the teens they most want to pass off — and given the two referring systems apply different thresholds of risk (schools' chronic absenteeism vs. juvenile justice's prior involvement), how will the program define and document a unified eligibility standard with clear authority over its application?

* **Cap capacity explicitly and make selectivity public so referral pressure cannot quietly convert into quality drift.** All four converge: state a hard maximum (around 20 matches), move to a transparent waitlist when full, and let referral partners see that selectivity. The Devil's Advocate sharpens it to publishing an acceptance rate; the others frame it as quotas per channel. The shared logic is that a full program under pressure starts accepting inappropriate matches unless the cap is visible and enforced.
  * **Maximum capacity + public waitlist referral partners can see.** Quarterly waitlist review signals "we're not taking everyone," protecting teens from an overstretched program. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Publish your acceptance rate ("we accept 20% of referrals").** Prevents referring staff from inflating volume past what you can serve well. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Written referral policy: no single channel above 40%; rotate recruitment.** Cap mentors per quarter, then waitlist; document source, volume, and risk profile quarterly. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Stated cap (20) with per-system quotas (up to 8 each, plus 4 self-referrals).** Closed at cap until a match ends; transparency will irritate an over-eager source but protects quality. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Co-author one unified, written eligibility standard with both systems and assign a single named authority to apply it.** All four converge on resolving the two-threshold problem (absenteeism vs. prior involvement) with a written rubric both systems sign, plus a clear exclusion of teens needing intensive clinical/forensic care first. The distinctive addition across personas is naming who decides: an executive director or intake coordinator signs off on every referral against the standard, giving the program enforceable authority rather than deferring to each system's own criteria.
  * **Define "at-risk" with the community, codify decision rules, audit for drift quarterly.** Listening session with youth, counselors, justice staff; surface drift data and renegotiate jointly, not unilaterally. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **One operational gate regardless of source.** Inclusion criteria (age, availability, no active substance-use disorder, no active violent ideation) become the shared gate; otherwise you serve two different populations without realizing it. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Unified written definition with executive sign-off on every referral.** A multi-criterion "at-risk" definition plus designated intake authority prevents drift and holds partners accountable. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Co-author a one-page rubric both systems sign; you are not diversion or therapy.** Age, attendance/structure, no active substance treatment, no credible threat history; explicit non-substitution prevents "throw the kid you're tired of managing." *—The Pragmatist*

* **Build a quarterly outcomes-by-source feedback loop and tie continued referral privileges to it, to realign misaligned counselor and officer incentives.** All four name the incentive problem directly: counselors are rewarded for offloading the easiest absentees, probation officers for completion stats, and neither has a stake in year-two match success. The shared fix is transparency that forces accountability: quarterly reports on match quality, duration, and outcomes by referral source. The Devil's Advocate and Lawyer go furthest, conditioning continued referral access on meeting minimum standards.
  * **Audit the motivation behind each referral and give counselors permission to say no.** A referral-conversation template ("would this teen be in your top three?") surfaces incentive misalignment; a bad referral wastes capacity. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Condition continued referral privileges on minimum outcome standards.** Quarterly reports on match quality and duration force accountability on both sides. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Reframe the incentive so partners stop treating you as a dumping ground.** Quarterly outcome reports, public recognition, and joint capacity planning lead partners to self-regulate. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Share anonymized outcome data tied to referral patterns as a lever.** "85% completed three months; 60% of breakups cited scheduling" incentivizes appropriate referrals. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Diversify deliberately beyond the school–probation duopoly with a self-referral and community pathway.** Three personas treat over-reliance on two institutional channels as the structural root of the load-shifting and incentive problems. Adding libraries, recreation centers, faith and youth-led groups, and a self-referral form gives teens an entry through places they already trust and creates outside accountability on the program itself.
  * **Partner with libraries, rec centers, faith and youth-led groups for natural accountability.** A teen referred through a trusted place arrives with different energy; if a partner stops referring, that's a signal worth investigating. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Rotate recruitment so no single channel dominates.** Diversification is a documented, conscious quarterly practice. *—The Lawyer*
  * **A parallel self-referral pathway with a modest incentive breaks the duopoly.** An online form via libraries and rec centers, plus a $50 gift card for a successful match, adds 2–3 matches/year with minimal overhead. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Treat sudden referral-volume spikes as a load-shifting alarm, not validation, and freeze intake to investigate.** This is a load-bearing single-persona reframe. The Devil's Advocate argues that rising volume usually signals a structural change at the referring institution (counselor funding cuts, probation caseload surges) rather than program success, and prescribes a "referral shock" protocol: a spike over ~20% quarter-over-quarter triggers an intake freeze until the cause is understood. The others manage capacity but do not name the spike itself as a diagnostic signal to act on.
  * **A "referral shock" protocol: freeze new intakes when volume spikes >20% QoQ.** Track the stability and composition of referral streams; a 40% jump is usually a structural change you had nothing to do with, not validation. *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 4: Cross-system intake as a warm handoff

**Full question**: School counselors and juvenile justice coordinators each have distinct relational authority with a teen — how might the program design its intake process to borrow from the "warm handoff" model used in healthcare transitions, where the referring professional personally introduces the mentee to the program rather than passing paperwork alone?

* **Have the referring professional personally introduce the teen to the mentor in person, vouch briefly, then step out; design the moment, do not leave it to paperwork.** All four converge on a structured introduction where the trusted adult is physically present at the first meeting, frames the mentor, and departs, with a remote (Zoom) variant for tight schedules. This is treated as a cheap, high-leverage moment that changes the teen's emotional entry point from "stranger to stranger" to "an adult I trust backs this."
  * **The referrer's presence at the first meeting changes everything.** A five-minute introduction and a two-week check-in promise establishes trust transfer; showing up alone signals "you're on your own." *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Split relational and administrative load; presence not required at every stage.** The referrer frames the introduction in a 15-minute conversation; the intake coordinator does logistics. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Three-step handoff with the mentor present at intake.** Counselor previews in advance, attends intake to introduce the mentor, stays looped in for the first month; research shows this moment predicts engagement. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Require the referrer at the first 30-minute meeting; Zoom allowed for POs.** They vouch for both parties briefly, then step out; 30 minutes of their time transfers credibility. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Structure the probation/justice handoff to separate authority from support, with a participation-only update channel back to the officer.** Three personas treat the probation handoff as distinct from the school one because the officer reads as supervisor, not supporter. The shared design: the officer states the program is voluntary with no reporting obligation, program staff take over relationship-building, and the officer receives only a monthly participation update with no clinical notes, so the mentor is visibly not a surveillance agent.
  * **Use a neutral adult, not the referring authority, for the consent-adjacent framing.** A teen referred by an officer hears "you have to"; a neutral introduction breaks that. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Authentic commitment matters; a grudging compliance-box handoff signals "the system doesn't think this will help."** Measure whether referrers actually sit down with the teen vs. hand over a flyer; retrain or retire low-buy-in pathways. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Juvenile-justice handoff variation separates authority from support.** PO states the program is voluntary with no probation reporting; staff send a monthly participation-only update, clarifying the mentor is a supporter, not surveillance. *—The Lawyer*

* **Coach the referrer to make a specific, goal-aligned endorsement rather than a generic one, so the handoff transfers respect and not just a name.** Three personas stress that an unspecific "here's a guy who's gonna help" does not tell the teen what the trusted adult actually thinks. Provide a script that names the teen's goal and the mentor's relevant background ("Mr. Chen used to struggle in school like you and has spent two years helping teens with study strategies"), so the handoff conveys perceived alignment.
  * **A returning-to-counselor checkpoint within a week catches silent dropout.** "How did it go? Do you want to keep going?" lets the teen tell a familiar adult before quitting on a stranger. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Trust transfer is conditional on visible belief alignment; coach specificity.** A goal-specific, background-specific endorsement transfers respect and perceived alignment, not just a referral. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A half-page transition script the referrer reads in private invites the teen's voice.** "This person is trained, background-checked, and I've met them. What questions do you have?" makes the handoff explicit. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Manage referring adults' expectations and instrument the handoff so you can tell which ones worked.** Two personas add the operational scaffolding: a one-page "how to support your teen in mentoring" guide that tells referrers what mentoring is not and how to escalate concerns without inserting themselves, plus a simple post-meeting form capturing whether the referrer attended and the teen's comfort level, so you have evidence to enforce attendance.
  * **A "How to Support Your Teen" guide for referring adults manages expectations.** Names what mentoring does and doesn't provide and routes concerns to the director, not the mentor. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Document the handoff in the mentor's first report.** Referrer present/absent, introduced yes/no, teen comfort 1–5; if skipped-handoff teens drop out faster, you have evidence to enforce the expectation. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Keep system-level coordination open without case-level surveillance, and recognize mentoring lacks clinical urgency.** Two distinctive notes: the Lawyer's structured monthly coordination call deliberately stays at the system level (volume, timing, barriers) to avoid breaching individual confidentiality, and the Devil's Advocate observes that mentoring has no clinical deadline, so the warm handoff can be lightweight, though that same patience is a liability if a teen who can "wait a month" simply never shows.
  * **A system-level monthly coordination call avoids case-level breach.** "We onboarded 4 teens, matching took 2 weeks, no major barriers" keeps systems engaged and surfaces pullback early. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Healthcare's model doesn't transpose directly; mentoring has no clinical urgency.** The lack of deadline is also a liability: paperwork-only referral means the teen may never show; design a lightweight handoff to counter it. *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 5: Referral as a trust-transfer mechanism

**Full question**: In franchise models, brand trust transfers from the franchisor to a new location the moment a customer walks in; there's a structural parallel here with referral — how might the program design the mentor-matching step so that the trust a teen has in their school counselor or parole officer actually transfers forward into their first interaction with their mentor?

* **Match on the relational dimensions the teen actually values, gathered from the referrer (and the teen), so the mentor inherits relational context rather than a bureaucratic handoff.** All four converge that trust transfers through demonstrated alignment, not a transferred form. Ask the referring professional, with the teen's knowledge, what the teen values in trusted people (humor, reliability, shared interests, similar trajectory) and match on those; the Pragmatist operationalizes this as a "trust map" built at intake.
  * **The mentor demonstrates they already listened to the referrer.** The mentor picks up a specific thread ("you're into cars?"); trust transfers through presence plus immediate demonstrated listening, not the handoff itself. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Inherit relational context by matching on what the teen values in trusted people.** Ask the referrer what the teen values; match on humor, reliability, shared interests, similar trajectory. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Activity-selection autonomy signals the trust transfer.** Genuine early choice ("basketball, cooking, a project you care about") makes the program a space the teen has voice in, unlike school or probation. *—The Lawyer*
  * **A "trust map" at intake honors the teen's existing trust architecture.** Ask who the teen trusts and why; the mentor leads with the same quality (e.g., listening without judgment). *—The Pragmatist*

* **Coach the mentor to acknowledge that early trust is borrowed from the referrer rather than to over-correct it or pretend it is already earned.** All four reach the same posture: name the borrowed trust honestly ("Ms. Chen thought we'd work well together; let's see if she was right"), set the bar as proving the referrer's judgment sound, and expect trust to mature over 4–6 weeks from "my counselor trusts this person" to "I trust this person."
  * **Honor borrowed trust; don't be falsely modest or rush to earn "real" trust.** "Let's see if she was right" sets an honest bar; trust matures over weeks into the teen's own. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **The first 4–6 weeks are about building autonomous trust, not productivity.** A mentor-training module reframes the mentee initially seeing the mentor as an extension of the system. *—The Lawyer*
  * **A 20-second acknowledgment transfers and validates the referrer's trust.** "I know [referrer] recommended me; I'm grateful for their trust in us both" sets the mentor as trustworthy by respecting, not competing with, the original relationship. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Trust framing by the referrer shapes expectations for better or worse.** "Part of your probation requirements" vs. "someone I think you'd genuinely like" produces opposite teen expectations from the same mentor; train referrers on framing. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Make the mentor's distinct, independent role explicit and back it with transparent confidentiality boundaries and referral-partner restraint.** Three personas argue the trust transfer completes only when the teen sees the mentor is genuinely independent of the system. State plainly what the mentor is not (not a PO, not a counselor), disclose exactly what breaks confidentiality up front, and train referral partners to step back to quarterly aggregate updates so the teen does not sense ongoing institutional surveillance.
  * **Name what the mentor is NOT to reassure justice-involved teens.** "I'm not here to be a probation officer or a counselor" reinforces that the teen is transferring trust to a different kind of relationship. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Transparency on confidentiality boundaries paradoxically deepens trust.** Stating exactly what breaks confidentiality up front lets the teen build trust without surprise. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Referral-partner restraint is the final piece of trust transfer.** A counselor checking in weekly signals surveillance; restraint to quarterly aggregate updates protects the teen's trust in the mentor's independence. *—The Lawyer*

* **Protect against trust collapse and negative transfer: borrowed institutional trust is fragile and can run in reverse.** This is a load-bearing single-persona reframe from the Devil's Advocate, partly corroborated by the Lawyer and Pragmatist on mentor reliability. Borrowed trust is conditional, not blanket; an unreliable mentor does not just fail to earn trust but can collapse the teen's trust in the referrer ("my counselor's judgment isn't as good as I thought"). And a teen burned by a prior mentor carries negative transfer that must be actively reset before they will try again.
  * **Institutional trust wears off the moment the mentor disappoints; enforce mentor reliability.** A mentor missing the first meetings can collapse trust in the referrer; missed meetings need a structured warning and retraining, not a shrug. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Negative transfer is likely if unmanaged; reset expectations explicitly.** A teen burned before views all mentoring with suspicion; "this program is different... if it's not working, we'll find you someone else" can rebuild enough trust to try again. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Mentor standards must be non-negotiable because one person carries the brand.** A 20-hour training (trauma-informed communication, confidentiality, boundaries, crisis response) ensures everyone plays from the same playbook; require mentors to articulate the teen's situation within two meetings. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Keep the referrer as a live "touchstone," coordinating in parallel, rather than severing the original relationship at handoff.** Two personas argue the resilient structure is distributed trust: the mentor checks in with the referrer quarterly ("anything I should know to be a better mentor?"), the referrer celebrates progress, and the teen experiences multiple adults coordinating to support them rather than one mentor replacing the trusted adult.
  * **Multiple adults coordinating makes the teen feel held, not managed.** A visible touchpoint (the counselor texts a reminder or calls after) shows the teen they matter; invite the referrer back quarterly. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Mark the referrer as a "touchstone" contact for the mentor.** A quarterly check-in form keeps the original relationship alive; distributed trust is more resilient than a single-mentor model. *—The Pragmatist*
