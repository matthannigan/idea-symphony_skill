### Question 1: Outcome variable selection, baseline acquisition, and teens' own definitions

**Full question**: Given the program's goals, what are the two or three quantifiable proxies (school attendance delta, disciplinary incident rate, mentor-reported relationship quality score) that are both meaningful and obtainable without burdening participants or requiring data-sharing agreements the organization cannot easily execute, how do we measure outcomes without making mentors feel surveilled or teens feel over-surveyed, what is the realistic pre-program baseline-collection window (with which indicators sourced from school counselors or juvenile justice referrers rather than self-report), and how does the measurement framework include a 15-year-old's definition of "a good year with a mentor" alongside the funder's metrics, treating data collection as something done with participants rather than to them? [User Q]

* **Pull baselines from schools and referral partners, never from the teens themselves.** All four perspectives converge: school counselors and juvenile justice case managers already track attendance, disciplinary incidents, and grades, so request that historical data directly (a 6-month pre-match window is a reasonable cutoff) rather than asking a guarded 15-year-old to fill out a self-assessment. This is cheaper, more accurate, lower-friction, and avoids the dignity cost of asking teens to document their own dysfunction. One dissenting note tempers it: different referrers (counselors vs. justice) apply different "at-risk" thresholds, so cross-cohort baseline comparisons can be misleading and should be read as trends within a cohort, not absolute benchmarks.
  * **Partner with counselors and case managers who already hold this data; teens have earned skepticism about data collection.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Request historical attendance and incident reports from referral partners with a ~6-month pre-match cutoff; faster, more accurate, no resented forms.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Collect baseline attendance from school records before program start; low friction, no new agreements.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Caution: referrer thresholds differ, creating a selection artifact that makes cross-cohort baselines meaningless.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Build teen definitions of "a good year" directly into the measurement framework through co-design.** All four converge on asking teens what success means to them, early and structurally, and treating their answers as real measurement rather than decoration. Convene a youth advisory group or hold a short structured conversation at intake or month 2, capture goals verbatim, and fold them into the mentorship agreement. The recurring caution is to translate soft language ("feeling heard") into observable proxies so it survives funder scrutiny, and to track the gap between teen definitions and funder metrics as actionable intelligence in its own right.
  * **Convene a 5-6 teen advisory group: "What would make this year matter?" Capture verbatim, translate to observable actions; track "voice included" as a core indicator.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **At month 2, hold a 10-minute structured conversation per teen; record themes and use them as outcome proxies. Funder gets metrics; teens get heard.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Co-design at intake: capture 2-3 teen-defined goals into the mentorship agreement; when teen definitions diverge from funder metrics, that gap is actionable.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Teen-defined outcomes risk being too soft for funders; teach teens to quantify their own goals so aspirations bind to concrete proxies they own.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Replace mentor surveys with low-burden reflection that mentors keep.** Three perspectives converge on reframing mentor data collection as reflection rather than reporting: a monthly one-page guide or three-sentence journal that lives in the mentor's hands, plus a periodic 1-10 relationship-solidity rating. The aim is a relationship trend line without surveillance. The adversarial caution is that mentor self-report inflates as emotional investment grows, so it should not stand alone.
  * **Monthly self-directed reflection guide ("What surprised you? What were they proud of?") gives qualitative windows without making mentors data collectors.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **5-minute check-ins at months 3, 6, 12: "How solid is the relationship (1-10)?" and "Any major shifts?" A trend line without paperwork.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Three-sentence monthly journals that stay in the mentor's hands; surveys interrogate, journals illuminate.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Mentor self-report is prone to confirmation bias; add a third-party observer who rates one interaction per quarter as a ground-truth check.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Offer a measurement-light track so deeper data collection never costs the relationship.** Surfaced by one perspective: let mentors choose between a standard track (quarterly check-in plus baseline/midpoint data pull) and a lightweight track (annual reflection plus year-end data only). Over-measurement costs the relationships you are trying to measure.
  * **Two pathways, mentor's choice, because over-measurement costs the relationships you measure.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*

* **Prototype a literacy-light teen instrument before scaling anything.** Surfaced by one perspective: if you collect anything from teens directly, pilot a photo-elicitation or visual-scale tool ("point to where you feel supported") with five teens first. It lowers literacy barriers, increases honesty, and takes five minutes.
  * **Photo-elicitation / visual scales, piloted with 5 teens, reduce literacy barriers and increase honesty.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

### Question 2: Attrition exit data as a feedback signal

**Full question**: When a match ends before the one-year commitment, what structured exit data (reason category, duration, initiating party, antecedent events) should be collected, and how does that data feed back into refining the matching process and identifying systemic program weaknesses?

* **Collect structured, coded exit data from both parties: reason category, duration, initiating party, and a short narrative.** Three perspectives converge on a compact 5-7 field exit form completed independently by mentor and teen, with coded reason categories (schedule conflict, mismatch, mentor unavailability, disengagement, relocation, safety, other) plus duration and a one-sentence narrative. Fed to leadership monthly, this surfaces patterns within six months. One important dissent: tracking "initiating party" can be weaponized against mentors and make them defensive about reporting early warning signs, so the framing must be explicitly non-punitive (capture the category, use only aggregate patterns).
  * **5-7 fields, 10 minutes, both parties independently; spot patterns like "matches break after month 4 with mentors 55+."** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Coded reasons plus initiating party, duration, and a one-sentence narrative from both sides, fed monthly; attrition is a diagnostic, not a failure.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Capture an emotional arc, not just a checkbox reason: "schedule conflict" often masks an unheard or disconnected moment.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Tracking "initiating party" risks weaponizing data against mentors; keep it non-punitive and use only aggregate patterns to improve systems.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Run the teen's exit conversation separately from the mentor, framed as learning rather than blame.** Three perspectives converge: have program staff (not the mentor) hold a low-stakes conversation with the teen ("What would have made this work? What did we miss?"), because teens name micro-issues mentors never perceive. Frame it as relationship repair and program improvement, not as the teen explaining their failure.
  * **Conduct exit as relationship repair, not data extraction; teens tell surprising truths when they aren't blamed.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Program staff (not the mentor) ask the teen what would have made it work; one teen's "he talks about his job the whole time" becomes mentor training.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Two-phase exit: immediate one-question survey plus a 30-day staff follow-up to separate surface narrative from underlying pattern.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Treat attrition as a leading indicator that feeds matching and triggers fast intervention.** Three perspectives converge on reframing exits from shame to early-warning signal: high month-3 attrition tells you about matching, training, or expectations while you can still fix it, and exit themes (geography, age, justice-track) feed the next cohort's matching criteria. The Systems Thinker frames the attrition rate itself as the program's "immune system"; the Pragmatist adds a concrete rescue mechanism for the high-risk window.
  * **40% attrition at month 3 is useful data that keeps you from sinking 12 months into a flawed process; feed themes into rematch criteria.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Plot attrition trends against training and intake changes; a drop signals systemic improvement, a spike is a warning.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Match-rescue protocol: if half of breaks fall in months 2-4, check in with each pair separately before month 4; a 15-minute call can prevent an exit.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Hand-code themes and act on the top two; don't wait for statistical significance you'll never reach.** Surfaced by the adversarial perspective and load-bearing for a 40-volunteer program: with low, variable volume you cannot statistically distinguish "mentors who leave early" from random noise, so code qualitative narratives by hand, focus on the top one or two themes, and flag systemic weakness only after the third or fourth similar case rather than the first exit. This also implies front-loaded rapid-cycle feedback (pause, adjust training, resume) rather than slow endpoint analysis.
  * **Volume is too low for statistical significance; hand-code narratives and act on the top 1-2 themes; flag systemic issues only after the 3rd-4th similar case.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 3: Short-cycle versus long-cycle feedback and the measurement-burden chilling effect

**Full question**: Many meaningful outcomes (educational attainment, justice system involvement, economic stability) manifest over years, while surveys, check-ins, and reporting requirements add friction for both mentors and teens. At what point does the measurement apparatus start degrading the participation it's trying to study, and what shorter-cycle signals function as reliable proxies for long-term outcomes without optimizing for proxies in ways that miss the deeper changes the program actually cares about?

* **Adopt a tiered measurement cadence and reuse data schools already collect, so you add almost no friction.** All four converge: track only viability signals in months 1-3 (did they meet, do they want to continue), add a brief relationship check and reused school data in months 4-12, and save the heavy long-term metrics for the final report. The recurring move is to automate by reusing existing school discipline and attendance records rather than surveying, which puts zero burden on mentors or teens.
  * **Distinguish "staying in the match" from "life improving"; the short-cycle signals are attendance, declining cancellations, deepening conversation, felt naturally.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Tiered approach: months 1-3 track attendance and "want to keep meeting?"; add school data months 4-12; save heavy metrics for the final report.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **One mentor checklist plus one teen touchpoint per month; reuse quarterly school discipline records already collected for other reasons.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Embed one qualitative observation per month into regular mentor meetings rather than deploying formal surveys, and test whether that lowers attrition.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Proxy long-term outcomes with faster-moving behavioral signals, but write down how each proxy could mislead.** Three perspectives converge: instead of waiting five years for recidivism or graduation, track semester disciplinary referrals, course completion, and GPA, which move years earlier at near-zero cost. The adversarial perspective adds the essential guard: for each proxy, document in one sentence how it could be gamed or diverge from the true goal (attendance can signal compliance, not re-engagement), and revisit annually so the easy metric doesn't quietly replace the goal.
  * **Track semester disciplinary referrals and GPA as fast behavioral proxies; spot trajectory shifts years before long-term metrics land.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Reuse school discipline records as an engagement proxy; the gap between "incidents" and "relationship quality" is where mentoring does invisible work.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Proxies are seductive and will replace the goal; for each one, write how it could be gamed and revisit annually.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Freeze measurement in the final quarter so the relationship has room to deepen.** Surfaced by one perspective and reinforced by the chilling-effect framing across the cluster: stop adding forms and surveys around month 9 and let mentors and teens simply be together for the last three months. A cohort with a light final quarter often shows better long-term outcomes because people stop gaming or fatiguing on metrics.
  * **Measurement freeze at month 9; the chilling effect is real, and the lighter final quarter can produce better outcomes.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Meta-measure the apparatus: set a friction floor and kill any metric whose completion rate falls.** Surfaced by the systems perspective: before rollout, declare an acceptable burden ceiling (e.g., 10 min/month for mentors, one question/month for teens), then track completion and response rates; any metric missing completion by more than ~20% is retired. Watch teen-touchpoint response rates and dial frequency back when they drop below ~60%. The measurement system has to survive contact with reality.
  * **Track completion rates and time cost of measurement itself; retire any metric mentors and teens won't actually complete.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **The deeper cost of measurement burden is selection bias, not just fatigue.** Surfaced by the adversarial perspective and load-bearing: heavy reporting is survived disproportionately by already-organized mentors and already-connected youth, so the hardest-to-reach participants drop out under load, the remaining sample becomes unrepresentative, and apparent program success is inflated. The counterintuitive implication is to reduce friction most for the hardest-to-reach (auto-capture through mentors, no youth surveys) and accept noisier data to preserve representativeness.
  * **Heavy measurement selects for the organized and motivated, biasing the sample; cut friction for the hardest-to-reach and accept noisier data.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Let mentor and teen review a shared one-page dashboard together instead of reporting upward.** Surfaced by the adversarial perspective: shared visibility ("we've met 8 times, you mentioned school 6 times, you got a B last month") replaces top-down reporting, lowers burden, and creates two-way accountability.
  * **A jointly reviewed one-page dashboard replaces top-down reporting and improves outcomes without research burden.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 4: Measure failure, not success

**Full question**: What if instead of tracking program outcomes (attendance, graduation rates, "relationship quality") you tracked every moment a mentor or teen wanted to quit but didn't? What would that data tell you that success metrics never could? Resilience and durability in relationships are invisible to outcome measurement but may be the actual product being built.

* **Track "wanted to quit but stayed" moments as core program data, captured in the natural rhythm of mentor check-ins.** All four converge: ask mentors at monthly or quarterly check-ins whether there was a moment they or the teen nearly quit and what kept them in, then log these as resilience data. Across a year these accumulate into a portfolio that says more about impact than attendance rates, because the choosing-to-stay-despite-difficulty is the durable relationship being built.
  * **Ask quarterly "Was there a moment you were ready to step back? What kept you in?" The resilience moments are what standard metrics miss.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Collect near-quit stories in a simple spreadsheet (date, trigger, resolution); the real outcome may be "mentors learned to show up even when teens are hostile."** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Add Y/N near-quit questions for both mentor and teen each month; a match that survives three near-quits has deeper durability than one that cruises.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Capture "I wanted to quit but didn't" as the core metric; these stories show the actual work happening.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*

* **Distinguish genuine resilience from resignation, or the metric lies to you.** Surfaced by the adversarial perspective and load-bearing for the whole "measure failure" frame: a teen who stayed might have built grit, or might have lost hope and seen no point in leaving. The diagnostic question is "what changed your mind?" Answers like "my mentor helped me see a way forward" signal resilience; "I was afraid to tell my mentor" signal resignation wearing resilience as a mask. Without this filter, near-quit counts can mistake quiet despair for durability.
  * **Resilience metrics are meaningless if you can't separate grit from resignation; ask "what changed your mind?" to tell them apart.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Read durability in continuous behavior, because not every fraying is a dramatic near-quit.** Two perspectives converge on a corrective to the near-quit frame: some relationships quietly drift without a visible crisis moment, so track no-shows, reschedule patterns, conversation length, and whether the teen progressively shares more, drawn from mentor observation logs rather than self-report. Durability lives in consistency, not only in surviving acute stress.
  * **Track no-shows, reschedules, and conversation quality continuously; quiet fraying has no dramatic near-quit moment to catch.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Invert success into persistence: "the pair met 90% of sessions despite barriers," "the mentor stuck through a withdrawn month."** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Log repair moves and obstacles-overcome, not just survival.** Three perspectives converge that the observable signal is what people do under strain: a mentor who texts the coordinator instead of quitting, a meeting that happened despite a family crisis, a conflict the pair navigated together. Build a narrative log of a few critical conflicts per cohort and how they resolved, because navigating friction (not avoiding it) is where transformation happens.
  * **Track repair moves: reaching out to other mentors, calling the coordinator, talking to the teen about the strain; that's how relationships regenerate.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **An "obstacle overcome" metric: scheduled meetings that faced obstacles but happened anyway captures grit standard metrics miss.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **A "resilience library" of anonymized near-failure stories shows new mentors that successful matches weather storms.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Pair durability inside the match with small risk-taking outside it, so you don't reward a low-demand match.** Surfaced by the adversarial perspective: you can preserve a match by lowering expectations, but that isn't building resilience for the wider world. Ask whether the teen tried something new (raised a hand in class, joined a club, was vulnerable with a peer); that is where in-match durability bleeds into real life change.
  * **Pair "durability in the match" with "small risk-taking outside the match," or you mistake a low-demand match for resilience.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Listen for what the teen sacrifices to keep showing up.** Surfaced by one perspective: a teen who chooses the weekly meeting over something they wanted to do is exercising the exact resilience being built ("my cousin was visiting but I figured you'd be disappointed if I canceled"). Asking "what was hard about coming today, and why did you anyway?" surfaces it.
  * **Ask what teens sacrifice to attend; their answers reveal the resilience being built.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*

### Question 5: What if outcomes were designed to be felt, not counted

**Full question**: What if we completely abandoned the standard outcome measurement frameworks (attendance, grades, recidivism) and designed a program whose primary evidence of success was qualitative transformation visible to the teens themselves and the community around them? What entirely new forms of witnessing and documentation would we need to invent?

* **Make structured community witnessing the primary qualitative evidence.** All four converge: recruit the people already around the teen (mentor, family, school counselor, peers, community elders) to notice and document specific observed shifts, then triangulate across witnesses. The recurring discipline is to make this rigorous rather than anecdotal: assign a set of anchors per teen, ask the same question quarterly ("What change have you noticed?"), and look for consensus, so you capture witnessed transformation instead of cherry-picked stories.
  * **Invite teachers, counselors, and family to observe and write reflections, then share them back with the teen.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Quarterly "open gathering" where the community witnesses each teen's story; recorded as a qualitative evidence archive.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Collect 5-10 narrative snapshots per teen, triangulated by mentor, parent, counselor, and staff.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Without structure you cherry-pick; recruit five anchors per teen and ask each quarterly, then look for consensus on the shift.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Center the teen's own voice as evidence through first-person narrative capture.** Three perspectives converge on collecting teens' own accounts of their change (a written, voice-memo, or video answer to "tell the story of a moment you felt different this year"), kept unscored as a library of real transformation. This both produces richer evidence than any external measure and embodies the dignity principle of treating the teen as the authority on their own growth.
  * **Teen-authored impact narratives near year-end honor the teen as the authority on their own growth.** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **A 2-3 minute teen voice memo at 6 and 12 months ("what's different about you this year?"), collected, not scored.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Six-month youth panels surface transformation through group dynamic that no survey captures.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*

* **Document observable shifts in mindset and presence rather than abstract transformation.** Three perspectives converge on capturing concrete, witnessed micro-changes ("he used to say 'I'm just like my dad,' now he says 'I'm trying something different'"; from silent to laughing; from defensive to curious) as the actual unit of qualitative evidence. These presence and mindset shifts often precede measurable outcomes and are what the community can actually see.
  * **Replace attendance with presence: what did the teen do differently in meetings over time?** Original detail from The Audience Advocate. *—The Audience Advocate*
  * **Replace recidivism with "re-engagement with possibility": does the teen now see setbacks as temporary and themselves as having choices?** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Anchor success in local, observable shifts: "which teens now help younger kids make different choices?"** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Do not abandon the quantitative floor; dual-track instead.** Surfaced by the adversarial perspective and load-bearing as a direct counter to the question's premise: foundations report to boards in numbers and cannot tell donors a program works because teens "feel" different, so fully abandoning quantification is "programmatic suicide" and also removes the comparability needed to convince a skeptical superintendent. Keep standard metrics (attendance, grades, a brief monthly dyad-quality score) as a floor and layer felt outcomes on top: show funders the numbers, show communities the stories.
  * **Abandoning quantification entirely costs you future funding and all comparability; dual-track numbers for funders and stories for communities.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Keep a monthly dyad-quality score so qualitative mystique can't hide a failing match.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Guard against optimizing for the poetic and missing the quiet, vulnerable teens.** Surfaced by the adversarial perspective: purely qualitative evidence is easiest to capture for articulate, already-engaged, culturally-aligned teens, so quiet or trauma-affected youth may transform deeply in ways the program never narrates. Explicitly train witnesses to notice quiet transformation (persistence, small risk-taking, protective behaviors) and create witnessing that does not require eloquence.
  * **Fully qualitative measurement optimizes for the eloquent; train witnesses to see quiet transformation in the vulnerable.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Create durable artifacts and rituals that make transformation visible and shared.** Two perspectives converge on building tangible records of change: a physical or digital "community gallery" of mentorship moments (a photo, a quote, a reflection, with consent) and a year-end "ritual of recognition" naming each teen's specific, concrete growth. These make success a felt, witnessed reality usable with funders, recruits, and the community.
  * **A "community gallery" of photos, quotes, and reflections turns success from a statistic into lived evidence.** Original detail from The Systems Thinker. *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A year-end "ritual of recognition" names each teen's specific growth ("you kept showing up when your family fell apart").** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Treat the mentor's own transformation as program evidence too.** Surfaced by one perspective: ask mentors at year-end what they learned about themselves and how this teenager changed them; the program's deepest outcome may be mentors becoming more patient and more connected to the community.
  * **Capture mutual transformation: how the mentor is different because of the teen.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*
