## Synthesized Insights by Question

### Question 1: One-on-one meeting-location risk profile and the public-vs-private question

**Full question**: The one-on-one model with minors creates a distinct liability surface — what is the matrix of acceptable meeting settings (public vs. private, observed vs. unobserved), which combinations are categorically prohibited regardless of mentor tenure or relationship quality, and are there specific circumstances where private or home settings are appropriate and defensible, recognizing that overly prescriptive rules may prevent the natural trust-building interactions that make mentorship effective? [User Q]

* **Home-alone meetings are categorically prohibited; build the default around public settings with built-in observation.** All four perspectives converge here. Mentor-and-teen-alone-in-a-private-residence is indefensible to insurers and to a future jury regardless of tenure or background-check status. The defensible default is public venues with implicit observation (libraries, community centers, parks with foot traffic, coffee shops) or semi-private program spaces (a church office or community-center room where a third party can verify activity without intruding). Define "public" precisely so the rule does not backfire: a venue with staff present, sightlines to entry and exit, and posted hours.
  * **Public observable settings reframed as the safety architecture itself.** Coffee shops, library reading rooms, and community centers where mentors are visible become natural trust signals to families; the visible, documented environment *is* the safeguard. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Home-alone meetings are indefensible and likely uninsured.** Carriers will probably deny coverage for incidents in unobserved private residences; the defensible policy is public-with-implicit-observation or semi-private settings with a verifying third party nearby. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Define "public" specifically or it backfires.** A vague public-only rule can push pairs toward remote parks or car visits to escape crowds; require staff present, sightlines, and posted hours, plus a pre-visit location log. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Tier locations and start with pre-approved public venues.** Library study rooms, church common spaces, parks with foot traffic for the first three months; cost roughly $500/year for reserved rooms, 30 days to map venues. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Pair the public default with a low-burden check-in that creates a paper trail without creating surveillance.** A short before-and-after text to the coordinator ("Meeting [Teen] at Lincoln Park, 3-4 PM," then a completion confirm) demonstrates reasonable oversight to insurers while reading to teens as normal coordination, not interrogation. Keep it light: a standardized code, or a monthly location log rather than per-meeting filing, with staff spot-checking patterns.
  * **Public-with-accountability check-ins are the liability sweet spot.** Pre- and post-meeting texts create a verifiable oversight trail that satisfies carriers without a surveillance culture. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Use a standardized-code check-in (L=library, P=park, H=home) to a shared number.** Accountability without intrusion; flag only mentors who show a pattern of avoiding documented venues. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Reframe advance notification as planning together, not oversight.** Mentor and mentee jointly confirm the plan and the mentor texts the liaison afterward for connection, not approval, preserving coordinator awareness without scripting the relationship. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Allow private and home settings only as a structured, consent-gated exception with a second adult present.** No persona supports open-ended home access, but three describe a defensible exception path: written guardian consent naming the activity and attendees, a second adult (mentor's spouse or another mentor/coordinator) present throughout, advance notification to the coordinator, and a documented reason the setting serves the relationship. Family outings (a museum, a game, dinner with the mentor's spouse present) carry far less exposure than a one-on-one home visit and are the more realistic version of "private."
  * **Private settings require structural controls.** Written parental consent specifying activity and attendees, a second adult present throughout, 48-hour coordinator notice, and a written rationale; home visits remain a rare exception with two adults and a coordinator pre-visit. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Document private spaces with context rather than banning them.** Home visits permitted only after relationship milestones, conditional on another adult in the home, scheduled in advance, and restricted to mentors who completed advanced safety training; update the matrix annually on incident patterns. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Keep home-based mentoring off the table for most of the program's life.** A 40-volunteer, $15K program cannot fund the oversight (unannounced visits, re-screening, riders) that home settings require; if ever permitted, require family presence for the first three meetings and a higher-bar insurance rider. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Challenge the assumption that time-in-relationship lowers risk; gate escalation on behavior and consent, not the calendar.** This is a load-bearing dissent. Three personas propose a 6-month or 12-month progression toward private settings, but one argues directly that duration does not correlate with safety and that many abuse cases involve long-term trusted mentors. The reconciled stance: progression is reasonable as a *trust-building* device, but the gate that unlocks a less-observed setting should be a behavioral checkpoint (completed advanced training, direct reference verification, the teen's own written request, guardian consent), not merely "six clean months elapsed."
  * **Relationship maturity does not reduce liability; anchor rules to behavioral checkpoints, not time.** Long-term trusted mentors feature in many abuse cases; tie any escalation to advanced training, direct references, and written teen/family consent at each step. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Frame milestone-gated home access as deepening, not risk management.** Restrict private settings to a later phase after consistent meetings and explicit family approval, treating the constraint as an earned relationship marker. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Graduated freedom tied to relationship maturity, reviewed pair-by-pair.** Months 1-3 public; months 4+ public-but-unobserved activities with notice; staff review each pair's readiness before approving the next level. *—The Lawyer, The Pragmatist*

### Question 2: Insurance coverage architecture for a small program

**Full question**: What level of liability insurance is appropriate for a program of our size — what combination of general liability, directors-and-officers, volunteer accident, and sexual-abuse-and-molestation (SAM) coverage does a one-on-one youth mentorship program require, is the church's existing liability policy likely to extend to program volunteers and off-site mentoring activities, and should we carry a separate sexual misconduct policy in addition to general liability before the first mentor-mentee meeting occurs?

* **Carry standalone SAM coverage before the first meeting; general liability explicitly excludes abuse and molestation.** The strongest convergence in the cluster. General liability does not cover the scenarios a youth program most fears; SAM is a separate rider covering both abuse allegations and "failure to prevent" claims, including investigation and defense costs. It is relatively inexpensive (roughly $500-$2K/year for a 40-mentor program) and non-negotiable for a youth-serving organization. Procure it before a single mentor-teen meeting.
  * **SAM is non-negotiable and separate from general liability.** It covers allegations and failure-to-prevent claims plus investigation, defense, settlements, and awards; budget $500-$1K/year and name "abuse and molestation" in mentor agreements. *—The Lawyer*
  * **A standalone SAM policy adds ~$1.5K/year if the church policy excludes misconduct defense.** Buy a bundled youth-serving policy (GL $1-2M, D&O $1M, SAM $1-2M, volunteer accident) for roughly $3K-$5K/year; get three quotes now. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Read the SAM exclusion schedule before buying.** Some policies exclude first-six-month allegations or claims when specific training was not implemented; cross-check exclusions against your training timeline and ensure defense costs are covered, not just settlements. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Confirm the SAM rider is included rather than over-buying the enterprise stack.** Focused, adequate coverage reads as credible; underinsurance reads as negligence, so confirm SAM is present and add a low-cost volunteer accident policy. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Do not assume the church's existing policy extends to off-site, volunteer-led mentoring; get written confirmation first.** All insurance-focused perspectives agree the congregation's general liability likely covers on-property activities during standard hours but may exclude off-site meetings, evening/weekend activity, and one-on-one work with minors. Contact the church's broker immediately and obtain a written coverage letter before enrolling anyone, with particular attention to whether justice-system referrals trigger an exclusion.
  * **Get a written clarification letter; do not assume coverage.** Congregation policies often carve out contracted programs and off-site activity; if it does not extend to mentors, buy a separate $1.5K-$3K/year program policy. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Call the broker this week and ask the precise questions.** "Does this cover off-site mentoring, and does it include sexual-misconduct defense costs?" If no, add SAM; total roughly $4K-$6K/year for 40 mentors. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Justice-system placements sit in a different risk category and may be excluded.** Get a binding coverage letter specifying whether "referred through juvenile justice" triggers exclusions before enrolling a single justice-referred mentee. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Budget for the legal-defense and operational shock of a claim, not just premiums.** A distinctive single-persona escalation worth surfacing: insurance covers settlements and defense, but programs often fold mid-conflict because the organization cannot absorb the operational disruption and reputational hit of even an unfounded claim. Confirm the grant or a contingency fund can retain youth-program defense counsel, and model the impact of a $50K defense before a claim arises.
  * **The undercapitalization risk.** After premiums, ~$12K remains for delivery; model a $50K defense even for an unfounded claim and confirm a contingency or lawyer retainer exists, because organizations collapse from operational disruption, not only inadequate coverage. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Tie coverage to actual risk data and treat insurance as one layer, not the safety mechanism.** Insurance is a financial backstop; presenting it as comprehensive can falsely signal the program is now "risk-free" and crowd out training and supervision. Review incident logs annually, adjust coverage to real exposure, and in all family and mentor communication lead with screening, training, and supervision, keeping insurance as supporting infrastructure.
  * **Insurance-as-accountability theater is a trap.** Comprehensive insurance can signal risk-free to mentors and families; position it as one layer and make the training and supervision practices the visible safety story. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Keep a simple risk registry tied to annual insurance review.** Each January review incident logs, update the broker, and adjust limits as the program scales; tie decisions to data, not worst-case fear. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Let insurance cost function as a forcing signal.** A spike in quotes is data about a high-risk mentor pool or high-contact activities; respond by tightening screening and narrowing activities rather than only buying more coverage. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Have the board explicitly accept defined risks in writing and insure the gaps.** Articulate which risks are covered and why so families know exactly what is and is not insured. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Use the program's small scale to negotiate, not as an excuse to under-insure.** One perspective adds that contacting peer nonprofits to share carrier recommendations and negotiate group rates turns limited scale into cooperative buying power and a peer network.
  * **Bundle discovery as network-building.** Contact 3-5 similar local programs to negotiate group rates and share providers, turning small scale into a learning network. *—The Constraint Flipper*

### Question 3: Safety-as-care and the threshold where safeguards become burdens

**Full question**: Rather than safety protocols that feel like surveillance, what would a program look like where every safeguard — background checks, meeting-location policies, mandatory-reporter training — was designed so that teens, mentors, and families all felt genuinely held and trusted? Where is the threshold at which safeguards shift from reassuring to burdensome, and how does the program design training so that mandatory reporting obligations don't suppress the very openness the program is trying to build?

* **Sequence onboarding so relationship precedes legal paperwork; presenting all safeguards up front signals "we are protecting ourselves from you first."** Strong convergence. Background screening and mandatory-reporter training are non-negotiable and come before any mentor-teen contact, but liability waivers and deeper safety discussions land better after the first few meetings, once the teen has watched the mentor show up. By then the conversation becomes "we are protecting this thing that is working" rather than self-protection.
  * **Phase enrollment to prevent the trust-erosion spiral.** Phase 1 (pre-match): background and mandatory-reporter screening; Phase 2 (after three meetings): waivers and deeper safety talk, once the mentor has demonstrated follow-through. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Graduated disclosure over upfront waivers.** Lead with relational safety and let accountability structures and harder conversations deepen as trust deepens rather than front-loading consent documents. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Anchor safety language in relationship and trust, not control.** "We start with people we've vetted" and "if a teen is hurting, we have a plan" shift how rules feel; audit all materials for this language. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Train mandatory reporting for judgment and proportionality, not hair-trigger escalation, and make the obligation transparent to teens.** Convergent across all four. The risk is that undertrained mentors over-report normal adolescent disclosure (a party, sexuality questions, ordinary angst), teaching teens to hide vulnerability. Train mentors to listen first, distinguish reportable harm from disclosure that benefits from conversation, and consult the coordinator confidentially before filing borderline reports. Tell teens plainly what must be reported and that the mentor is not reporting *on* them.
  * **Pair reporting training with proportionality coaching.** "You will hear concerning things; your job is to listen, not immediately escalate"; report to the coordinator first to assess the threshold, preventing the chilling effect. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Distinguish reportable abuse from adolescent disclosure.** Teach clear thresholds and require confidential coordinator discussion of borderline cases so compliance stays intact without automatic bureaucratic escalation. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Make reporting feel like empowerment via scenario role-play.** Three clear triggers, a one-page escalation path, and a child-advocacy trainer (~$1K for a 3-hour session) so mentors feel protective, not policed. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Reframe reporting to teens as relational transparency.** Mentors say plainly "I'm required to tell the director if you're being hurt, because we're committed to your safety, not to getting you in trouble." *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Reframe background checks as a shared entry standard rather than suspicion of the individual.** Convergent. Run checks early, celebrate clearance as entry into a trusted cohort, and tell teens "your mentors went through the same screening local schools use." Normalizing the check as a belonging condition removes its sting.
  * **Background checks as entry validation, not surveillance.** Complete checks before training and frame clearance as joining a trusted cohort vetted the way schools vet. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Background checks as a basic belonging condition.** Make vetting a visible, shared gateway so it reads as taking the teen's circle seriously, not a secret process. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Share clear results with mentors and assess disclosures case-by-case.** A 30-minute conversation for any flagged mentor focused on what they learned, not what they did wrong. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Set the threshold empirically by asking teens and mentors whether safeguards feel necessary, and cut what does not.** A practical test for where care becomes burden: run an early anonymous pulse survey and simplify policies that a meaningful share of participants find stifling or time-consuming. This signals willingness to refine from experience, not theory.
  * **Test safeguards with month-2 feedback.** If 30% of teens find location rules stifling or mentors report 45-minute consent forms, simplify; design the survey in two weeks. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Acknowledge the time-cost of genuine care safety.** Safety-as-care needs more staff time than checkbox safety; conserve it by limiting cohort size and routing in-kind church staff to family orientation and liaison check-ins. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Distribute accountability into mentor peer cohorts so support replaces top-down policing.** Several personas propose monthly mentor circles where mentors surface challenges and hold each other to safety principles, shifting the felt experience from being monitored to being held.
  * **Peer accountability as an alternative to institutional surveillance.** Structured cohorts of 4-5 mentors meeting monthly produce stronger culture than top-down monitoring. *—The Constraint Flipper*

### Question 4: Imagine the safeguards cause the harm

**Full question**: What if the mandatory reporting obligations, background checks, and liability waivers — designed to protect teens — are the primary reason at-risk teens distrust adults and disengage from the program? What if the program was redesigned from the assumption that bureaucratic safety infrastructure is the thing making the relationships feel unsafe? Which protection mechanisms signal institutional distrust to the people they're meant to protect?

* **Recognize that at-risk teens read official paperwork as a precursor to loss, so lead with care and put privacy and non-judgment promises before disclosures.** Strong convergence. Forms, waivers, and background-check rituals echo the eviction notices, detention orders, and child-welfare contacts that have controlled these teens' lives. The fix is sequencing and framing: let teens experience care first and conditions second, and never weaponize the background check in conflict.
  * **Vulnerability gatekeeping through bureaucracy.** Put privacy and non-judgment commitments before disclosures so a teen who feels held will accept safeguards; one who feels surveilled will not. *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **The consent cascade replicates the power dynamics teens are escaping.** Separate information-gathering from consent, explain obligations verbally first, and offer an outside teen advocate or ombudsman to review policies. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Don't weaponize the check; present it as a shared baseline and move on.** "We all went through the same screening to be here," then past it. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Reduce documentation to the legally necessary minimum, because every form reads as evidence-building, not trust.** A distinctive reframe with practical teeth: the paper trail exists for legal defense, and teens and mentors intuit that. Cut feel-good forms (weekly progress notes, quarterly surveys) and replace them with low-stakes human check-ins. This directly tensions against documentation-heavy oversight proposed elsewhere in the cluster, and the reconciliation is to keep only attendance, mandatory-reporter alerts, and insurance-required incident reports.
  * **The documentation trap as proof of distrust.** Keep only legally necessary records; replace weekly notes and surveys with a monthly liaison call that listens rather than collects. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Audit safeguards for whose fear they center.** Per-meeting location logging serves staff liability, not teen safety; switch to monthly logging with quarterly spot-checks. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Make institutional process visible as good faith, not hidden.** Show teens monthly progress notes, celebrate milestones together, invite critique, so documentation reads as accountability *to* them. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Rethink screening so it does not filter out the credible-messenger mentors at-risk teens trust most.** A load-bearing single-persona insight, partly corroborated. Blanket background disqualification can exclude exactly the mentors with lived experience of poverty, the justice system, or recovery whom teens may trust more, while admitting only the most civically conventional volunteers. Build a parallel-track vetting process that distinguishes categorically disqualifying offenses (violent felonies, sexual offenses) from histories that demonstrate recovery, assessing current character rather than imposing permanent exclusion.
  * **The self-selection mechanism filters out authentic connectors.** Create a parallel-track vetting process with legal advice on which offense types are disqualifying versus which show recovery; design screening for current character. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A two-tier system offsets minor records with character verification.** Universal criminal and sex-offense checks stay non-negotiable, but references and community standing can offset minor records, with the rationale documented. *—The Lawyer*
  * **A lighter-touch, probationary process may surface character better.** Relational-fit interviews and a three-month provisional pairing can reveal character more reliably than comprehensive pre-vetting that teaches bad actors to craft applications. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Make consent a genuine choice by separating verbal understanding from signature, and simplify waivers to plain language.** Convergent. Broad legalese waivers read as "they want legal cover, not my safety." Walk families through the document verbally (bilingually, by a non-lawyer), simplify to a short plain-language version after legal review, and surface that some family hesitation reflects legitimate fears (child-welfare or immigration contact) that deserve transparent discussion.
  * **The waiver paradox in urban poverty.** Have a bilingual non-lawyer review waivers verbally, simplify concerning language without removing legal necessities, and treat family fears as legitimate and worth addressing. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Reframe waivers as specific and transparent, explained conversationally.** Limit scope to normal activities and have mentors talk teens through it rather than handing over a legal barrier. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Eliminate punitive-feeling waivers.** One paragraph explaining the program, one signature line, one question number; brevity signals respect after legal review. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Make the reporting obligation transparent so it reads as support, not hidden surveillance.** Convergent across the cluster. Teens know mentors are reporters and will stay silent if they fear instant escalation. Tell teens explicitly what must be reported and that they will be told if it happens; train mentors to receive hints with gentle follow-up rather than reflexive filing.
  * **The reporting chilling effect on disclosure.** Train mentors to listen to hints, explain what a report means before filing, and tell teens "they're not reporting on you, they're reporting situations that need adult help." *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Transparent reporting instead of hidden escalations.** Tell the teen their mentor shared something because they care, what is being done, and how the teen can shape the next step. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Mandatory reporting as relationship terminator if mishandled.** Report imminent risks immediately, but co-investigate ongoing situations with the coordinator before reporting to preserve the relationship. *—The Lawyer*

### Question 5: Inventing a new kind of safety

**Full question**: What if "safety" in a youth mentorship program didn't mean protocols, waivers, and rules — but was instead designed from scratch as an emergent property of the relationships themselves? What would a program look like where safety arises from trust architecture rather than legal architecture?

* **Treat mentor selection and matching as the primary safety tool, ahead of policy.** Convergent. A genuine, well-matched relationship is a bad actor's worst environment: the teen reports concerns, the mentor will not jeopardize the bond, and staff notice problems early through organic check-ins. Recruit for demonstrated relational capacity and assess emotional resilience and authentic motive in interviews, then train relentlessly in empathy, boundaries, and de-escalation rather than compliance checklists.
  * **Matching is your primary safety tool; spend two weeks on it, not on policies.** A great match means the teen will surface concerns and the mentor will protect the relationship. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Anchor safety in mentor selection and training, not documents.** Recruit people who have sustained friendships and earned community trust; probe repair and care in interviews. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Mentor self-selection into the work as a safety mechanism.** Use scenario interviews ("if a teen disclosed substance use, how would you respond?") so volunteers who are not ready opt out; mentors who chose this specific work burn out less. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Mentor preparation that surfaces assumptions about trauma and poverty.** Role plays and reflective interviews self-select for mentors who understand relationship is the safety mechanism. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Make consistent, long-term presence the core safeguard; choose reliability over protocol when they compete.** A distinctive reframe corroborated across personas: relational predictability is precisely what at-risk teens lack, and a mentor who shows up weekly for a year and knows the teen deeply prevents harm in a way no protocol does. Design the program's central commitment around reliability (weekly meetings, one-year minimum, matching for credible time commitment) and invest in mentor support so consistency is achievable.
  * **Safety as consistent presence; when protocols and consistency compete, choose consistency.** A reliably present mentor lowers actual risk more than one who cancels often but completes every check. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Continuity over oversight as protection.** Depth of knowing equals safety; a mentor who knows a teen's school, family, triggers, and dreams for three years is the safest design. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Replace surveillance with structured peer connection that doubles as an early-warning system.** Convergent. Monthly mentor circles, or mentor-teen group gatherings, surface struggles organically and build observation into the design without logging. Personas split on form (mentor-only support circles vs. mixed mentor-teen circles with built-in witness) but agree the group conversation reveals more than location logs.
  * **Monthly mentor circles are your early-warning system.** A 90-minute group conversation teaches you more than a year of location logs, at near-zero cost in church space. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Mentorship circles as collective witness.** Mixed mentor-teen monthly gatherings create observation without surveillance and reduce liability while increasing trust. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Mentor circles distribute safety accountability beyond the coordinator.** Add a family representative or teen advocate so peer and family voices enter decisions teens find more trustworthy than top-down rules. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Conflict-resolution circles make repair the safety system.** Peer mediation when tensions arise builds the ability to repair rupture together rather than routing everything to administration. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Co-create the relationship's safety boundaries with the teen rather than imposing them, keeping legal guardrails non-negotiable underneath.** Convergent. A jointly authored "mentoring covenant" or relationship agreement (what we will do together, what feels good, how we name discomfort) shifts safety from compliance to culture, while the program's legal floor (mandatory reporting, no solo transportation, no significant gifts) stays fixed and is presented as protective guardrails, not the relationship's primary architecture.
  * **A mutual "Mentoring Covenant" shifts safety from compliance to culture.** Behavioral commitments (meet on schedule, be honest, address conflict directly) double as legal documentation and signal partnership. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Safety as earned clarity; the teen and mentor set their own ground rules.** A three-meeting protocol where the pair defines comfort zones, with legal boundaries kept as non-negotiable guardrails. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A co-created relationship agreement makes teens co-designers of their protection.** Safety emerges from ongoing renegotiation, not pre-set rules. *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Make transparency the core architecture: publish the rules, explain the limits, and build graduated accountability with appeal.** Several personas converge on transparency as both the relational and legal optimum. Publish disqualification criteria, matching logic, and escalation triggers so secrecy stops breeding distrust; train mentors to explain *why* a boundary exists ("I follow the rules so I can keep showing up for you"); and replace one-strike removal with a graduated, appealable response that protects mentors and models reciprocal accountability.
  * **Radical transparency satisfies both relational and legal needs.** Publishing the framework eliminates secrecy and makes consistently applied policy more defensible against wrongful-termination claims. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Graduated accountability with built-in appeal.** Conversation and retraining, then suspension, then appealable removal creates a good-faith paper trail and models second chances. *—The Lawyer*
  * **Safety as transparency about limits.** Mentors saying "here's what I can't do, and why" and admitting uncertainty builds trust in their judgment, which becomes the actual safety mechanism. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Mentors as co-learners, not experts, to remove the power asymmetry.** "I'm learning your world with you" reduces the hierarchy that makes formal safeguards feel necessary. *—The Constraint Flipper*
