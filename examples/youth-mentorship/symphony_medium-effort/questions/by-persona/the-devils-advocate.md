# Brainstorming Questions from The Devil's Advocate: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## The Volunteer Commitment Gap

1. **Attrition before launch**: Have we considered that a substantial share of the 40 "interested adults" may not complete background checks, training, and the formal commitment process — and if half or more drop out before matching, how does the program avoid launching with mismatched pair counts that strand enrolled teens?

2. **One-year commitment versus real life**: The model requires a one-year weekly commitment, but volunteers are drawn from a congregation with its own seasonal rhythms, job changes, and family pressures — what happens to a teen's trajectory when their mentor has to withdraw at month four, and is the program prepared to absorb that rupture without re-traumatizing youth who may already have unstable adult relationships?

3. **Congregation homogeneity as a matching constraint**: One potential challenge might be that a faith-based volunteer pool skews toward a particular demographic (age, race, class, worldview) that may not align well with teens referred through juvenile justice — how will the program recruit outside the congregation to ensure matches that feel culturally safe and relevant to each teen?

---

## Referral Integrity and Selection Bias

4. **Counselor and probation officer incentives**: Have we considered whether school counselors and juvenile justice officers will refer the teens they most want to help, the teens easiest to manage, or the teens they most want to pass off — and how the program will distinguish between these motivations when evaluating whether the referral pipeline is actually reaching highest-need youth?

5. **Consent as a filter for participation**: If parental or guardian consent is required before a teen can enroll, and if the most at-risk teens often come from households with the least parental capacity or stability, does the consent framework inadvertently exclude the very youth the program most intends to serve — and if so, what alternative consent pathways exist under applicable law for older teens or those in foster care?

6. **Defining "at-risk" without a shared standard**: The program draws referrals from two distinct systems — schools (chronic absenteeism) and juvenile justice (prior system involvement) — that apply different definitions and thresholds of risk; one potential challenge is that this dual pipeline could create an internally inconsistent cohort, making it difficult to design a training curriculum or measure outcomes that are meaningful across both groups. How will the program define and document a unified eligibility standard, and who holds authority to apply it?

---

## Safety Architecture Stress Tests

7. **One-on-one meetings as a liability structure**: Have we considered that the "one-on-one" model — while relationship-building — is precisely the configuration that most child-protection frameworks treat as highest risk, and that standard mitigations (public locations, check-in protocols, digital communication auditing) may be burdensome enough to deter volunteers or constrain the naturalness of the relationship? What is the minimum safety infrastructure that is legally defensible without making mentorship feel like supervised visitation?

8. **Mandatory reporting training timing**: One potential challenge might be that mandatory reporter obligations apply from the moment a volunteer has contact with a youth, but training may occur after initial meetings if the onboarding sequence is not carefully designed — how will the program sequence background checks, training, and first contact to ensure no volunteer interacts with a teen before achieving certified mandatory-reporter status?

9. **The $15K gap between ambition and infrastructure**: The seed grant must cover background checks, insurance, training, coordinator time, and outcome measurement for up to 40 pairs — have we modeled whether $15K is actually sufficient, and if it falls short at month six, what is the contingency that keeps already-matched pairs intact rather than abruptly terminating relationships mid-program?

---

## Institutional Dependency Risks

10. **Single-coordinator single-point-of-failure**: One vulnerability in community programs of this scale is concentration of operational knowledge in one or two staff members supported by the church — if the administrative coordinator who manages consent records, match files, and mandatory-report logs leaves or becomes unavailable, does the program have documented procedures and backup access to continue operating without interruption or compliance exposure?

11. **Faith framing as a double-edged referral signal**: Have we considered that the organization's 20-year faith-based identity — an asset for trust in the congregation — may function as a deterrent for secular families, LGBTQ+ teens, or families from different religious traditions who are referred through school or juvenile justice channels, and that without explicit policy clarity on religious neutrality in program delivery, the program risks both reduced uptake and potential civil rights questions about publicly-referred participants being placed in a faith-directed program?
