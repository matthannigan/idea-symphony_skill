# Brainstorming Questions from The Questioner: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Hidden Assumptions in Program Design

1. **Assumption of availability**: Why do we assume that teens identified through juvenile justice referrals or school counselors are in a position to commit to weekly meetings for a full year — and what does it mean for the program design if a significant portion cannot?

2. **Assumption of readiness**: What if the assumption that adult volunteers from a congregation already share values compatible with supporting at-risk teens is itself a source of mismatch — what screening process surfaces that misalignment before it becomes a failed match?

3. **Assumption of one-size fit**: Why do we assume a single one-on-one weekly meeting model is the right structure for every teen in this cohort, rather than a flexible model that adapts to the teen's stability, school schedule, or family situation?

4. **Assumption of relationship as outcome**: What if the relationship itself is not the primary outcome that moves the needle for these teens — what evidence-based mechanisms (skill transfer, advocacy, network access) should we be designing around rather than assuming the relationship delivers them automatically?

---

## What Success Actually Looks Like — and for Whom

5. **Whose definition of success**: How are we defining program success — and have we asked the teens themselves what they would need to feel this program was worth a year of their time?

6. **Measurement without surveillance**: What would meaningful outcome tracking look like if we designed it from the teens' perspective rather than the funder's, especially given the sensitivity of juvenile justice involvement and the 35% chronic absenteeism baseline?

7. **Long-term signal vs. short-term proxy**: What are we actually measuring at the end of year one that we believe predicts genuine long-term impact — and what if those proxies (attendance improvement, mentor contact hours) don't correlate with what actually matters?

---

## Boundaries, Safety, and the Paradox of Protection

8. **Paradox of safety protocols**: What if overly prescriptive meeting-location rules and interaction boundaries — designed to protect teens — actually prevent the kinds of natural, trust-building interactions that make mentorship effective? How do we draw the line without drawing it in the wrong place?

9. **Mandatory reporting as a deterrent**: Have we fully considered how mandatory reporter obligations might change what teens choose to disclose to mentors, and how that dynamic affects the depth of the relationships we're trying to build?

10. **Who protects the mentor**: What if the safety protocols we design protect the organization and the teen but leave the mentor exposed to burnout, secondary trauma, or ambiguous situations without adequate support — what does a reciprocal protection framework look like?

---

## The Institutional Ecology Around the Teen

11. **Cross-system coherence**: What if a teen's school counselor, juvenile justice case manager, parent or guardian, and mentor are all working from different frameworks and giving conflicting guidance — who resolves that, and is the program designed to handle it?

12. **Faith-based identity as asset and risk**: What if the congregation context — which gives this organization its 20-year community trust — also creates discomfort or barriers for teens from different religious backgrounds or no background at all? How should that tension be designed for explicitly?

---

## Structural Sustainability and Volunteer Commitment

13. **Commitment cliff**: What happens at the one-year mark — and have we designed for the possibility that teens and mentors who've built real relationships will face an abrupt structural ending? What does the off-ramp look like, and what signals tell us a relationship should continue versus conclude?
