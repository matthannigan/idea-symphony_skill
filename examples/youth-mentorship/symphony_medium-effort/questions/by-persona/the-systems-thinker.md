# Brainstorming Questions from The Systems Thinker: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Referral and Intake Dynamics

1. **Referral source diversity**: If the program relies primarily on school counselors and juvenile justice referrals, what feedback loops might emerge — does early success with one channel make staff increasingly dependent on it, gradually narrowing who the program actually reaches over time?

2. **Gatekeeping and youth agency**: How does the referral structure shape who decides a teen "needs" mentorship — and what happens when teens arrive feeling labeled or coerced rather than self-selected? Does that entry condition propagate through the relationship and affect match durability?

3. **Institutional load-shifting**: As the program proves effective, what prevents referring institutions (schools, juvenile justice) from increasing referral volume faster than program capacity can absorb — and how does an overloaded program respond in ways that quietly degrade quality?

---

## Match Stability and Relationship Dynamics

4. **Match quality as a compounding factor**: When a match is strong, the teen shows up, the mentor stays engaged, and both sides invest more — a self-amplifying pattern. When a match is weak, disengagement feeds more disengagement. What early signals could detect a weakening match before the dynamic becomes irreversible, and what intervention points exist before exit?

5. **Mentor attrition and program reputation**: If mentor burnout causes visible departures in the first cohort, how does that reshape the volunteer pipeline — does word-of-mouth from burned-out mentors reduce future recruitment, and does the program's trajectory depend heavily on the first-year mentor experience?

6. **The long commitment signal**: A one-year minimum commitment filters for motivated volunteers — but it also filters out people who might thrive with a shorter initial trial. What does the program lose by setting the bar high at the door, and is there a phased commitment structure that preserves quality without sacrificing pool depth?

---

## Compliance, Safeguards, and Organizational Trust

7. **Mandatory reporting as relationship disruptor**: Mentors are legally required to report disclosures — yet disclosure is also the signal that a relationship of trust has formed. How does the program design training and communication so that mandatory reporting obligations don't suppress the very openness the program is trying to build?

8. **Safeguard intensity and mentor confidence**: More rigorous safety protocols protect youth and the organization — but they can also make volunteers feel surveilled and distrusted. Where is the threshold at which safeguards shift from reassuring to burdensome, and how does that threshold vary across volunteer backgrounds?

9. **Juvenile justice data and stigma propagation**: Teens referred through juvenile justice arrive with records the organization may access for safety purposes. How does the organization prevent that background information from leaking into mentor attitudes and match decisions in ways that confirm rather than interrupt a teen's trajectory?

---

## Program Capacity and Sustainability

10. **Seed funding dependency and behavioral lock-in**: The $15K grant funds initial infrastructure, but what organizational behaviors become load-bearing on that funding — and if the grant isn't renewed, which of those behaviors collapse versus which ones the organization has built capacity to sustain independently?

11. **Volunteer congregation vs. community expansion**: Starting with ~40 volunteers from the congregation provides social trust and accountability, but the program's long-term capacity may require recruiting beyond that network. Does success with congregation volunteers create conditions that make external recruitment easier or harder — and which dynamic is more likely?

---

## Outcome Measurement and Learning Loops

12. **Measurement burden and participation chilling**: Outcome measurement produces program intelligence — but surveys, check-ins, and reporting requirements add friction for both mentors and teens. At what point does the measurement apparatus start degrading the participation it's trying to study, and how should the program sequence data collection as relationships develop?

13. **Short-cycle versus long-cycle feedback**: Many meaningful outcomes for at-risk teens — educational attainment, justice system involvement, economic stability — manifest over years. What shorter-cycle signals can function as reliable proxies for long-term outcomes, and how does the program avoid optimizing for proxies in ways that miss the deeper changes it actually cares about?
