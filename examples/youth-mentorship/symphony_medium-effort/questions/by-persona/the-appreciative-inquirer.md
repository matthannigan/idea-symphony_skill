# Brainstorming Questions from The Appreciative Inquirer: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Discovery: Peak Moments in Mentorship

1. **When mentorship transformed a teen's trajectory**: Think about the most powerful youth mentorship story you've encountered — what was it about that relationship that made the difference, and what conditions in the program made that relationship possible?

2. **The volunteer who went above and beyond**: When has a volunteer mentor surprised everyone with the depth of their commitment and creativity? What drew that person in, what sustained them through difficulty, and what does that tell us about what we should cultivate in our mentor recruitment?

3. **When a faith community became a youth's lifeline**: Tell us about a moment when this organization's 20-year presence in the neighborhood became genuinely irreplaceable for a young person — what was it about the community's trust and rootedness that no outside program could have replicated?

---

## Dream: The Program at Its Best

4. **Three years from now, a model for others**: Imagine it's 2029 and delegations from other cities are visiting to learn from this program. What do they notice first when they walk in the door? What do they hear teens and mentors saying to each other? What metrics are on the wall, and what stories are being shared?

5. **The match that became a life-long relationship**: Picture a mentor-mentee pair who met through this program and, ten years later, still celebrate milestones together. What was it about how they were matched, how their early meetings were structured, and how the program supported them through rough patches that made that bond durable?

6. **Safety as a foundation for flourishing**: Rather than safety protocols that feel like surveillance, what would a program look like where every safeguard — background checks, meeting-location policies, mandatory reporting training — was designed so that teens, mentors, and families all felt genuinely held and trusted? What does "safety as care" look like in practice?

---

## Design: Structures That Reliably Create Success

7. **Building on what the congregation already does well**: This organization has sustained a 20-year presence in a high-poverty neighborhood — what relational practices, cultural knowledge, and community trust already exist here that a mentorship program could directly plug into, rather than building from scratch?

8. **Training that mentors actually want**: When volunteer training programs work brilliantly — when people leave more energized and capable than when they arrived — what is happening in the room? How might we design mandatory-reporter training, boundary-setting, and matching orientation so that mentors experience it as a gift rather than a compliance burden?

9. **Cross-system referrals as bridges, not handoffs**: The teens in this program are coming through school counselors and juvenile justice — institutions that often feel adversarial to families. What would it look like to design the referral and consent process so that families experienced crossing from those systems into this program as crossing a threshold into genuine welcome and support?

---

## Destiny: First Steps and Lasting Commitments

10. **The assets already in the room**: Among the 40 interested volunteers, the congregation's existing relationships, the school counselors' knowledge of individual teens, and the foundation grant — what is already in place that this program could mobilize immediately, before a single formal policy is written?

11. **What one mentor-mentee pair this year could teach us**: Rather than designing the whole program in advance, what would it look like to start with a small cohort of three to five carefully supported pairs, use their experience as discovery data, and let what works well in those relationships shape the program design for everyone who follows?
