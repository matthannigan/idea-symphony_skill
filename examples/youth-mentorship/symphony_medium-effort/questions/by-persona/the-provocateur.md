# Brainstorming Questions from The Provocateur: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## The Problem May Be Backwards

1. **Flip the referral**: What if the teens chose and vetted the mentors — not the other way around? If a 15-year-old from the juvenile justice system had full veto power over who entered their life, what would the selection criteria actually look like, and what does that reveal about whose comfort the current process is really designed to protect?

2. **Abolish the one-year commitment**: What if instead of requiring mentors to commit to a year, you designed a program where *any* relationship lasting more than three meetings was considered a success — and the whole infrastructure was built around making short, intense, honest encounters valuable rather than long obligatory ones? What does this reveal about whether "sustained relationship" is the goal or just the metric?

---

## Safety As the Disease, Not the Cure

3. **Imagine the safeguards cause the harm**: What if the mandatory reporting obligations, background checks, and liability waivers — designed to protect teens — are the primary reason at-risk teens distrust adults and disengage from the program? What if the program was redesigned from the assumption that bureaucratic safety infrastructure is the thing making the relationships feel unsafe? *The real insight: which protection mechanisms signal institutional distrust to the people they're meant to protect?*

4. **Deliberately worsen the matching process**: What if you matched mentors and mentees on the most jarring possible mismatches — different race, class, age, religion, hobby, and worldview — and built the curriculum around navigating those differences honestly? What would be lost if you optimized for similarity, and what might be gained by making discomfort the explicit starting condition?

---

## The Faith Organization as Obstacle

5. **Strip the church entirely**: Imagine the program existed with exactly the same teens, mentors, and funding — but with no faith-based organizational home. What would change? What would the teens who were referred through juvenile justice gain or lose? *The insight: which elements of the faith-based context are genuine assets versus implicit barriers to entry for specific youth?*

---

## Measuring the Wrong Thing at the Wrong Time

6. **Measure failure, not success**: What if instead of tracking program outcomes — attendance, graduation rates, "relationship quality" — you tracked every moment a mentor or teen *wanted to quit* but didn't? What would that data tell you that success metrics never could? *The insight: resilience and durability in relationships are invisible to outcome measurement but may be the actual product being built.*

---

## The Volunteer Is the Risk

7. **Assume the 40 volunteers are the problem**: What if you started from the premise that well-meaning adult volunteers in high-poverty communities cause more harm than good — through pity, savior dynamics, inconsistency, and premature dropout — and designed the entire mentor training program to actively counteract the damage motivated volunteers naturally want to do? What would "don't be helpful in the ways you want to be helpful" look like as a curriculum?
