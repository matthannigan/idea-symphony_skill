# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Teen Experience and First Impressions

1. **The arrival moment**: What does a teen experience in the first 15 minutes of their first mentor meeting — what are they thinking, feeling, and silently assessing — and how does that first encounter either build or destroy the trust that makes the rest of the year possible?

2. **Consent versus coercion**: When a teen is referred through juvenile justice or flagged by a school counselor, how do we ensure they experience the program as an opportunity they chose rather than a condition imposed on them, and what concrete design choices signal that difference?

3. **The dignity of being matched**: What does the matching process feel like from the teen's perspective — are they an object being assigned, or a person being heard — and what would it mean to design intake as an experience that itself communicates respect?

---

## Family and Guardian Inclusion

4. **Parent as partner, not gatekeeper**: Beyond obtaining consent signatures, how might parents and guardians be meaningfully included as participants in the program's success — and what barriers (work schedules, language, distrust of institutions) might prevent genuine engagement?

5. **When home is complicated**: For teens whose home environment is part of what makes them "at-risk" — unstable housing, incarcerated parents, family conflict — how does the program design account for the reality that "parental involvement" may be fraught, inconsistent, or impossible?

6. **Guardian communication that doesn't backfire**: How do we design ongoing communication with parents so that it builds trust rather than creating a surveillance dynamic that causes teens to shut down or disengage from their mentor?

---

## Barriers to Sustained Participation

7. **The invisible logistics problem**: What practical barriers — bus routes, after-school jobs, childcare for younger siblings, lack of a phone — might cause a teen to miss meetings without ever explaining why, and how does the program design catch and address these before they read as "disengagement"?

8. **Cultural and linguistic fit**: From the perspective of teens from communities that may historically distrust faith-based institutions, authority figures, or the juvenile justice system — what signals in the program's design, language, and environment either invite them in or quietly tell them this isn't for them?

9. **The stigma of being "at-risk"**: How do teens referred through school counselors or juvenile justice experience the label that got them into this program, and what choices in naming, framing, and program culture help participants see themselves as capable young people rather than problems to be solved?

---

## Mentor Relationship Dynamics from the Teen's Perspective

10. **What teens need vs. what adults think teens need**: Research consistently shows that young people's unarticulated needs often differ from adults' assumptions — so what mechanisms does the program build to continuously surface what teens actually want from their mentor relationships, not just what the program was designed to provide?

11. **Power, race, and cross-cultural mentorship**: When mentors and mentees come from different racial, class, or cultural backgrounds — which is likely given the volunteer pool drawn from a congregation — what are the teens' experiences of navigating that difference, and how does the program prepare both parties to cross it with care?

12. **When a match isn't working**: From the teen's perspective, what does it feel like to be in a mentor relationship that isn't clicking — and does the program make it genuinely safe and easy for a teen to say so, or does the process of requesting a change feel like another failure on a long list?

---

## Outcome Measurement and Voice

13. **Measuring what matters to teens**: The people being served have their own definition of program success — what does a "good year with a mentor" mean to a 15-year-old in this community — and how does the measurement framework include those definitions alongside the organization's outcomes, rather than treating data collection as something done to participants rather than with them?
