# Topic Cluster 03: Mentor Training, Mandatory-Reporter Competency & Ongoing Support

## Questions

1. **Mandatory-reporter competency, internalized vs. box-checked**: Mandatory reporter obligations vary in specificity — what are the distinct knowledge components (recognition of abuse indicators, reporting thresholds, chain of notification, documentation requirements) that a mentor must demonstrate competency in before being cleared, how is that competency verified rather than assumed from attendance, and how do we structure the training so mentors actually internalize and act on it rather than just checking a box during onboarding? [User Q]
2. **Concern escalation when below the mandatory-report line**: How should a mentor raise a concern about a mentee when they're unsure whether it rises to the level of a mandatory report — and what formal incident-documentation protocol (initial disclosure, chain of report notification, regulatory filing timelines, record retention) ensures mandatory reporting obligations are met without creating conflicting instructions between program staff, church leadership, and external agency mandates? [User Q]
3. **Training as a gift, not a compliance burden**: When volunteer training programs work brilliantly — when people leave more energized and capable than when they arrived — what is happening in the room, and how might we design mandatory-reporter training, boundary-setting, and matching orientation so that mentors experience it as a gift rather than a compliance burden?
4. **Reciprocal protection and burnout prevention in the first six months**: What ongoing support and check-in cadence do mentors need to prevent burnout, especially during the first six months of a match — and what does a reciprocal protection framework look like that protects the mentor from secondary trauma and ambiguous situations, not just the organization and the teen? [User Q]
5. **Mandatory reporting as a shared protocol layer**: Air traffic control works across dozens of airlines and airports through a single shared protocol layer that all parties implement independently — what would it look like to design mandatory reporting obligations and escalation procedures as a shared protocol that the faith org, school, and juvenile justice system each implement in their own language, rather than training mentors to navigate each institution's separate reporting chain?
6. **Assume the 40 volunteers are the problem**: What if you started from the premise that well-meaning adult volunteers in high-poverty communities cause more harm than good — through pity, savior dynamics, inconsistency, and premature dropout — and designed the entire mentor training program to actively counteract the damage motivated volunteers naturally want to do? What would "don't be helpful in the ways you want to be helpful" look like as a curriculum?
7. **The test of a hard week**: Walk through the moment six months in when a teen ghosts two scheduled meetings after a crisis at home — what structures, relationships, and rituals does the program have in place so the mentor knows what to do, feels supported rather than abandoned, and the teen has a clear path back without shame?
8. **The moment trust breaks**: Imagine a mentor who, trying to be helpful, shares something a teen told her in confidence with the teen's parent — and the teen finds out — what policies, training moments, and repair conversations does the program have ready so this breach doesn't end the relationship and teaches both people something true about trust?

**Cluster focus**: Building mentor competency that is internalized rather than checked-off, and sustaining mentors so they don't burn out, drop out, or cause harm.
