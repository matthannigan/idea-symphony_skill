# Topic Cluster 05: Consent, Referral Integrity & Juvenile-Justice Privacy

## Questions

1. **Layered consent with contested guardianship and juvenile-justice power dynamics**: Given that participants are minors ages 13–17, what layered consent framework — distinguishing between initial program enrollment consent, photo/media release, data collection authorization, and ongoing participation acknowledgment — must the organization obtain from parents or legal guardians, what happens when guardianship is contested, shared, or held by the state through juvenile justice involvement, and how do we obtain genuinely informed consent from teens referred through juvenile justice given the power dynamics and the privacy stakes of their records (recognizing that requiring parental consent may inadvertently exclude the most at-risk youth from households with the least parental capacity)? [User Q]
2. **Juvenile-record confidentiality and data minimization**: When teens are referred through juvenile justice channels, what restrictions do state juvenile-record confidentiality statutes place on what information school counselors and juvenile justice officers may legally share with program staff, what is the minimum data the program must collect to operate effectively, and how should retention policies be structured so that sensitive information doesn't migrate outside its lawful disclosure boundary or create a private record that outlasts the legal protections governing the official one?
3. **Referral source diversity, institutional load-shifting, counselor incentives, and a shared "at-risk" standard**: If the program relies primarily on school counselors and juvenile justice referrals, what feedback loops emerge — does early success with one channel make staff increasingly dependent on it, gradually narrowing who the program reaches, and as the program proves effective, what prevents referring institutions from increasing volume faster than capacity can absorb (institutional load-shifting that quietly degrades quality)? Will counselors and probation officers refer the teens they most want to help, the teens easiest to manage, or the teens they most want to pass off — and given the two referring systems apply different thresholds of risk (schools' chronic absenteeism vs. juvenile justice's prior involvement), how will the program define and document a unified eligibility standard with clear authority over its application?
4. **Cross-system intake as a warm handoff**: School counselors and juvenile justice coordinators each have distinct relational authority with a teen — how might the program design its intake process to borrow from the "warm handoff" model used in healthcare transitions, where the referring professional personally introduces the mentee to the program rather than passing paperwork alone?
5. **Referral as a trust-transfer mechanism**: In franchise models, brand trust transfers from the franchisor to a new location the moment a customer walks in; there's a structural parallel here with referral — how might the program design the mentor-matching step so that the trust a teen has in their school counselor or parole officer actually transfers forward into their first interaction with their mentor?
