# Topic Cluster 06: Safety Architecture, Meeting-Location Policy & Insurance

## Questions

1. **One-on-one meeting-location risk profile and the public-vs-private question**: The one-on-one model with minors creates a distinct liability surface — what is the matrix of acceptable meeting settings (public vs. private, observed vs. unobserved), which combinations are categorically prohibited regardless of mentor tenure or relationship quality, and are there specific circumstances where private or home settings are appropriate and defensible, recognizing that overly prescriptive rules may prevent the natural trust-building interactions that make mentorship effective? [User Q]
2. **Insurance coverage architecture for a small program**: What level of liability insurance is appropriate for a program of our size — what combination of general liability, directors-and-officers, volunteer accident, and sexual-abuse-and-molestation (SAM) coverage does a one-on-one youth mentorship program require, is the church's existing liability policy likely to extend to program volunteers and off-site mentoring activities, and should we carry a separate sexual misconduct policy in addition to general liability before the first mentor-mentee meeting occurs? [User Q]
3. **Safety-as-care and the threshold where safeguards become burdens**: Rather than safety protocols that feel like surveillance, what would a program look like where every safeguard — background checks, meeting-location policies, mandatory-reporter training — was designed so that teens, mentors, and families all felt genuinely held and trusted? Where is the threshold at which safeguards shift from reassuring to burdensome, and how does the program design training so that mandatory reporting obligations don't suppress the very openness the program is trying to build?
4. **Imagine the safeguards cause the harm**: What if the mandatory reporting obligations, background checks, and liability waivers — designed to protect teens — are the primary reason at-risk teens distrust adults and disengage from the program? What if the program was redesigned from the assumption that bureaucratic safety infrastructure is the thing making the relationships feel unsafe? Which protection mechanisms signal institutional distrust to the people they're meant to protect?
5. **Inventing a new kind of safety**: What if "safety" in a youth mentorship program didn't mean protocols, waivers, and rules — but was instead designed from scratch as an emergent property of the relationships themselves? What would a program look like where safety arises from trust architecture rather than legal architecture?
