# Consent Frameworks and Data Privacy - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Informed Consent Across Custodial Complexity in Juvenile-Justice Referrals

* **The authority mapping problem will be wrong the moment it is completed.** A consent matrix that assigns authority to "legal guardian" or "probation officer" assumes stable custody arrangements — but a teen's custodial status can change overnight following a court hearing, a family crisis, or a placement disruption. The program needs not just a mapping but a re-verification trigger: any time a participant's household status changes, consent must be re-obtained from the newly authoritative adult. Without that trigger, the organization may be operating on stale consent.

* **Power dynamics make consent from juvenile-justice referrals structurally suspect.** Have we considered that a teen referred through juvenile justice may understand participation as quasi-mandatory — something their probation officer expects, not something they freely choose? If the youth believes declining will have consequences for their case, their "consent" is coerced in all but name. The mitigation is explicit, documented assurance from the referring PO that program participation is voluntary and carries no consequences for the youth's standing.

* **Non-traditional guardians often lack legal authority to consent.** An older sibling raising a teen, or a grandparent without formal guardianship, cannot legally consent on a minor's behalf — yet they may be the de facto caregiver. Asking an uninvested biological parent to sign forms they don't understand, while ignoring the actual household adult who will manage the relationship, creates paperwork compliance without meaningful informed consent. The program should have a legal pathway — emergency guardianship, formal kinship placement documentation — rather than accepting a signature from whoever is present.

* **The mid-program custody change is not an edge case.** For teens in the juvenile justice system or foster care, placement changes are common — sometimes multiple times per year. The organization needs a clear policy: does a custody change pause participation, require new consent, or trigger mandatory re-matching? Absent a written protocol, staff will improvise inconsistently, creating both legal exposure and inequitable treatment across participants.

* **Consent for program data use and consent for participation are often conflated.** The enrollment form may include both, but they are legally and ethically distinct. A guardian who consents to participation has not necessarily consented to the teen's outcome data being shared with the funding foundation, or their photo appearing in a newsletter. Bundling these into a single signature creates future liability when a guardian says "I only agreed to the mentorship, not the report."

## Layered Ongoing Consent and the Age-18 Transition

* **A one-time consent form is not a consent framework — it is a consent event.** Have we considered that ongoing activities (off-site outings, photography, data sharing with funders) each carry independent risk profiles that may not have been contemplated when the original form was signed? A guardian who consented to weekly meetings at the church did not necessarily anticipate their teen's photo in a foundation annual report. Each activity category should require a discrete, revisable consent decision rather than a blanket enrollment signature.

* **The age-18 transition creates a documentation gap with real liability.** When a participant turns 18 mid-program, the organization's legal relationship with them changes entirely — the guardian's authority terminates and the youth becomes the sole consent authority. If the organization does not have a documented process for this transition (including re-execution of data-sharing agreements in the youth's own name), it may be retaining and using data on an adult without valid authorization. A 30-day pre-birthday notification protocol with re-consent paperwork would close this gap.

* **Photo and program-materials consent is routinely under-resourced.** Many community nonprofits obtain blanket photo consent at enrollment and then forget about it. But a 13-year-old's guardian may consent to photos; that same youth at 17, with a more developed sense of their own privacy and a social media presence, may have very different preferences. Annual re-confirmation of photo/materials consent — separate from program participation consent — respects the youth's evolving autonomy and reduces reputational risk if a young person later objects to how their image was used.

* **Funder data-sharing consent is often an afterthought.** The program intends to share outcome data with the funding foundation — but have we asked what form that data takes? If it includes any individually identifiable information, even aggregate data with small cell sizes, it may re-identify participants. The consent form must specify exactly what is shared, with whom, and in what form. Consent to "share outcomes with our funder" is not meaningful if the participant cannot understand what that means.

## Minors' Data Architecture and the Firewall Around Juvenile Records

* **The firewall between referral-source information and what mentors know will be violated accidentally.** A well-intentioned program coordinator, trying to give a mentor useful context, will informally share that "Jordan came to us through the court system." That single disclosure can permanently alter the mentor's perception and the teen's reputation in the congregation. The mitigation is not policy alone — it requires structural separation: mentors should never see referral-source fields in any system, and intake staff should be trained to give mentors behavioral context ("Jordan responds well to structure") without source context.

* **FERPA-inspired policies are not FERPA compliance.** The organization is a faith-based nonprofit, not an educational institution, and FERPA does not directly apply. Have we considered that the organization may be receiving FERPA-protected data from school counselors who are bound by FERPA but passing it to an entity that is not? The receiving organization needs data-sharing agreements with each institutional partner that explicitly define permissible uses and prohibit re-disclosure. Without these agreements, the school counselor's referral may itself be a FERPA violation.

* **Access control is meaningless without audit trails.** A policy limiting juvenile-justice data to the program director is unenforceable without a log of who accessed what and when. Spreadsheets and shared drives do not provide this. If the organization stores sensitive referral data in Google Drive or a shared folder, any staff member with access to the folder can read it — and there is no record of having done so. The program should use a purpose-built case management system with role-based access and access logging, even if that means a modest technology investment.

* **Data minimization is hardest at intake.** The intake process creates pressure to collect everything — referral context, family history, school records — because it feels irresponsible not to. But every piece of sensitive data collected is a liability. The program should define in advance the minimum data set required to make a safe, effective match, and decline to collect anything beyond that. Information that "might be useful someday" creates risk without proportionate benefit.

* **The congregation community is not a confidential environment.** Unlike a clinical setting, a faith-based community is a social network where information travels. If a teen's mentor, their mentor's spouse, and two other congregation members all know a youth through the program, sensitive information about that youth will diffuse through informal conversation. The program's confidentiality training must address this social reality explicitly, not just recite legal obligations.

## The Privacy Paradox for Court-Referred Youth

* **Trust cannot be designed into a form — it is built through behavior over time.** The scenario of Jordan sitting across from a stranger filling in details from a case file illustrates a structural problem that intake redesign alone cannot solve. Jordan's trust will be determined by whether the adults in the program consistently behave in trustworthy ways — not by the aesthetics of a consent conversation. That said, the intake process can actively damage nascent trust: a clinical, form-heavy intake in a church conference room signals "processing" rather than "welcoming." The mitigation is to separate the relational welcome (meet-and-greet, tour, informal conversation) from the administrative intake (paperwork), and never conduct them simultaneously.

* **"You are protected" is a claim that must be demonstrated, not stated.** Have we considered that Jordan has likely heard institutional assurances before, from courts, schools, and social workers, and has evidence from lived experience that those assurances are not always kept? Telling Jordan "your information is private" during intake is insufficient. The program should show Jordan what privacy looks like in practice: who sees which information, where it is stored, what happens if someone asks about them. Concrete specificity is more credible than abstract reassurance.

* **The congregation setting creates a dual-identity problem for juvenile-justice youth.** Jordan may have a congregant identity (if their family attends the church) and a program participant identity — and may be managing what different people in that space know about them. The program must consider whether other congregation members, who see Jordan at Sunday services, know Jordan is in a mentorship program for "at-risk teens." That label, however well-intentioned, carries stigma. The program's name and framing within the congregation should not mark participants as court-involved or at-risk.

* **Informed consent for a youth like Jordan requires genuine comprehension, not just a signature.** A 16-year-old who has been through the juvenile justice system may have learned to sign what adults put in front of them without reading it. True informed consent requires checking for understanding: can Jordan explain back what the program will do with their information? Does Jordan know they can withdraw consent? Is there a trusted adult (not the program itself) who Jordan can consult before signing? Consent processes designed for administrative efficiency will systematically fail youth who most need genuine protection.
