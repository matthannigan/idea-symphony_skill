# Volunteer Screening, Background Checks, and Disqualifying Criteria - The Pragmatist

---

## Disqualifying Criteria Calibration Across The Categorical-To-Contextual Spectrum

* **Start with a two-tier policy.** A practical approach is to separate absolute disqualifiers (sex offenses involving minors, crimes of violence against minors, active registry status) from contextual review cases (old non-violent felonies, expunged records, misdemeanors over 10 years old). Categorical disqualifiers require no discretion and reduce legal exposure; contextual cases go to a small review panel — program director plus a board designee — with a documented decision rationale kept on file. This separates the clear-cut from the nuanced without pretending there's a simple formula.

* **Use state law as your floor, not your ceiling.** Many states publish specific lists of disqualifying offenses for youth-serving programs; verify your jurisdiction's requirements in the first 30 days. From there, add only the additional restrictions you can defend with a written rationale. Over-screening loses volunteers; under-screening creates liability. The goal is a policy document the board has formally adopted — so if a volunteer later causes harm, the organization can show it conducted a compliant check and followed its stated criteria. That paper trail is your primary legal protection.

* **Assign contextual review responsibility explicitly.** The worst outcome is a gray-area case that drifts through without a documented decision. A practical minimum is a two-person review panel with a written checklist: offense type, time elapsed, evidence of rehabilitation, nature of direct access to youth. The panel documents its conclusion in one paragraph and retains it in the volunteer file. This creates accountability, defensibility, and a learning record that improves future decisions. Assigning this responsibility to named roles — not just "leadership" — ensures it actually happens.

* **Budget for compliant checks from day one.** FBI fingerprint checks typically run $20–$50 per volunteer; state-level criminal history checks vary by jurisdiction. For 40 volunteers, that's a $1,000–$2,000 line item. Build it into the $15K seed grant budget rather than treating it as optional overhead. Background check costs are also an eligible use for most child-safety program grants and a strong indicator to funders that you take risk management seriously.

* **Revisit criteria annually, not never.** Disqualifying criteria are often set once and forgotten. A practical improvement is a once-a-year policy review — timed to the board's annual calendar — that asks: Did any incidents occur? Did comparable programs update their criteria? Has state law changed? This keeps the policy current, creates a documented governance record, and prevents the organization from running on a background check policy that is years out of date.

## Screening As A Sequential Funnel, Not A Single Gate

* **Plan active match capacity around realistic funnel yield, not headcount.** In comparable faith-based and community mentorship programs, the most significant attrition typically occurs between initial interest and completing training — not at background check. A reasonable planning assumption for a first cohort: 40 expressions of interest yield perhaps 25 completed applications, 20 cleared background checks, 15 who complete training, and 10–12 active matches by launch day. Build your first-year program model around 10 matches, not 40. This prevents overpromising to referred youth and to funders.

* **Make the application step low-friction and fast.** The biggest practical risk at the application stage is a multi-week lag between expressing interest and receiving an application. Volunteers are most motivated immediately after they raise their hand. A practical approach: prepare a one-page digital application (name, contact, brief motivation statement, consent to background check) that can be completed in under 10 minutes and submitted online. Capture interest before it cools. The substantive screening — interview, training — comes later in the funnel.

* **Use training completion as a self-selection filter.** Training is often where volunteers who are uncertain about the commitment quietly exit the pipeline. Rather than viewing this as loss, treat it as the funnel working correctly: people who complete training are more likely to fulfill their one-year commitment. A practical minimum training requirement is 4–6 hours covering mandatory reporting, boundary expectations, and program logistics. Keep it focused and scheduled on a weekend day to reduce dropout from scheduling conflict.

* **Track funnel conversion rates from day one.** Even in a first cohort, building a simple spreadsheet that records each volunteer's status at each stage — applied, background check initiated, background check cleared, interview completed, training completed, matched — creates data you can use to improve the next cohort. Which stage has the longest lag? Where do people drop off without explanation? This is free institutional knowledge if you track it; expensive guesswork if you don't.

## Parallel Screening Architectures From Adjacent Domains

* **Borrow the tiered supervision model, not the full gig-platform stack.** Gig platforms like Uber use tiered screening to determine who can work without oversight versus who requires additional checks, not to decide who gets any access at all. The directly transferable lesson for a faith-based mentorship program is this: distinguish volunteers who will work in supervised program settings (group activities, church-based meetings) from those conducting unsupervised one-on-one meetings in community locations. Apply a higher screening bar — and potentially a probationary supervised period — for the latter. This preserves pipeline while calibrating risk to the actual exposure level.

* **Adopt BBBS's two-check standard as a practical baseline.** Big Brothers Big Sisters requires both a national sex offender registry check and a state criminal background check as minimums. This two-check combination is widely recognized, defensible to funders and insurers, and costs under $50 per volunteer through most background check vendors. It's a practical starting point that your liability insurer and any future grant auditors will recognize. Add fingerprinting if your state mandates it for youth-serving nonprofits, which it may.

* **Foster care licensing criteria provide a ready-made contextual review framework.** Licensed foster care programs have developed robust, legally tested criteria for evaluating applicants with prior records — weighing offense type, time elapsed, and demonstrated rehabilitation. Rather than building your own framework from scratch, review your state's foster care background check guidance (usually public) and adapt its categorical structure for volunteers. This borrows legal and programmatic work already done, reduces the time needed to draft your own policy, and gives you a defensible precedent if criteria are ever challenged.

* **Keep the architecture simple enough to actually operate.** The risk of importing too much from gig platforms or enterprise trust-and-safety systems is building a screening process that a small faith-based organization with limited administrative staff cannot actually run. A practical approach might be: use an established national background check vendor (Checkr, Sterling, or a BBBS-affiliated provider) that handles compliance and registry checks automatically, reserve your staff time for the interview and training steps where human judgment adds value, and document everything in a simple volunteer file. Complexity in the screening system is a cost; simplicity is a feature when operating at this scale.
