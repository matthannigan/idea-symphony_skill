# Volunteer Screening, Background Checks, and Disqualifying Criteria - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Disqualifying Criteria Calibration Across The Categorical-To-Contextual Spectrum

* **Summary.** The legal-compliance floor is not the same as the organizational risk threshold, and conflating them creates false safety. Have we considered that state-mandated disqualifying offense lists were designed as legislative minimums, not as endorsements that anything above the floor is acceptable? A faith org pairing adults one-on-one with adjudicated teens faces reputational and civil liability that dwarfs the legal standard — if a volunteer with an old assault conviction causes harm, "we followed state law" will not protect the organization in a wrongful supervision lawsuit or in the court of community opinion.

* **Summary.** The "contextual review for borderline cases" framework sounds inclusive and sophisticated, but it quietly concentrates enormous legal exposure in whoever makes those judgment calls. Have we considered who specifically owns the contextual review decision, and whether that person has professional indemnification? If it's the program director or a volunteer board committee rather than a licensed social worker or attorney, the organization may be creating personal liability for staff that outpaces any benefit from expanding the volunteer pool.

* **Summary.** One potential challenge is that expunged records present an asymmetric risk picture that the word "expunged" obscures. Expungement means the offense is sealed from standard background checks — it does not mean the conduct did not occur, and it does not appear in FBI fingerprint results in most states. The program's written policy should explicitly acknowledge whether it is relying on self-disclosure for expunged records and what the consequences of non-disclosure are, rather than treating expungement as a clean slate by default. Failing to address this gap leaves a well-meaning policy with a visible hole.

* **Summary.** Have we considered that defining organizational thresholds for borderline cases in a written policy document creates a discoverable paper trail? If a volunteer clears a contextual review and later causes harm, plaintiffs' counsel will subpoena that policy and ask why someone with that offense history was approved. A policy that articulates nuanced criteria ("we weigh rehabilitation evidence and time elapsed") may actually invite more scrutiny than a conservative categorical rule, unless it is drafted with legal counsel explicitly reviewing the language. The cure here is to involve legal counsel before the policy is finalized, not after.

* **Summary.** Who bears legal responsibility is a question that deserves a direct answer rather than distributed ambiguity. One potential challenge is that small faith-based nonprofits sometimes assume their umbrella liability insurance covers all volunteer-related harm — but many volunteer liability policies explicitly exclude harm caused by volunteers with known prior offenses that the organization chose to approve over standard criteria. Before calibrating any contextual threshold, the organization should obtain written confirmation from its insurer that borderline-case approvals do not void coverage. Discovering that exclusion after an incident is catastrophic.

## Screening As A Sequential Funnel, Not A Single Gate

* **Summary.** Have we considered that framing the funnel as an attrition problem to be minimized may be exactly backwards? In programs serving high-risk youth, the funnel's friction is a feature, not a bug. Volunteers who drop out during a multi-step screening process were probably not prepared for the commitment of a year-long, weekly, one-on-one relationship with a teen in crisis. The real question is not how to reduce funnel drop-off but whether the funnel is filtering the right attributes — sustained commitment and resilience to difficult relationships, not just background check compliance.

* **Summary.** One potential challenge is that projecting active-match capacity from funnel completion rates requires actually knowing those rates, and comparable faith-based program data is sparse and often self-reported with survivor bias. Programs that publish funnel data tend to be well-resourced and well-managed — their completion rates are not safely generalizable to a small church-based program launching from scratch. If the program commits to a funder that it will achieve 30 active matches by month six, and funnel reality delivers 12, the grant relationship is at risk. Capacity commitments should be made conservatively and explicitly flagged as estimates.

* **Summary.** The funnel model assumes that the steps are sequential and independent, but in practice the interview step often reveals disqualifying information that the background check missed — undisclosed relationships with youth in the target population, personal motivations that raise safeguarding concerns, or boundary-setting deficits. Have we considered that placing the interview after the background check (because the check is administratively easy to schedule) may be the wrong order? Flipping the sequence — phone screen first, background check only for those who pass the interview — saves money and reduces the false sense of security that a clean background check confers.

* **Summary.** Have we considered that the 40-volunteer headcount is not a stable baseline? It represents people who expressed interest at a single congregational announcement. Interest-to-application conversion in volunteer programs typically runs 30–50%, and interest expressed in a congregational setting is often social rather than considered. Building program infrastructure around 40 volunteers when 12–20 actual applications is a realistic expectation creates an overbuilt intake process and understaffed delivery — a mismatch that demoralizes staff and produces unmet expectations with funders.

## Parallel Screening Architectures From Adjacent Domains

* **Summary.** Have we considered that the gig-platform trust-and-safety model — tiered, risk-weighted, "supervised vs. unsupervised" — was designed to solve a volume problem that does not exist here? Uber screens tens of thousands of drivers monthly; the marginal cost savings from a nuanced tiered system justify the design investment. For a program with 40 potential volunteers, a sophisticated tiered architecture is almost certainly over-engineered and creates policy complexity that small nonprofit staff cannot maintain consistently. Borrowing the conceptual framing is useful; borrowing the operational architecture is probably wrong for this context.

* **Summary.** One potential challenge with the Big Brothers Big Sisters benchmark is that BBBS has decades of litigation history, professional risk management staff, and national legal counsel shaping its screening protocols — and its standards are still periodically updated after incidents. A small faith org citing BBBS standards in its own policy may implicitly be accepting BBBS's liability exposure without BBBS's infrastructure to manage it. The better use of BBBS materials is as a floor-setting reference, not as a plug-and-play system, and the organization should document explicitly where it diverges from BBBS and why.

* **Summary.** Have we considered that licensed foster care's screening architecture — home studies, fingerprinting, reference checks, financial disclosure — is unusually rigorous precisely because foster parents have unsupervised 24/7 custodial access? Applying foster-care-level screening to weekly mentors who meet in supervised public or organizational spaces may actually harm the program by excluding willing, qualified volunteers over requirements calibrated to a different risk profile. The lesson from foster care is not the specific gates but the principle: calibrate screening depth to actual access level and supervision structure.

* **Summary.** All three adjacent-domain models share an assumption that the screening organization has consistent administrative bandwidth to run the process and maintain records. One potential challenge that none of them surface clearly is what happens when screening volume spikes — say, all 40 volunteers submit applications in the same month — and the faith org's administrative staff (in-kind from the church) is simultaneously managing other church programs. Bottlenecks in screening create awkward holding patterns where enthusiastic volunteers go cold, and there is no graceful way to communicate "we're behind on background checks." The program needs either a dedicated screening coordinator or a realistic capacity agreement with the church administrative staff before launch.
