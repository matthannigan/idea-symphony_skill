# Sustainability, Endings, and Long-Term Horizon - The Pragmatist

---

## Match-Termination Protocol And The Year-One Ending Without Abandonment

* **Build a graduated exit ladder, not a hard stop.** A practical approach: define three distinct match statuses — Active, Transitioning, and Alumni — each with written criteria and a coordinator check-in. When a match isn't working, the pair moves to Transitioning status, triggering a structured 30-day wind-down with at least two facilitated closure meetings before the relationship formally ends. This prevents abrupt abandonment while keeping the process manageable for a small staff.

* **Separate the contract from the connection.** The one-year commitment is a minimum floor for the volunteer, not a ceiling for the relationship. Document this distinction in the mentor agreement from day one. At month eleven, coordinators send every pair a "continuation form" with three options: renew formally, continue informally with periodic check-ins, or close with a celebration. This simple administrative step normalizes continuation without requiring policy overhaul.

* **Create a re-match pathway that treats teens as continuing clients.** When a match ends — whether at one year or earlier — the teen's file stays active for 90 days while staff works to identify a new mentor. A practical minimum: maintain a small reserve pool of trained mentors on standby (three to five volunteers who completed training but haven't been matched yet). This prevents the "mentor slot opens, teen waits six months" gap that does measurable harm.

* **Measure harm directly, not just relationship quality.** Track three concrete indicators at match close: school attendance in the 60 days following termination, any new juvenile justice contact, and whether the teen has at least one other identified adult support. These are quick to collect and give staff actionable signal — if attendance drops after matches end, the wind-down protocol needs more transition support built in.

* **Use exit interviews to distinguish failure modes.** Not all match terminations are equal. A coordinator 30-minute debrief with mentor and teen separately — structured with five standard questions — surfaces whether the problem was poor matching criteria, insufficient mentor support, or irreconcilable fit. This data costs almost nothing to collect and directly improves future matching. Log exit reason in a simple spreadsheet field from the start.

## The First 90 Days As An Investment In Five Strong Matches

* **Define "strong match" before you launch, not after.** A practical starting benchmark: the pair has met at least eight of twelve scheduled sessions, both parties report feeling heard in a brief monthly survey (five questions, five minutes), and the teen's school counselor confirms no deterioration in attendance or behavior. Five pairs meeting that bar at day ninety gives you a replicable template — and evidence for your Year 2 funder report.

* **Invest in match quality over match quantity in the pilot.** With $15K and a staff that likely includes part-time coordination, trying to place 20 pairs in 90 days risks quality collapse across all of them. A practical approach: close the first five matches with extended intake (two coordinator-facilitated orientation sessions per pair rather than one), then use what you learn to streamline the process for pairs six through fifteen. This costs more per pair upfront but dramatically lowers the failure rate.

* **Build a learning mechanism into the pilot structure.** Designate one coordinator hour per week for the first 90 days as "pilot review time" — reviewing match notes, flagging emerging problems, documenting what's working. At day 45, hold a formal mid-point review with all five mentors together (without teens) to surface patterns. At day 90, write a three-page internal brief before scaling. This is low-cost institutional memory that most small programs skip and later regret.

* **Treat mentor experience as the limiting variable.** The teens' circumstances vary enormously and are mostly outside your control. The mentor experience is the variable you can manage. In the first 90 days, over-invest in mentor support: biweekly coordinator check-ins by phone, a simple activity idea sheet distributed monthly, and a clear escalation path for mentor concerns. If mentors feel supported, they stay; if they feel abandoned, attrition compounds quickly.

## Seed-To-Sustaining Funding Bridge And The Neighborhood-Ripple Cost Of Failure

* **Build a cost model before you spend the first dollar.** A practical approach: map three budget scenarios — 15, 25, and 40 active matches — before launch. For each, calculate direct costs (training, background checks, activities stipend), coordinator time, and overhead. My estimate for a lean program: roughly $800–$1,200 per active match per year in direct costs, plus a 0.5 FTE coordinator. At 25 matches that's roughly $25–30K annually excluding staff. Know this number before you recruit your first mentor.

* **The 18-month survival question deserves an honest answer now.** Comparable community mentorship programs at this scale typically depend on three to four revenue streams: foundation grants, individual donors, in-kind contributions, and occasionally government contracts. With $15K seed funding and no established donor base, the honest probability of operating at 18 months is moderate — perhaps 50–60% — unless fundraising infrastructure is built in parallel with the program, not after. Assign this responsibility explicitly; if no one owns it, it won't happen.

* **Design a wind-down protocol as seriously as a launch plan.** A practical approach: write a one-page "orderly closure" procedure before you open your first match. It should specify: 90-day notice to all families and partners, systematic handoff of each teen to at least one other identified adult support, final documentation of all match records, and communication to referring schools and juvenile justice partners. Programs that close abruptly damage the referring ecosystem — schools stop referring to the next program, not just this one.

* **Diversify funding sources in Year 1, not Year 2.** The United Way, OJJDP mentoring grants, AmeriCorps VISTA placements, and individual donor campaigns are all viable sources for a faith-based community organization with a 20-year track record. A practical minimum: submit one additional grant application per quarter, starting in month two. A $5K grant from a second source before month six meaningfully changes your 18-month survival odds.

## A Program That Grows A Generation, Not A Cohort

* **Year-one structure is the foundation — don't over-engineer it, but don't ignore it either.** To make intergenerational emergence possible, three things need to be true from day one: program records must be maintained well enough that a 2030 mentor can read their own 2026 file, alumni must be formally tracked (not just Facebook-friended), and the program must have a name and identity distinct from any individual staff member or pastor. These are low-cost structural decisions that pay compounding dividends over 20 years.

* **The alumni-to-mentor pathway needs a concrete on-ramp, not just an aspiration.** A practical approach: in Year 3 or 4, create a formal "Junior Mentor" or "Peer Navigator" role for program alumni aged 18–22. Define it with a written job description, a modest stipend ($200–300/month), and supervision from the coordinator. This is the specific mechanism that converts a cohort program into an institution. Without it, the intergenerational vision remains a talking point.

* **Governance needs a community seat from the beginning.** If the program is governed entirely by the church leadership, it cannot evolve into a community institution. A practical minimum: create a five-person advisory committee in Year 1 that includes at least one school counselor, one parent of a current teen, and one community member who is not a congregation member. This is a low-cost structural decision that creates the governance surface area for future teen alumni to eventually participate in.

* **Twenty-year institutions require documentation discipline that most programs lack.** A practical approach: keep a shared folder (Google Drive works fine) with one document per year — program stats, lessons learned, key decisions, staff names. An hour per year, maintained consistently, creates the institutional memory that lets a 2036 program director understand what was decided and why in 2026. This costs almost nothing and is almost universally skipped.
