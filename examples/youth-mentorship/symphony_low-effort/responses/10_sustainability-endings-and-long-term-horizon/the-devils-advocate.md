# Sustainability, Endings, and Long-Term Horizon - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Match-Termination Protocol And The Year-One Ending Without Abandonment

* **The "no bad endings" assumption may be wishful thinking.** Have we considered that some matches will fail badly — mentor misconduct, teen disengagement, family crises — and that designing for a smooth off-ramp implies a level of control the program won't have? A protocol drafted in an orientation binder is different from a protocol actually used under pressure. We could address this by stress-testing the protocol in training simulations with realistic difficult scenarios, not just the clean "match wasn't clicking" case.

* **One year is an arbitrary cliff.** The commitment is framed as a floor, but it functions as a ceiling in practice — mentors hear "one year" and mentally mark an exit date. One potential challenge might be that a teen who has built genuine trust by month eleven is effectively counting down to loss, not celebration. We might reframe the commitment as "at least one year, with active renegotiation," and train mentors to surface the question of continuation proactively at month ten rather than waiting for the teen to wonder.

* **Transition "to another mentor" is often a polite fiction.** Programs frequently promise a warm handoff to a replacement mentor when a match ends, but replacement pools are rarely available on demand. Have we considered what the teen experiences when the promised handoff simply doesn't happen? The mitigation here is brutal honesty in the intake process: tell teens and guardians that matches may end and that a replacement is not guaranteed, so the emotional weight of the commitment is accurately calibrated from day one.

* **Measuring harm from match termination is harder than it sounds.** We talk about avoiding "measurable harm," but the metrics programs typically track — school attendance, recidivism, self-reported wellbeing — are noisy and lag the event by months. One potential challenge might be that a teen hides distress from program staff to protect the mentor they liked, producing clean-looking data on top of real harm. We could address this by routing post-termination check-ins through a neutral party (school counselor, not program staff) who has no stake in the outcome looking positive.

* **Teens referred through juvenile justice have less power to exit.** Have we considered that some matches feel non-voluntary to the teen — a condition of probation, an expectation from a counselor — which means "match not working" may not be something a teen feels safe voicing? A protocol designed for voluntary participants may fail to surface problems in coerced contexts. The mitigation is an explicit, private, no-consequences feedback channel that is fully separated from case management reporting.

## The First 90 Days As An Investment In Five Strong Matches

* **"Five strong matches" is a self-fulfilling success story waiting to become a myth.** Have we considered that cherry-picking the best early outcomes and calling them the foundation creates survivorship bias? The five pairs who thrive in 90 days are probably the easiest cases — motivated teens, available mentors, stable home situations. What the program actually learns from them may not transfer to the harder cases that will dominate intake once word spreads. We could address this by deliberately including at least one high-challenge match in the founding cohort and treating it as the real learning case, not a cautionary footnote.

* **90 days is too short to know if a match is strong.** One potential challenge might be that the enthusiasm of early novelty mimics genuine relationship depth. A mentor-teen pair that looks strong at day 90 — frequent meetings, upbeat check-ins — may be in the honeymoon phase, not yet tested by a hard moment. We might reframe the 90-day milestone not as a proof of strength but as a proof of functioning, reserving the "strong match" label until after the first serious conflict or disruption has been navigated.

* **What five pairs "experience and demonstrate" is almost impossible to standardize.** Have we considered that asking five different mentor-teen pairs to represent the program's potential produces five incomparable anecdotes rather than transferable lessons? The insight that matters is not what those five pairs felt but what structures, training elements, and matching decisions made them possible. We could address this by building a structured reflection protocol at the 90-day mark that asks pairs the same questions about what program supports helped versus what they improvised on their own.

* **Early success raises stakeholder expectations in ways that constrain future decisions.** If the founding five produce compelling stories, funders and leadership will anchor to those stories. One potential challenge might be that the program then faces pressure to replicate the feel of those early matches — the warm, narrative-friendly ones — rather than honestly serving the harder population it was designed for. The mitigation is to communicate early-stage results explicitly as "learning phase" outputs, not as proof of concept, and to track the ratio of high-challenge-to-easy intakes over time.

## Seed-To-Sustaining Funding Bridge And The Neighborhood-Ripple Cost Of Failure

* **$15K does not fund a learning curve.** Have we considered that most of the seed grant will be consumed by setup costs — background checks, training, insurance, materials — before a single relationship has a chance to prove its value? The program may reach its 12-month report date with insufficient relationship data to make a compelling case for Year 2 funding, not because the program failed but because the grant timeline was mismatched to the relationship timeline. We could address this by building a 6-month "proof of concept" report at months 8–9, giving funders early signal before the grant closes.

* **The 40-match scale assumption may be financially incoherent.** One potential challenge might be that per-match costs don't fall meaningfully as the program scales from 15 to 40 pairs, because the limiting factor is staff coordination time, not materials. A staff model that works at 15 matches may require a second coordinator at 25 and a third at 40, producing step-function cost increases that no bridge funding plan accounts for. We might commission a blunt cost-per-match analysis before publishing any growth projections to funders.

* **Orderly wind-down is easier to say than to design.** Have we considered that an "orderly wind-down protocol" is meaningless unless it's pre-funded and pre-staffed? When funding collapses, the people who would execute a graceful closure are the first to lose their jobs. We could address this by building a small restricted reserve — even $2,000 from the seed grant — explicitly designated for wind-down operations, and designating a volunteer board member (not staff) as the wind-down executor.

* **The neighborhood cost of failure is asymmetric and underappreciated.** Programs often calculate failure risk as "money lost" or "participants served minus participants who completed." But for this population, a program that recruits a vulnerable teen, builds real trust, and then closes abruptly may produce measurable harm — increased disengagement, reinforced distrust of institutions — that falls on the school and juvenile justice systems, not on the program that failed. One potential challenge might be that no one in the planning process owns that downstream liability. The mitigation is a formal conversation with the school counselors and juvenile justice liaisons who make referrals, asking them directly: "If we fold in year two, what does that mean for the kids you sent us?"

* **Comparable programs' funding sources are less transferable than they look.** Have we considered that the faith-based community organizations that successfully bridge to sustained funding often do so through congregation tithing or major-donor relationships that took years to cultivate — sources that can't be replicated on the $15K grant timeline? Copying their funding mix in a pitch deck without having their donor base is a plan that looks like a plan. We could address this by identifying two or three specific named funders in the region who have funded comparable programs, making direct contact in month three, not month eleven.

## A Program That Grows A Generation, Not A Cohort

* **The 20-year vision may be a governance liability in year one.** Have we considered that articulating an intergenerational institution design this early — before the program has matched a single pair — creates expectations that attract the wrong kind of board member? People drawn to legacy-building projects may not be well-suited to the unglamorous work of background-check administration and trauma-informed supervision that makes year one survive. We could address this by separating the 20-year vision into a "North Star" document that is not shared publicly until the program has at least two full cohort cycles behind it.

* **Teens-becoming-mentors-becoming-elders assumes continuity of place.** One potential challenge might be that the teens this program targets are among the most likely to relocate — for housing instability, for college, for economic opportunity, for justice system involvement. An intergenerational model that depends on alumni returning to the neighborhood may systematically exclude the teens who most successfully exit the conditions that made them "at-risk." We might consider building alumni engagement infrastructure that works remotely, not just locally.

* **"Program elder" and "community trustee" are roles with no existing precedent or compensation.** Have we considered that inviting former participants into governance roles without compensating them reproduces a pattern where the organization extracts community credibility while the community member absorbs the time cost? The mitigation is to design meaningful stipends and decision-making power into these roles from the beginning, not as an afterthought when someone finally asks why the board of trustees doesn't look like the community it serves.

* **Intergenerational emergence requires institutional memory, which is fragile.** The cultural continuity needed for a teen to one day become a program elder depends on the organization maintaining coherent identity across leadership transitions, funding crises, and strategy pivots. One potential challenge might be that faith-based organizations are particularly vulnerable to this — a new pastor, a congregation split, a shift in mission emphasis — and 20-year institutional memory is genuinely rare. We could address this by investing in documentation and alumni relationships early, treating them as infrastructure rather than nice-to-haves, so institutional memory lives in the community, not just in staff files.
