# Matching, Power, and Youth Agency - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Matching Criteria, Predictors Of Retention, And Matching As A Relational Craft

* **Summary: Shared interests are not the same as a shared relationship capacity.** Have we considered that most matching frameworks lean heavily on surface-level similarity — hobbies, neighborhood, demographic overlap — because it is easy to operationalize, not because it predicts retention? Research on programs like Big Brothers Big Sisters consistently finds that mentor *commitment and reliability* outpredicts almost every matching variable, including race concordance. If the program optimizes intake criteria around interests, it may screen out the most reliable volunteers who happen to share nothing obvious with their assigned teen. Mitigation: audit the matching rubric against retention data from comparable programs before finalizing it; weight mentor follow-through history over demographic similarity.

* **Summary: "Relationship-centered craft" can become a staffing bottleneck.** One potential challenge is that treating matching as a thoughtful, individualized practice sounds excellent but requires skilled staff time to execute — time a $15K seed grant and a church administrative assistant almost certainly cannot sustain at scale. If matching is designed as an artisanal process that only one coordinator can do well, the program has a single point of failure. A coordinator departure mid-year could freeze new matches for months. Mitigation: document the matching rubric explicitly enough that any trained volunteer could apply it, and build a secondary reviewer into the workflow from day one.

* **Summary: Shared aspiration can mask an incompatible relational style.** Have we considered that two people who share a stated goal — say, "go to college" — may have completely incompatible ways of processing accountability, emotion, and conflict? A mentor who tracks weekly milestones may be suffocating to a teen who needs space to fail quietly. Personality or communication-style compatibility is genuinely harder to assess than interests, but arguably more predictive of whether the relationship survives the first difficult conversation. Mitigation: include a simple structured communication-style self-assessment in both teen and mentor intake, and flag pairings where styles diverge sharply for early check-in at the 60-day mark.

* **Summary: The 12-month retention goal may select for teens with enough stability to sustain a relationship.** Have we considered that optimizing matching criteria for retention risk-stratification inadvertently filters toward teens who already have adequate relational infrastructure — those with engaged parents, stable housing, regular school attendance? The teens who present as "poor match risk" on a retention model may be exactly the teens who most need the intervention. Using retention probability as a matching input will systematically under-serve the highest-need youth. Mitigation: separate the matching function (who goes with whom) from the program-design function (what extra supports the highest-risk pairings receive), and avoid using predicted retention as a reason to delay or decline a match.

## Whose Criteria Drive Matching And How Teens Shape Who They Are Paired With

* **Summary: Youth preference surveys can extract a veneer of agency without delivering the real thing.** Have we considered that asking teens to fill out an interest form and then routing them into a coordinator-controlled match algorithm creates the *appearance* of youth voice while leaving all actual power with adults? If the teen's stated preferences can be overridden whenever a coordinator judges a different pairing "better," the form is theater. Teens who have been through juvenile justice or school intervention systems are often acutely skilled at recognizing when their stated preferences are being solicited but not weighted. The damage to trust if they recognize the theater may be worse than having no preference process at all. Mitigation: be explicit with teens about exactly which preferences are binding and which are advisory inputs — and honor the binding ones.

* **Summary: "What happens when the match feels wrong to the teen" needs a non-catastrophic offramp.** One potential challenge is that programs typically design match dissolution as a formal escalation event — a meeting with staff, a form, an explanation — which is a high barrier for a teen who may have learned that voicing complaints leads to punishment or removal from services. If the only way to exit a bad match is to trigger a visible process, many teens will silently disengage instead, producing match dropout statistics that look like "low motivation" rather than "bad match design." Mitigation: normalize rematch as a routine option with no fault assigned, and give teens a private, low-friction channel — a text to a neutral staff member — to flag that a match isn't working.

* **Summary: Adult-designed "youth-voice" components risk reproducing adult frameworks with teen inputs.** Have we considered that a youth advisory panel or preference survey designed by adults will naturally produce options that adults have already imagined? The teen who wants a mentor who will play video games with them, skip structured activities, and just "hang out" is expressing a legitimate relational need — but most adult-designed intake forms will not present that option. Mitigation: before finalizing the matching intake instrument, conduct at least two unstructured focus groups with teens from the target population, and include verbatim teen-generated language in the actual preference menu rather than staff-translated summaries.

* **Summary: The consequences of a bad match fall asymmetrically on the teen.** Have we considered that when a match fails, the mentor goes home to their regular life and the program staff document a program outcome — but the teen may internalize the failure as evidence that they are unlovable, difficult, or not worth the investment? This asymmetry is especially sharp for youth with attachment injuries from prior adult abandonment. A program that treats match failure as a neutral operational event, rather than a potentially harmful relational rupture for the teen, is likely to produce invisible harm that never appears in outcome data. Mitigation: build a structured rematch conversation protocol that explicitly names what went well, frames the ending as a fit problem rather than a youth problem, and provides a brief bridge support period before the new match begins.

## Power Dynamics And What "Voluntary" Means For Court-Involved Youth

* **Summary: "Voluntary" participation connected to case status is a legal fiction with real consequences.** Have we considered that when a juvenile justice referral explicitly names mentorship participation in a court case file, or when a case worker routinely asks whether a youth is enrolled, the program ceases to function as voluntary in any meaningful sense? A teen who understands — correctly or incorrectly — that declining will affect their case outcome will say yes regardless of genuine interest. This coercion may not be intended by the program, but the referral pathway creates it structurally. Mitigation: establish a written firewall with juvenile justice partners specifying that participation status will not be shared with case workers or used in case reviews, and communicate this boundary clearly to teens and families at intake.

* **Summary: Structural power imbalances are not dissolved by warmth or good intentions.** One potential challenge is that training mentors to be "relatable" and "non-judgmental" addresses the interpersonal layer of the power differential while leaving the structural layer untouched. An adult volunteer still has the ability to report the teen under mandatory reporting obligations, still has access to staff if the relationship becomes difficult, and still faces essentially no consequences if the relationship ends. The teen has none of these options. If staff frame the relationship as a friendship or an equal partnership, they may inadvertently teach teens to trust the framing rather than remain appropriately self-protective. Mitigation: be honest with teens, in age-appropriate language, about what the actual power structure is — who reports to whom, what the mentor's obligations are — so that youth can make informed decisions about what to share.

* **Summary: Power-balancing program features can introduce new paternalism.** Have we considered that program features designed to give teens more power — letting them choose meeting locations, letting them set the agenda — can become a new form of adult approval-seeking if staff praise compliant teens for "exercising their agency"? True agency is not rewarded by adults; it is expected, including when a teen uses it in ways the program did not anticipate. A teen who refuses a mentor's suggestion, declines a program activity, or wants to end a match early is also exercising agency. The program's structural test is whether it responds to unwelcome exercises of youth agency with the same affirmation as welcome ones. Mitigation: review intake, program, and exit documentation for language that frames adult-preferred choices as "healthy" and youth-preferred divergences as "resistance" or "challenges."

* **Summary: Long-term, the power imbalance may compound rather than diminish.** Have we considered the multi-year trajectory? A teen who builds genuine trust with a mentor over 12+ months is also accumulating relational dependency on a single adult who, statistically, will eventually leave the program — through burnout, life changes, or program funding loss. For youth whose histories include repeated adult departures, a warm mentorship that ends may produce net harm by reopening attachment wounds. This is not an argument against mentorship, but it is an argument for designing the relationship's inevitable ending as carefully as its beginning. Mitigation: build an explicit relationship-closure protocol that begins conversation about future contact and transition well before the formal program period ends.

## Drift Toward Serving The Easy Cases And The JJ-Referral As A Self-Fulfilling Dynamic

* **Summary: Program metrics incentivize the very drift they should prevent.** Have we considered that if the program measures success by match retention rates, it will — without anyone intending this — create organizational pressure to protect retention numbers by routing better matches to harder cases? Coordinators will feel this pressure implicitly: a stable, warm match with a high-risk teen is tempting to preserve by pairing them with the program's most experienced mentor, which is exactly the dynamic that leaves inexperienced mentors with the most difficult work. Mitigation: disaggregate outcome metrics by referral source and initial risk profile, and set explicit targets for the distribution of experienced mentors across the risk spectrum.

* **Summary: Mentor training on "difficult cases" can backfire by marking youth as problems before the relationship starts.** One potential challenge is that well-intentioned training that prepares mentors for trauma, erratic behavior, and trust deficits in JJ-referred youth effectively teaches mentors to enter those relationships in a heightened vigilance state — scanning for the warning signs they were trained to recognize, finding them, and confirming the prediction. This is a self-fulfilling dynamic that operates below the level of conscious bias. Mitigation: restructure mentor training to lead with youth strengths and aspirations rather than risk profiles; provide contextual information about individual youth circumstances only to mentors who are specifically matched with youth who have consented to that disclosure.

* **Summary: The "easy case" problem may be a staffing design problem more than a matching design problem.** Have we considered that the drift toward easier cases happens most aggressively when a single coordinator manages both matching and ongoing relationship support? If the same person who is trying to maintain overall program health also has to manage a cluster of difficult matches, they will — consciously or not — manage toward stability. Mitigation: structurally separate the intake/matching function from the ongoing support function, and assign the most intensive mentor support to staff with specific training in trauma-informed relational work, not to whichever coordinator has bandwidth.

* **Summary: Referral-source bias may be invisible in aggregate data but severe at the individual level.** Have we considered that even if the program can demonstrate statistically similar outcomes across referral sources, individual coordinators and mentors may maintain strong differential expectations that play out in micro-interactions invisible to program measurement? A mentor who was informally told a teen is "from the JJ pipeline" may unconsciously bring more structure, less playfulness, and more frequent check-ins with staff — all of which a teen could correctly read as surveillance rather than care. Mitigation: implement a structured referral-source blind intake protocol where the matching coordinator does not see referral source until after a provisional match is proposed.

## Teens As The Safety Architects

* **Summary: Youth-designed safety protocols face a credibility test the first time an adult overrides them.** Have we considered that convening a youth advisory panel to draft boundary frameworks and escalation procedures is a powerful symbolic act — and a potentially harmful one if the program subsequently modifies, overrides, or fails to implement what teens designed? Youth who have already learned that their input is solicited but not honored will treat the co-design process as evidence of bad faith rather than as genuine participation. The first time a staff member says "we value your input, but legally we can't do it that way," the protocol loses its legitimacy with the very teens it was meant to serve. Mitigation: define the boundaries of the co-design process honestly upfront — be explicit about which aspects are fully in scope for teen design and which have hard legal constraints — and commit in writing to implementing what teens produce within those bounds.

* **Summary: The teens available to design safety protocols may not represent the teens who most need protection.** One potential challenge is that youth advisory panels almost invariably attract teens who already have enough social capital, verbal fluency, and comfort with institutional authority to participate in an organized process. The teens most likely to experience boundary violations, coercive dynamics, or exploitation — those with the most trauma, the most distrust of adults, and the most experience with systems that failed them — are systematically underrepresented in co-design processes. A safety protocol designed by the most functional teens in the cohort may not address the most dangerous situations facing the most vulnerable. Mitigation: supplement the advisory panel with anonymous structured input mechanisms — written prompts, one-on-one intake conversations with a neutral staff person — that give non-panel teens a genuine channel to surface safety concerns they would not raise in a group.

* **Summary: Adult-designed policy has a track record; youth-designed policy has unknown failure modes.** Have we considered that the claim that adult-designed safety policies have never produced what youth design might produce is almost certainly true — but this observation cuts both ways? Youth may also design protocols that have blind spots adults would have caught: inadequate provisions for escalation when a trusted adult is the source of harm, inconsistent enforcement by staff who were not part of the design process, or boundary frameworks that work among the teens who designed them but fail with populations experiencing different forms of vulnerability. Mitigation: treat youth co-design not as a replacement for legal and organizational review but as a required input layer that precedes it — teen-drafted protocols go through both peer review within the panel and formal legal/programmatic review, with changes made transparently and with teen input.

* **Summary: The co-design process itself needs a power audit.** Have we considered that the staff facilitator of a youth advisory panel holds enormous agenda-setting power — and may unconsciously steer the group away from conclusions that would be inconvenient for the organization? A youth panel that produces a safety protocol recommending that all one-on-one meetings occur in public spaces with windows might be quietly redirected toward a protocol that preserves the private meeting option the organization's donors and mentors prefer. Mitigation: bring in an external facilitator with youth participatory design experience for the co-design sessions, and give the panel access to a youth-advocate advisor — someone whose role is to represent teen interests rather than program interests — throughout the process.
