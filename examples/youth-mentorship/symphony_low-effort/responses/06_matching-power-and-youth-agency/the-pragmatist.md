# Matching, Power, and Youth Agency - The Pragmatist

---

## Matching Criteria, Predictors Of Retention, And Matching As A Relational Craft

* **Start with shared interests, not demographics.** Research on BBBS and similar programs consistently shows that shared activities and communication style outperform demographic similarity as 12-month retention predictors. A practical matching intake captures three things from each teen: two or three activities they actually enjoy, how they prefer to communicate (texting vs. in-person, structured vs. unstructured time), and one thing they want help with. Cross-reference that with mentor intake data using a simple two-page matrix before any face-to-face interview.

* **Build a structured introduction period before finalizing matches.** Rather than committing to a permanent pairing upfront, run a four-week provisional match with a 30-day check-in. This lowers the stakes for both sides, gives coordinators real behavioral data, and surfaces mismatches early when they are still easy to correct. The added coordination burden is about two hours per pair — worthwhile against the cost of a failed year-long match.

* **Treat proximity as a hidden retention variable.** Travel time is rarely listed in matching criteria, but it predicts dropout. For a $15K program with volunteer mentors, a match that requires a 45-minute bus ride will fail at a higher rate than one that does not. Build a travel-time filter into intake: flag any pairing where the teen must travel more than 20 minutes to a meeting location and prioritize on-site or school-adjacent alternatives before confirming the match.

* **Use a coordinator as a matching broker, not a matching algorithm.** A spreadsheet match is a starting point, not a final answer. The coordinator should meet briefly with each teen one-on-one before finalizing — not to assess deficits, but to hear what kind of adult relationship the teen is hoping for. That conversation, even 20 minutes, adds qualitative signal that no form captures and gives teens a stake in the outcome.

## Whose Criteria Drive Matching And How Teens Shape Who They Are Paired With

* **Give teens a structured voice before any adult criteria are applied.** A practical youth-input form asks four concrete questions: What kind of activities do you want to do with your mentor? What do you want your mentor to know about you? Is there anything that would make this feel uncomfortable? Is there anything that would make this feel good? Answers go to the matching coordinator before any adult referral notes are reviewed, so teen preferences shape the frame rather than reacting to it.

* **Create a low-stakes rematch pathway teens can use without explanation.** The clearest signal that a match feels wrong is that the teen stops showing up. A practical protocol gives teens a named, confidential channel — a text line to the program coordinator — where they can say "this isn't working" without having to justify it to anyone, especially not to the mentor. The coordinator then manages the conversation with the mentor. Removing the burden of explanation from the teen is the minimum viable version of youth agency.

* **Train mentors that a teen requesting a rematch is a success indicator, not a failure.** Programmatically, a teen who asks for a different mentor has engaged with the program enough to advocate for themselves — that is the desired behavior. If the match-termination rate is zero, it likely means teens are not being given real exit options. A practical target: expect 10–15% of initial matches to request adjustment in the first 60 days, and build that into coordinator workload planning from day one.

* **Separate referral notes from matching decisions.** School counselor and juvenile justice referral documentation describes problems, not the whole person. A practical workflow gives matching coordinators a one-page teen profile built from the teen's own intake responses first, and only references referral documentation to flag safety considerations — not to characterize the teen for the mentor. This is a procedural change that costs nothing and reduces the risk that adult-assigned labels drive pairing logic.

## Power Dynamics And What "Voluntary" Means For Court-Involved Youth

* **Name the coercion openly in the intake conversation.** For court-involved teens, "voluntary" is a legal fiction if participation is tied to case outcomes. A practical approach acknowledges this directly in intake: the coordinator explains, plainly, that participation will not be reported to the court and that declining or leaving will not affect their case status. This requires a clear written agreement with the referring juvenile justice office — negotiate it before accepting any JJ referrals, not after.

* **Redesign the intake setting to reduce institutional cues.** Holding intake at the church building, with the program coordinator rather than a caseworker or counselor, immediately reduces the institutional power signal. Small logistics matter: teen brings a parent or trusted adult if they want, the meeting is scheduled around the teen's availability rather than the office schedule, there is no uniform or official-looking paperwork. These are zero-cost structural adjustments that shift the relational frame.

* **Build in opt-out moments at regular intervals, not just at the start.** Informed consent is not a one-time event. A practical protocol builds a 60-day and 120-day "do you want to continue?" check-in into the standard coordinator workflow — a brief, private conversation separate from the mentor relationship. This gives court-involved teens ongoing, legitimate exit points and makes clear that staying is a real choice, not an expectation.

* **Monitor for mentors who treat court-involved status as the teen's primary identity.** Power imbalance is also relational, not just structural. A practical safeguard includes a monthly mentor check-in question: "How are you talking about how you met this teen with others?" Mentors who default to describing teens through their referral source are reinforcing a deficit frame. Catch this in supervision early, before it shapes the mentoring relationship.

## Drift Toward Serving The Easy Cases And The JJ-Referral As A Self-Fulfilling Dynamic

* **Assign coordinator caseloads by need level, not by volume.** If every coordinator manages the same number of pairs regardless of complexity, experienced coordinators will informally gravitate toward the less demanding matches. A practical fix: designate one coordinator role as the high-needs specialist who handles all JJ-referred teens and any match flagged as high-complexity in the first 30 days. With 40 mentors and a $15K budget, this is one person's partial focus — not a separate hire.

* **Use mentor experience as a matching input, not just mentor availability.** First-year mentors should not be matched to the highest-need teens. A practical intake question for returning mentors: "What was the hardest moment in your last match, and how did you handle it?" Use that response to calibrate whether a mentor is ready for a more complex pairing. This costs nothing and generates useful supervisory data.

* **Create a mentor preparation session specifically on referral-source bias.** Before mentors receive any information about their matched teen, run a 90-minute session on self-fulfilling dynamics — using concrete scenarios, not abstract principles. The facilitator presents two identical teen profiles, one labeled "referred by school counselor" and one "referred by juvenile justice," and asks mentors to rate how challenging they expect the match to be. The gap in ratings makes the bias visible without requiring anyone to admit personal prejudice.

* **Track match quality metrics separately by referral source from month one.** If the program is not disaggregating 12-month retention, meeting frequency, and relationship quality scores by referral source, it will not detect the drift until it has already happened. This is a spreadsheet-level intervention: two additional columns in whatever tracking tool the program already uses. Set a threshold — if JJ-referred teens have a retention rate more than 15 percentage points below school-referred teens at 6 months, that triggers a program review, not just individual case management.

## Teens As The Safety Architects

* **Convene a paid youth advisory panel before finalizing any safety protocols.** A practical implementation: recruit five to seven teens who are not yet enrolled in the program (to avoid coercive dynamics), compensate them at $15–20/hour for three two-hour sessions, and give them a concrete task — review the draft boundary framework and identify what would make them feel safer or less safe. This is a $300–400 investment within the existing $15K budget and produces policies with teen legitimacy rather than teen compliance.

* **Use the panel's output as the primary document, not a supplement to adult-designed policies.** The operational difference between "we consulted youth" and "youth designed this" is which document is treated as authoritative. A practical approach: publish the youth panel's framework as the program safety document and have staff add legal/compliance annotations in a separate section. This inverts the usual hierarchy without removing necessary legal safeguards.

* **Start with location norms, not boundary lists, because teens have concrete experience with what feels unsafe.** Adults default to abstract rules ("no one-on-one meetings in private spaces"). Teens, especially those with trauma histories, have specific, contextual knowledge about which kinds of settings feel dangerous and which do not. A practical facilitation prompt: "Describe a time you felt safe with an adult who wasn't a parent. Where were you? What was happening?" The patterns that emerge across five to seven teens will generate more usable location guidance than any compliance checklist.

* **Build an escalation protocol that teens can actually use.** Most escalation protocols are designed for adult reporters, not teen initiators. A practical teen-accessible protocol has three elements: a specific person to contact (named, not just a role), a communication channel that does not require a phone call (most teens will text before they call), and a 24-hour response commitment. Pilot this with the advisory panel before finalizing it — ask them whether they would actually use it, and revise until the answer is yes.
