# Program Identity, Framing, and Hidden Assumptions - The Pragmatist

---

## The "At-Risk" Label as a Framing Decision

* **Reframe at intake, not after.** The simplest practical intervention is controlling the label from day one: the application packet and intake form should never use "at-risk" in any youth-facing document. Replace it with something like "program participant" internally and, in youth-facing materials, language tied to goal-setting — "teens working toward their next chapter." This is a one-time copy edit with zero budget cost; do it before the first cohort.

* **Build alternative framing into mentor training as a concrete script.** A mentor who shows up thinking "my job is to fix this kid" will telegraph that in week one. A practical approach is to write a short (one-page) mentor orientation script that gives mentors a direct answer when a teen asks "why am I in this program?" — something like "because someone who knows you thought you'd be good at this." Draft and test the script before training begins.

* **Create a staff language protocol with specific substitute phrases.** Don't rely on general sensitivity reminders. A practical approach is a one-page cheat sheet listing specific phrases to avoid ("at-risk," "troubled," "case") alongside approved substitutes. Distribute it in staff onboarding. This is implementable in a week and gives the org something concrete to enforce.

* **Track whether referral source correlates with mentor relationship quality.** The "at-risk" framing problem has a measurable downstream effect: if teens referred through juvenile justice show lower engagement or shorter match lengths, that's evidence the labeling pipeline is shaping outcomes. Build referral source as a data field in your tracking from day one so you can run this analysis after 12 months. Cost: 30 minutes of spreadsheet setup.

* **Let teens name their own goals in the first meeting using a structured tool.** The most operationally direct counter to deficit framing is giving teens an asset-mapping activity (what are you already good at? what do you want to learn?) in the first mentor session. Several free, validated tools exist from MENTOR and Search Institute. Download one, pilot it with your first five matches, and decide whether to standardize it. This costs nothing and generates qualitative data on what teens actually want from the program.

## Who the Formal Referral Pipeline Reaches and Misses

* **Map your current referral sources and their coverage gaps before launch.** A practical first step is a 30-minute conversation with two or three school counselors to ask: "Who in your school is struggling but you can't refer to a program like this?" That conversation will surface the teens who don't get flagged — chronic quiet withdrawal, kids who age out of IEP support, students who've given up rather than acted out. Do this before you finalize your intake process, not after.

* **Add two or three community referral points in the first year.** The formal pipeline — school counselors and juvenile justice — will fill your initial cohort but will systematically miss certain teens. A practical expansion is adding a barbershop owner, a faith-adjacent youth sports coach, or a corner store owner who knows families. Approach them personally, give them a one-page referral card, and ask them to pass it to a family they trust. This can run in parallel to formal channels at near-zero cost.

* **Set an enrollment cap that keeps you from being overwhelmed by one referral source.** If juvenile justice referrals fill all 40 spots, you've built a program shaped by that one pipeline. A practical safeguard is a soft cap: no more than 50% of participants from any single referral source. This forces diversification without requiring you to build new pipelines immediately.

* **Treat referral source as a program design feedback loop.** After year one, review which referral source produced the most durable matches and the most positive outcomes. If school counselors are 3x more likely to refer teens who complete the full year, that tells you something about information quality at intake. Build this analysis into your annual program review from the start so you're making data-driven adjustments, not guessing.

## What the 40 Volunteers Actually Want and What Teens Actually Need

* **Run a structured volunteer interest survey before the matching process.** Before you form a single match, give every prospective volunteer a short (10-question) intake survey that asks directly: What do you hope to get out of this? What kind of teen do you want to be matched with? What would make you quit? Honest answers surface misalignment before it wastes everyone's time. This takes one week to design and distribute.

* **Set minimum commitment expectations in writing at volunteer sign-up.** A practical filter is making the commitment explicit and non-negotiable upfront: one year, weekly meetings, two training sessions, one mandatory check-in per quarter with staff. Some volunteers will self-select out. That's the intended outcome. Build the commitment document before your first recruitment push, not during it.

* **Design the match to include a mutual fit conversation, not just staff assignment.** Rather than staff assigning pairs based on intake forms alone, build a brief structured "introduction meeting" where staff facilitate a 30-minute conversation between a prospective mentor and teen. Staff observe and make the final call, but both parties have a voice. This takes more coordinator time but substantially reduces early dropout. Pilot it with your first cohort before deciding whether to scale.

* **Identify volunteers who already have natural teen rapport before training.** Among your 40 interested adults, several likely already coach youth sports, run Sunday school, or have their own teenagers. A practical approach is asking the congregation's youth-facing leaders to nominate five volunteers who are especially good with young people. Those five become your first cohort and informal model for what good mentoring looks like. They cost nothing extra to identify.

* **Build a low-stakes exit path for mismatched volunteers.** Some mentors will discover the work isn't what they expected. A practical approach is a 90-day check-in where volunteers can honestly flag that a match isn't working — without shame and without abandoning the teen mid-relationship. Staff can facilitate a graceful transition rather than forcing a bad match to continue. Design this exit protocol before launch so it doesn't feel improvised when you need it.

## Faith Identity as Both Trust Asset and Filtering Effect

* **Write an explicit non-discrimination policy before you accept the first referral.** If the organization doesn't already have a written policy stating that program participation is open regardless of religious background, family structure, or identity, draft one now. One page, reviewed by your attorney, approved by leadership. This protects the org legally, signals inclusion to families, and gives staff a clear answer when hard questions arise at intake. It's a week of work.

* **Separate program operations from religious programming in all materials.** A practical approach is ensuring that every youth-facing document — welcome letter, program handbook, mentor agreement — makes clear that the mentorship program is distinct from the congregation's religious programming. Teens are not required to attend services or religious events. This distinction costs nothing to communicate and opens the program to families who would otherwise hesitate.

* **Pilot outreach in at least two non-congregational locations before launch.** To test whether the faith identity is narrowing your reach, distribute program flyers through one secular channel — the public library, a community center, a barbershop — before your first cohort. If you get zero inquiries from those channels, that's signal worth acting on. If you get a few, you've expanded your pool at minimal cost.

* **Train staff on a specific, consistent answer to "do I have to be religious?"** Staff need a clear, rehearsed answer that is both honest about the organizational context and genuinely welcoming. Draft this answer in your staff orientation materials. It should take two sentences: one acknowledging the faith context, one making clear the program itself has no religious participation requirements. Ambiguity at this question will cost you families.

* **Identify three families the organization has served across 20 years who have different religious backgrounds.** Those families are proof that the org can work across faith lines. A practical approach is asking the executive director to name them in the first staff meeting and document how those relationships were built. Those stories become your internal training material for how to navigate faith-identity questions with new families.

## Hidden Assets in the Community's 20-Year Relational Track Record

* **Start with an internal asset audit before any external program design.** A practical first step is a 90-minute facilitated conversation with three to five long-tenured staff or congregation leaders asking: who are the adults in this community who teens already trust? What programs have we run that teens actually showed up for? What did we do that worked and never wrote down? This costs one afternoon and will generate more useful design input than a formal needs assessment.

* **Identify your "natural connectors" among the 40 volunteers.** Among those 40, some already have informal relationships with teens in the neighborhood — they coached a team, ran a summer program, know the families. A practical approach is adding two questions to the volunteer intake form: "Do you currently have any informal mentoring relationships with teens in this neighborhood?" and "How did you meet them?" These answers identify your most valuable starting assets.

* **Use existing trust relationships to accelerate parental consent.** Families who are skeptical of institutions may be far more receptive to a program application hand-delivered by someone they already know from the congregation than one that arrives by mail. A practical approach is asking long-tenured congregation members to personally deliver and explain the consent packet to three or four families in their social network. This leverages existing trust at zero cost.

* **Document informal program knowledge before it walks out the door.** The org's 20-year track record is likely concentrated in a small number of people who will eventually leave. A practical approach is scheduling one-hour interviews with three to five long-tenured staff in the first month to capture what they know about this community's families — what the barriers are, what language resonates, what has failed. This institutional knowledge should be written down before the program launches, not after.

## Beyond Guidance to Co-Creation

* **Start small: build one co-designed element into the pilot rather than reimagining the whole structure.** Full co-creation is a significant operational lift. A practical approach is selecting one program component — the matching questionnaire, the first-session activity, or the goal-setting framework — and asking four or five teens from the target community to help design it before launch. This is achievable in two or three facilitated sessions and produces immediate, practical input without requiring a structural overhaul.

* **Add a "what I want to learn from you" field to the mentor intake form.** The simplest operational move toward mutual discovery is asking mentors at intake: "What's something you genuinely don't know how to do that a teenager might?" This small reframe signals that the relationship runs both directions and begins training mentors to show up with curiosity rather than just expertise.

* **Design the first meeting as an exchange of questions, not a mentor introduction.** A practical approach is structuring the first mentor-mentee meeting so both parties arrive with one question they can't answer alone. Staff write the prompt, facilitate the exchange, and document what comes up. This takes no additional budget and produces qualitative data about what teens actually want to explore — data that can sharpen matching criteria over time.

* **Treat teens' goal-setting input as binding, not advisory.** In many mentorship programs, teens fill out goal-setting forms that staff and mentors then largely ignore. A practical approach is committing that the teen's stated goals become the official goals of the match — documented, shared with the mentor, and reviewed at each quarterly check-in. This gives the co-creation frame operational weight rather than just symbolic weight.

## The Faith Org as a Deliberate Trusted Intermediary

* **Name the intermediary role explicitly in your program description.** The org likely doesn't currently describe itself as a trusted go-between for families and institutions. A practical approach is adding one paragraph to the program description that says exactly that: "We work with schools and juvenile justice to serve teens — but our relationship is with the family, not the institution. What you share with us stays with us unless mandatory reporting applies." This sets expectations and positions the org clearly.

* **Create a written data-sharing protocol before accepting the first juvenile justice referral.** The intermediary role creates legal exposure the org may not have mapped. A practical first step is a 60-minute conversation with an attorney to document: what information the org will and won't share with referral sources, under what circumstances, and what the teen and family consent to at intake. This protocol should be written and approved before the first referral arrives.

* **Build a family advocate role into the program structure.** A practical approach is designating one staff member (even part-time) as the formal contact point between families and institutional referral sources. That person's job is to translate, buffer, and advocate — not just coordinate. This role doesn't require a full-time hire; it could be a part-time position or a defined responsibility within an existing role. Define it before launch.

* **Train mentors explicitly on what information to keep confidential from referral institutions.** Mentors will sometimes learn things from teens that referral sources (schools, juvenile justice) would find relevant. A practical approach is a dedicated training module — 30 minutes, with case examples — on the distinction between mandatory reporting obligations (which are non-discretionary) and program confidentiality (which protects the relationship). This training should happen before any matches form.

* **Use your 20-year track record to build formal MOUs with referral institutions.** The org's standing in the community gives it leverage to negotiate written memoranda of understanding with schools and juvenile justice that define data-sharing boundaries, referral criteria, and each party's obligations. A practical approach is starting with one MOU — the school most likely to refer — and treating it as a template. This protects the org and formalizes the intermediary role in a way that lasts beyond any individual relationship.

## What If the Church's 20-Year Presence Is Doing Harm by Being Present

* **Test the hypothesis before building around it.** Before making structural changes based on the assumption that the building is a barrier, do a quick street-level test: ask five families who were approached but didn't enroll why they declined. If "it's the church" comes up repeatedly, you have evidence to act on. If it doesn't, you've avoided an unnecessary redesign. This test takes two weeks and costs nothing.

* **Identify two or three neutral community locations for mentoring meetings.** Even if the org doesn't go "functionally invisible," a practical approach is offering meeting locations that aren't the church building — the public library, a coffee shop, a community recreation center. Most MENTOR-certified programs already recommend public, third-party locations for safety reasons. Expanding the approved venue list addresses both the institutional-bias concern and the safety protocol in one move.

* **Develop a program brand that stands independent of the congregation's name.** A practical approach is giving the mentorship program its own name, its own logo, and its own one-page handout that doesn't feature the church's name prominently. The org remains the legal and financial sponsor, but the program faces outward with a neutral identity. This is a branding and design task; it takes two to three weeks and doesn't require any structural changes.

* **Partner with one secular community anchor to co-sponsor outreach.** The org becomes less visibly "the church program" if a secular co-sponsor — the neighborhood library, a community health clinic, a local YMCA — is listed on program materials. A practical approach is approaching one such partner before launch with a simple ask: allow us to use your name on our materials and host one info session in your space. This broadens perceived reach without requiring the org to diminish its own identity.
