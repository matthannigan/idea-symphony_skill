# Safety Protocols and the Relational Core - The Pragmatist

---

## Public vs. Private Meeting Locations and Safety Systems That Strengthen Rather Than Chill the Relationship

* **Start with a tiered location policy, not a blanket rule.** A practical approach is to define three tiers: always-public for the first three months, semi-private (a reserved room at the church with a window in the door) after three months with coordinator sign-off, and home settings only with explicit written approval and a safety plan. This gives mentors clear guardrails without permanently restricting relationship depth. Budget roughly two hours of staff time per month to review tier-change requests.

* **Frame check-in logs as a relationship tool, not a surveillance tool.** Brief session logs — five fields, two minutes to complete — can double as prompts that help mentors reflect on what went well. Reframe the language: call it a "connection note," not an "incident report." Mentors who understand why the log exists are far more likely to complete it accurately, and teens are less likely to perceive it as monitoring when mentors explain it naturally.

* **Pilot a "meeting choice" protocol for established pairs.** After six months, let teen and mentor jointly propose their preferred meeting format — coffee shop, library, park, church — and have the coordinator approve it in a 10-minute check-in call. This costs almost nothing and gives teens a concrete stake in the relationship's structure. Track which formats correlate with longer match durations; use that data to refine the default policy within 12 months.

* **Use background check scope to calibrate, not expand, restriction.** A practical approach is to set location policy based on actual risk indicators surfaced during screening, not worst-case assumptions. If a mentor clears enhanced screening with references, there is no operational reason to permanently confine meetings to public spaces. Tying location flexibility to demonstrated match quality — not just time elapsed — lets the program reward trust without abandoning oversight.

* **Build a simple escalation trigger into location reviews, not a separate system.** Rather than creating a new process, add a one-question flag to the existing monthly coordinator check-in: "Has anything about this match made you want to change the meeting format?" If yes, it routes to a 15-minute call. Costs nothing to implement and catches problems early without making every pair feel scrutinized.

## Faith-Based Context, Teen Authenticity, and Institutional-Trauma-Aware Adult Authority

* **Build trauma-informed authority into the mentor training curriculum from day one.** A practical approach is to dedicate a full two-hour block in pre-match training to how teens who have had negative encounters with institutional authority — courts, schools, police — may read even well-intentioned adults. Pair this with a 30-minute scenario exercise. This is not a soft add-on; it is a prerequisite for mentors to be effective and reduces the risk of early match ruptures that are expensive to recover from.

* **Create explicit opt-out language for faith content in the program covenant.** Draft a one-page document signed by mentors, teens, and guardians that states clearly: mentors will not proselytize, teens are not required to participate in religious activities, and the relationship is the program. This protects the organization legally, sets expectations for mentors who joined through the congregation, and signals to teens referred via juvenile justice that the program is not a conversion program. Costs a few hours of legal review upfront.

* **Give teens a low-barrier way to flag discomfort with a specific mentor.** A practical minimum is a texted check-in from the program coordinator after month one: "On a scale of 1 to 5, how comfortable do you feel with your mentor? Reply with any number." Numeric responses are less intimidating than open-ended questions. If a teen texts back 1 or 2, it triggers a follow-up call. This costs almost nothing and gives teens who distrust adults a face-saving way to signal problems without confrontation.

* **Acknowledge the faith context in orientation — once, plainly, and without pressure.** Rather than ignoring the organizational setting or over-explaining it, have the program coordinator say at the first group orientation: "This program is hosted by a faith community. Your mentor may have personal beliefs that differ from yours. That is fine. The relationship is about you." Simple, honest, and done. Teens who have experienced institutional dishonesty respond better to direct acknowledgment than to careful omission.

## What If Safety Protocols Are the Danger

* **Audit existing protocols for teen-facing visibility before adding more.** A practical first step is to inventory which protocols teens actually encounter versus which are invisible to them: the check-in log, the public-space requirement, the app-based communication channel. For each visible protocol, ask: does this communicate "we protect you" or "we monitor you"? Revise language and framing before adding new systems. This costs staff time, not money, and the findings will be more useful than any consultant's framework.

* **Reduce protocol density in the first 90 days, not after.** The highest-risk period for match failure is the first three months. A practical approach is to front-load relationship-building and back-load administrative requirements. Push the first formal check-in report to week six, not week two. Hold the first coordinator review call at month two, not month one. Teens are watching whether the program trusts the mentor; start by demonstrating that it does.

* **Replace the two-adult rule with a "reachable adult" standard where legally permissible.** For many one-on-one mentorship programs, a strict two-adult presence rule is not legally required — it is a risk-management default. A reachable adult standard — mentor logs meeting start and end, coordinator is reachable by phone, location is confirmed — achieves nearly the same protection with far less relational friction. Get a legal opinion on this specific point; it may free up significant flexibility at low cost.

* **Test protocol framing with a small cohort before scaling.** Before rolling out to all 40 mentor pairs, pilot the full protocol package with five pairs in month one and collect qualitative feedback from teens via informal check-ins. Ask specifically: "Did anything about how the program works feel weird or uncomfortable?" Use those responses to redesign the intake experience. A small cohort pilot is cheap; a program where teens disengage in month two because the protocols felt punitive is expensive.

## The First Meeting Moment

* **Design a structured-but-light first-meeting protocol that both parties prepare for.** A practical approach is to give mentors a one-page "first meeting guide" with three suggested conversation starters and one explicit instruction: do not ask about the referral source or legal history. Give teens a brief orientation that names what to expect: "You will meet in the church meeting room, you do not have to share anything you do not want to share, and you can tell us afterward if you want a different mentor." Costs one hour to write, reduces first-meeting anxiety on both sides.

* **Use a brief shared activity as the opening frame, not open-ended conversation.** For a teen like Marcus — referred through juvenile justice, likely practiced at reading adults for threat signals — sitting across a table and making eye contact for an hour is high-stakes and exhausting. A practical alternative is to open the first meeting with a low-stakes shared task: picking toppings for pizza from a paper menu, filling out a "favorites" card together, choosing a game from a shelf. Activity-based openings reduce face-to-face pressure and give both parties something to talk about without requiring disclosure.

* **Have the coordinator introduce the match in person, not just on paper.** A brief three-way meeting — coordinator, mentor, teen — at the start of the first session provides a human bridge between the institution and the new relationship. The coordinator can model how they talk to the teen (not at them), make a brief personal statement about why the program exists, and then physically leave the room. This transition moment signals to the teen that the program is stepping back, not watching. Adds 15 minutes to the first session; worth it.

* **Build a low-stakes exit option into the first meeting.** Inform both mentor and teen before they meet: after this session, you can tell the coordinator you would prefer a different match and no explanation is required. Knowing this option exists reduces the stakes of the first meeting and makes it easier for both parties to be present rather than performing. Practically, fewer than 10% of pairs will use it, but its existence changes the relational dynamic from the start.
