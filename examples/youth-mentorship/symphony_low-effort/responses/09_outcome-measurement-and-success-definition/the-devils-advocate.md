# Outcome Measurement and Success Definition - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Measurement That Doesn't Overburden Teens or Surveil Mentors

* **The consent illusion.** Have we considered that asking already-stressed teens to complete surveys — even brief ones — may feel less like voluntary participation and more like a condition of staying in the program? If teens believe honest negative responses could jeopardize their match, they will self-censor, making the data not merely thin but actively misleading. Mitigation: separate data collection from program participation structurally — make it clear that a teen's answers cannot affect their match status, and have someone other than their mentor administer any check-ins.

* **The mentor surveillance paradox.** One potential challenge might be that any system measuring mentor consistency — check-in logs, attendance records, contact frequency — signals distrust to volunteers who signed up based on mission alignment, not performance management. Volunteers who feel monitored may disengage or game metrics rather than doing the harder relational work. Mitigation: reframe tracking as a coordinator support tool ("how can we help you?") rather than an audit, and share aggregate findings with mentors rather than individual scorecards.

* **Minimum viable data becomes minimum viable program.** The instinct to minimize survey burden risks producing a measurement set so thin it cannot detect program failure — only program existence. If the only "outcome" tracked is whether meetings occurred, a program where mentors and teens sit in silence for an hour satisfies the metric. Mitigation: include at least one qualitative signal per quarter — a brief open-ended check-in with either the teen or the mentor — that can surface qualitative failure modes that quantitative counts will miss.

* **Who owns the data is not a detail.** Have we considered that teens referred through juvenile justice are accustomed to information about them flowing upward to people who have power over their lives? If data collection procedures don't explicitly address where information goes and who cannot access it (probation officers, school administrators), teens may assume the worst and answer accordingly. Mitigation: produce a one-page plain-language data promise — written at a middle-school reading level — that specifies who sees what and what cannot happen as a result of their answers.

## Funder Metrics vs Teen-Defined Success and the Timescale Mismatch

* **The reporting cycle shapes the program whether you want it to or not.** One potential challenge might be that even with good intentions, staff will unconsciously prioritize activities that generate legible 12-month outputs — attendance gains, enrollment persistence — over harder-to-document relational work. Grant renewals depend on the former; teens' long-term outcomes depend on the latter. Mitigation: establish an explicit internal "shadow metrics" report reviewed quarterly by staff only, tracking qualitative relationship depth indicators that never appear in funder reports, to keep staff anchored to the work that actually matters.

* **Youth-defined success metrics are harder to collect than they sound.** Have we considered that asking teens to define what success means to them is a meaningful intervention that takes time and trust to do well? A teen who has been failed repeatedly by institutions may not have a vocabulary for success, or may give back whatever answer they think adults want. Collecting authentic youth-defined outcomes is not a data collection problem — it is a relationship depth problem. Mitigation: defer youth-defined success elicitation until at least month three, when enough trust may exist for honest answers.

* **Foundation relationships have their own politics.** The regional foundation funding this program has its own board, its own theory of change, and its own pressure to show impact to its donors. Treating funder metrics as a fixed external constraint may underestimate how much room exists to negotiate — or it may overestimate it. Mitigation: have an early honest conversation with the program officer, not just the RFP, about what they actually need to defend the grant internally. The stated metrics and the real threshold for renewal may not be identical.

* **Defining success through teen voice can entrench present-tense thinking.** One potential challenge might be that teens who have grown up in constrained environments may define success narrowly — staying out of trouble, not failing school — rather than ambitiously. Centering teen-defined success without expanding their sense of what's possible could inadvertently set a low bar that the program then meets without actually transforming anything. Mitigation: pair youth-defined success elicitation with structured horizon-expanding conversations about futures teens haven't yet imagined for themselves.

## Leading vs Lagging Indicators and a Baseline Data Window

* **Baseline data collection at match initiation is a legal and ethical minefield, not just a logistics problem.** Have we considered that pulling school attendance and grade records at match initiation requires signed release forms, may intersect with FERPA, and will face variable cooperation from schools depending on existing relationships? Assuming this data is cleanly available will delay program launch. Mitigation: begin the legal and data-sharing agreement process with partner schools six months before program launch, not six weeks, and design a fallback baseline methodology that relies only on self-report if formal records access falls through.

* **Leading indicators borrowed from other programs may not transfer.** Mentor check-in consistency as a leading indicator assumes the program has enough structure and accountability to actually drive consistency — which a volunteer-run, faith-community program with $15K in seed funding may not. Established mentorship evaluation frameworks were often developed in more resourced contexts with paid staff. Mitigation: treat borrowed leading indicators as hypotheses to validate rather than facts to report; include a year-one component specifically designed to test whether these indicators actually predict anything in this particular community context.

* **School counselor data relationships can evaporate.** One potential challenge might be that the school counselors who are the primary referral source and potential data-sharing partners are not a stable institutional commitment — they are individuals who will change jobs, change schools, and have their own caseload pressures. Building a measurement system dependent on their voluntary cooperation creates a single-point-of-failure. Mitigation: formalize data-sharing through district-level agreements, not individual counselor relationships, so that personnel turnover doesn't destroy the measurement infrastructure.

## Healthy Graduation vs Premature Exit and the Unmeasurable Core

* **The distinction between healthy graduation and premature failure is impossible to make in real time.** Have we considered that the program coordinator making this call — probably a part-time staff member managing dozens of matches — will not have the information, the time, or the clinical training to reliably distinguish a teen who has genuinely grown past the program from a teen who has given up on it? Systematically miscoding premature exits as healthy graduations protects program metrics while providing no information about what actually happened. Mitigation: require a structured exit interview — with the teen, not just the mentor — within two weeks of any match end before 12 months, administered by someone other than the mentor.

* **The foundation may not accept relationship quality as evidence.** One potential challenge might be that building a case for the foundation that "the program is working even when quantitative indicators don't show it yet" requires a level of funder sophistication about youth development theory that many foundations lack or won't accept in a renewal application. Relying on this argument could result in the program losing funding during precisely the years when the most important developmental work is happening. Mitigation: do not wait for a renewal crisis to educate the funder on developmental timelines — build this into the first funder briefing, cite specific research, and get the program officer's buy-in before, not after, the first reporting cycle.

* **Defining a "healthy graduation" creates an incentive to use it.** Have we considered that once a category of "successful early exit" exists in the program's taxonomy, staff and mentors may apply it liberally to matches that ended for less flattering reasons — volunteer dropout, teen disengagement, poor matching — in order to avoid the stigma of a "failed match"? Taxonomy shapes data. Mitigation: require that "healthy graduation" status be approved by a supervisor, not self-reported by the mentor, and define specific observable criteria (teen articulates specific future goals, mentor provides narrative summary of growth areas) that must be documented before the designation applies.

## What If Chronic Absenteeism Is the Correct Response to the Environment

* **The program's implicit theory of change assumes school deserves more of these teens' time and attention.** Have we considered that designing a mentorship program whose measurable outcomes include improved school attendance accepts, without examination, that the schools these teens attend are worth attending? A faith-based organization with a 20-year neighborhood presence is likely to have views on this that its funding source does not share — and those views may be accurate. Mitigation: before finalizing the outcome framework, convene a listening session with teens and families specifically about what school does and doesn't do for them, and let those answers genuinely constrain which school-related outcomes the program commits to improving.

* **Mentor matching based on school success orientation may filter out the most relevant adults.** One potential challenge might be that if the program emphasizes academic improvement outcomes, it will implicitly favor mentors who are comfortable in academic institutions — college-educated, professionally employed — over mentors whose own paths diverged from conventional education and who might be more credible and relevant to teens navigating institutional distrust. The mentor pool from the congregation likely includes both types. Mitigation: explicitly include in mentor recruitment messaging that non-traditional educational and career paths are assets, not disqualifiers, particularly for teens whose relationship with school is adversarial.

* **Reframing absenteeism as rational adaptation rather than a problem could be correct and still be unhelpful.** Have we considered that even if chronic absenteeism is a reasonable response to genuinely poor schools, teens still bear the consequences of not having a diploma in an economy that treats credential absence harshly and unjustly? A mentorship program cannot fix structural educational failure. The question is whether to help teens navigate an unjust system strategically or to refuse complicity — and that is an organizational values question, not an outcome measurement question. Mitigation: name this tension explicitly in the program's theory of change document rather than hiding it inside attendance improvement metrics; the organization's credibility with teens depends on its honesty about what it can and cannot change.

* **Optimizing away from school attendance metrics exposes the program to funder misalignment risk.** The regional foundation funding this program almost certainly views school attendance as a core success indicator for at-risk youth programming. Publicly reframing absenteeism as potentially rational could jeopardize both this grant and future funding relationships, regardless of the intellectual merits. Mitigation: maintain the distinction between the program's internal theory of change — which can be more honest about institutional failure — and its external communications to funders, while ensuring this distinction doesn't drift into dishonesty about what the program actually does and why.
