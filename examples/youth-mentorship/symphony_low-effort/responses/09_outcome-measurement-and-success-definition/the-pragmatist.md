# Outcome Measurement and Success Definition - The Pragmatist

---

## Measurement That Doesn't Overburden Teens or Surveil Mentors

* **Keep the data set to three core items.** A practical minimum: mentor meeting logs (date, duration, location — filled by mentors, not teens), a brief quarterly check-in with the teen (three questions, five minutes, verbal if preferred), and school-supplied attendance data pulled through existing counselor relationships. That's it for Year 1. Adding instruments before you know what you can actually collect reliably is a way to drown staff and still get bad data.

* **Frame data collection as a feedback channel, not a report card.** To make this feasible and honest with teens, the quarterly check-in should be explicitly framed: "We use your answers to improve the program, not to share with your school or probation officer." Put that in writing in the intake packet. Teens who trust the frame give real answers; teens who suspect surveillance give safe answers that tell you nothing useful.

* **Pre-populate from existing sources before asking anyone new questions.** School counselors already track attendance and flag concerns — a data-sharing agreement (typically a simple MOU) lets you pull that without burdening teens. The marginal cost to the organization is low, the data quality is high, and you've avoided duplicating a bureaucratic load onto a population already navigating multiple institutions.

* **Assign data entry to mentors, not teens.** A short monthly log (10 minutes max, mobile-friendly form) that mentors self-complete reduces friction for the most vulnerable party and creates a regular touchpoint that doubles as mentor accountability. Use a tool the volunteers already have — Google Forms costs nothing and the staff can see submissions in real time.

* **Build a privacy disclosure into intake, not as boilerplate.** A practical approach: one page, plain language, that explains what is collected, who sees it (program staff only, aggregated for funder reports), and what it cannot be used for (shared with juvenile justice, shared with schools without separate consent). Teens and guardians sign it at enrollment. This satisfies funders and builds the trust that makes the data meaningful.

## Funder Metrics vs Teen-Defined Success and the Timescale Mismatch

* **Report funder metrics honestly and separately from program quality metrics.** A practical approach: maintain two parallel measurement tracks. Track A is funder-legible (attendance, suspension rate, grades, match retention) — report these faithfully. Track B is internal and qualitative (teen-reported goals at match start, mentor narrative notes, self-defined milestones). Be transparent with the funder that Track B exists and matters, even if you only share Track A in formal reports. Most foundation officers are not naive about what grades measure; showing you know the difference builds credibility.

* **Capture teen-defined success at match initiation, not retrospectively.** At the first or second meeting, have the mentor and teen complete a simple "what does a good year look like to you?" sheet — three to five goals in the teen's own words. This takes 20 minutes, costs nothing, and gives you a genuine baseline for whether the program helped the teen achieve what they wanted, independent of what the funder wants to see. Review it at six months.

* **Negotiate the timescale mismatch with funders up front.** When reporting to the regional foundation, include one paragraph in every progress report that names the lag explicitly: "Youth development outcomes at the relationship level typically manifest over 3–5 years; we are 12 months in and reporting leading indicators as agreed." This positions the program as rigorous rather than evasive, and gives you a defensible record if Year 1 lagging indicators are flat.

* **Design the match commitment length to generate at least one data cycle.** A one-year minimum match is already in the program model — this is good. To make this feasible as a measurement asset, conduct a structured 12-month check-in comparing baseline intake data to current status on the four or five agreed metrics. That single comparison point is the deliverable for the funder, even if the full story takes longer to emerge.

## Leading vs. Lagging Indicators and a Baseline Data Window

* **Capture four baseline data points at match initiation.** A practical, low-burden set: (1) prior-semester attendance rate from school counselor, (2) current GPA or credit accumulation status, (3) a brief self-efficacy item — "on a scale of 1–5, how much do you feel like adults in your life are in your corner?" — and (4) whether the teen has any current justice-system obligations. These four items are collectable in under 30 minutes and give you a meaningful before/after comparison at 12 months without administering a clinical instrument.

* **Use meeting consistency as your primary leading indicator.** Research on mentorship effectiveness consistently shows that match duration and meeting regularity are the strongest predictors of positive outcomes — not program content, not mentor expertise. A practical early-warning metric: any match with fewer than two meetings in a month triggers a check-in call from staff. This is actionable, observable, and costs only coordinator time.

* **Leverage existing school data channels rather than building new ones.** A data-sharing MOU with participating schools lets you pull attendance and grade data directly — no additional assessment burden on teens. Most school counselors who are already referring students are motivated partners here. A practical first step: draft a one-page MOU template, have your legal advisor review it once, and use it for all partner schools. This is a two-week task, not a six-month infrastructure project.

* **Establish a 90-day early-indicator review.** At 90 days into each match, have the coordinator review: meeting frequency, mentor log quality, and whether the teen's self-efficacy baseline has shifted (one follow-up question). This is not a formal assessment — it's a five-minute staff review per match. Matches flagged as at-risk at 90 days can be supported before they dissolve; matches that look healthy at 90 days have strong odds of reaching 12 months.

## Healthy Graduation vs. Premature Exit and the Unmeasurable Core

* **Establish a simple exit classification at match close.** A practical approach: when any match ends, the coordinator documents the reason using a four-category taxonomy — (1) natural graduation (teen and mentor mutually agreed the relationship has run its course positively), (2) life transition (relocation, aging out, schedule incompatibility with no relationship failure), (3) program-initiated close (safety concern or policy violation), (4) premature exit (match dissolved before goals were met, for unclear or negative reasons). This costs five minutes per exit and gives you defensible data over time on what proportion of exits are healthy.

* **Define healthy graduation criteria before the program launches, not after.** To make this feasible, draft a one-page document that specifies what a healthy graduation looks like: minimum months matched, documented completion of at least one teen-defined goal, mutual agreement by mentor and teen, and a brief exit interview. Give mentors this document at training. When a match ends at nine months with the teen saying "I feel ready to move on," staff have a standard against which to assess whether that's graduation or early exit.

* **Use mentor narrative logs to hold the unmeasurable core.** Quantitative metrics will not capture whether a relationship was meaningful. A practical substitute: require mentors to write a brief exit narrative (one page, unstructured) at match close describing what changed, what didn't, and what they observed in the teen over the match period. These narratives are not reportable to funders, but they accumulate into an institutional record of program quality that can inform design revisions, support accreditation applications, and anchor annual program reviews in something real.

* **Build a 30-day post-exit check-in into the standard protocol.** One month after a match closes, have the coordinator make a brief call to the teen (or guardian for younger teens) — three questions, five minutes. This captures whether a "healthy graduation" actually held up, surfaces needs that emerged after the match ended, and signals to the teen that the organization's care didn't stop when the formal match did. The cost is low; the signal value is high.

## What If Chronic Absenteeism Is the Correct Response to the Environment

* **Don't embed school-attendance improvement as a program goal; embed teen-reported wellbeing instead.** A practical approach: if attendance is a funder metric you must track, track it — but do not make increasing attendance a program intervention goal. Let mentors respond to what teens actually need. A teen who is avoiding a school where they are being bullied does not need a mentor who helps them attend more; they need an adult who sees the situation clearly. Program design should leave mentors free to advocate for the teen's actual interests, including helping them navigate alternatives.

* **Use mentor training to address the absenteeism question directly.** Most volunteer mentors will arrive with a school-attendance-is-good assumption. A practical approach: include a 20-minute module in the initial training that presents the context — 35% chronic absenteeism in this community, the range of reasons teens miss school, the difference between school avoidance and school rejection — and asks mentors to sit with the question "what would I actually want for this teen?" before defaulting to conventional success metrics. This costs nothing beyond curriculum design time.

* **Separate program-level funder metrics from individual-match success criteria.** At the program level, you may report attendance trends because funders require it. At the match level, the mentor and teen should be setting goals based on what the teen wants. A practical structure: intake documents define individual match goals (set by the teen), while program-level outcomes are reported separately and aggregated. This prevents a mismatch where a mentor feels pressure to push school attendance when the teen's self-defined goal is something entirely different.

* **Treat the absenteeism rate as a community asset for program design.** The 35% figure tells you something real about this environment. A practical implication: program design should not assume that conventional school-engagement is the right destination for every teen. Mentors paired with highly avoidant teens should have explicit permission — in writing, in their training — to support the teen in exploring alternatives (vocational programs, GED pathways, community college early enrollment) rather than reinforcing the message that the current school environment is where they must succeed. This is a low-cost design decision with significant implications for program integrity.
