# Summary: Program Identity, Framing, and Hidden Assumptions

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension across this cluster is that the faith organization's 20-year neighborhood presence operates as both the program's core asset and its primary filter. It is trusted by families who already trust it, and invisible or actively off-putting to families who don't. A feasibility-oriented view holds that this existing trust infrastructure is precisely what makes the program buildable on a $15K seed budget. It supplies volunteer pipeline, meeting space, and institutional relationships with schools and juvenile justice that a startup program couldn't otherwise establish. A risk-oriented counterpoint is that the program's current design (mediated entirely through the church building, the congregational volunteer pool, and faith-affiliated administrative staff) treats that institutional trust as universal when it is demonstrably selective. Both lenses point at the same element from opposite directions. The 20-year presence is simultaneously named as the solution vector (leverage what already works) and the risk source (the institution's identity quietly shapes who enrolls). This inverted framing represents the highest-stakes design question in this cluster.

Beyond the institutional identity question, both lenses converged on deficit framing as a high-confidence problem requiring structural intervention, not just cultural awareness. The "at-risk" label travels into the match before trust is built through intake forms, referral documentation, and mentor orientation. It shapes how mentors perceive their mentees from the first meeting. Both perspectives recommended making teens' own goals the operative frame at intake, stripping referral source from mentor-facing materials, and establishing a staff language protocol with specific phrase substitutions. The lenses diverged on formal referral pipeline design. A feasibility-oriented view argues for soft caps on any single referral source and community-level outreach cards as a low-cost expansion. A risk-oriented counterpoint presses harder on whether the entire pipeline is pre-filtering toward teens whose families are already somewhat institutionally engaged, and whether the program is reaching highest-need youth or simply the most accessible subset.

The volunteer motivation question produced a similar convergence-then-divergence structure. Both perspectives agreed that the 40 interested adults represent a waiting room, not a vetted pool, and that misaligned motivations are most dangerous precisely when a match is most at risk. A feasibility-oriented frame focuses on practical screening tools: a short intake survey, a mutual fit conversation, a graceful 90-day exit protocol. A risk-oriented lens pushes a harder point. The weekly-meeting-for-a-year commitment structure may be self-selecting for a volunteer demographic that is helpful and well-intentioned but structurally distant from the teens' own communities, limiting relational resonance where it matters most.

## Key Themes

### Deficit Framing Requires Structural, Not Just Cultural, Intervention

Both lenses converged that the "at-risk" label is an operational problem, not merely a language sensitivity issue. Intake forms, mentor orientation scripts, and referral documentation all transmit the deficit frame into the relationship before trust is built. The feasibility-oriented view identified specific, zero-cost fixes: a staff language cheat sheet, a teen asset-mapping activity in session one, a structured mentor script for "why am I in this program?" The risk-oriented view flagged that even a well-launched vocabulary will drift back within 12 months. A designated language-audit mechanism and supervision practices that surface tacit staff categorizations are needed to prevent this drift.

### The Referral Pipeline Is Pre-Filtering, and Speed Pressure Will Make It Worse

Both lenses flagged that school counselors and juvenile justice systems identify teens after something has already gone wrong, meaning the most isolated, quietly struggling teens may never surface in either channel. The risk-oriented view pushed hardest on this point. The $15K seed budget creates utilization pressure that will favor easy, paperwork-complete referrals over slower informal outreach. The program should set a deliberate cap (no more than 70% from formal channels) before launch, not after. The feasibility-oriented view offered the operational path forward: 30-minute conversations with school counselors to map coverage gaps, low-cost referral cards to two or three community anchors, and referral source tracked as a data field from day one. This enables a year-one analysis of pipeline effectiveness.

### The Faith Identity Is a Trust Asset With an Unacknowledged Admission Filter

The faith organization's presence opens doors with families who already trust it and quietly closes them with families who don't. This includes secular households, different-faith families, or families for whom the building itself carries social risk in a dense urban neighborhood. Both lenses agreed that a written non-discrimination policy, separated program branding, and at least one off-site meeting option should be in place before the first referral is accepted. The risk-oriented view went further. The program needs a written policy on values-tension scenarios (a teen's identity or family structure in tension with the host institution's values) before the first match is formed. Those cases will arrive unpredictably, and ad hoc responses will damage trust faster than any other failure mode.

### Volunteer Motivations Are Most Dangerous When the Match Is Most At Risk

Both lenses converged that faith-based mentorship contexts draw volunteers motivated by visible transformation, a misalignment that becomes harmful precisely when a teen is in crisis, disengaged, or simply unavailable. A feasibility-oriented frame pointed to practical mitigation: a structured intake survey, an explicit written commitment document, and a 90-day check-in with a non-stigmatizing exit path. The risk-oriented lens added that the program should pressure-test the full 40-volunteer pool with a specific scenario: "your mentee has missed three meetings and isn't responding; what do you do?" That answer reveals whether a volunteer's motivation will cause them to disengage or stay. The program needs to know this before matches are formed.

### The Organization's 20-Year Track Record Is Fragile and Partially Unmined

Both lenses converged that the relational equity built over 20 years is concentrated in a small number of long-tenured staff and congregants, not institutionalized in the program design. The feasibility-oriented view identified a concrete first step: a 90-minute facilitated conversation with three to five long-tenured leaders to surface informal mentors already operating in the community, successful prior relationships, and prior failures worth designing against. The risk-oriented view emphasized that the people with genuine relational gifts may not be among the 40 who raised their hand for a formal program. People who mentor through relationship tend to find formal programs bureaucratic. A training curriculum built on generic best practices rather than what proven informal mentors already do risks replacing working instinct with institutional bureaucracy.

# Summary: Volunteer Screening, Background Checks, and Disqualifying Criteria

## Executive Summary

The core tension in designing this program's screening framework is not whether to do background checks (both lenses agree on that) but whether a contextual review process for borderline cases is an asset or a liability. A feasibility-oriented view argues that a two-tier policy (categorical disqualifiers plus a documented contextual review panel) expands the volunteer pipeline, borrows legally tested precedent from foster care and BBBS, and provides the paper trail that protects the organization if harm occurs. A risk-oriented counterpoint flags that every contextual approval creates a discoverable written record. If a borderline-approved volunteer later causes harm, plaintiffs' counsel will subpoena the policy and ask why someone with that history was cleared. A nuanced, well-intentioned criteria document may invite more scrutiny than a conservative categorical rule, unless it is drafted with legal counsel reviewing the specific language before board adoption.

Both lenses converge on two high-confidence points: funnel attrition is severe and predictable, and the raw 40-volunteer headcount should not drive capacity commitments. A realistic planning assumption of 10–12 active matches at launch is far more defensible than building infrastructure around a pool of 40. Both lenses also agree that simplicity in screening architecture is a feature at this scale. The gig-platform tiered model offers a useful conceptual frame (calibrate screening depth to actual supervision level) but its operational machinery is over-engineered for a faith org with in-kind administrative staff.

The most important unique contribution from the risk lens is the insurance gap. Many volunteer liability policies explicitly exclude harm caused by volunteers approved over standard criteria. This is not a theoretical risk but a potentially catastrophic one. Confirming coverage for borderline approvals with the insurer in writing must precede any contextual review policy going live. The most important unique contribution from the feasibility lens is practical pipeline management: interview before background check, a low-friction digital application that captures interest immediately, and a simple stage-tracking spreadsheet that turns the first cohort into institutional learning for the second.

## Key Themes

### Funnel Reality vs. Headcount Assumptions (Convergent)

Both lenses independently arrive at the same conclusion: the 40-volunteer headcount is not a stable planning baseline. Interest expressed at a congregational announcement is social commitment, not considered commitment. A realistic funnel (40 expressions → 25 applications → 20 cleared checks → 15 training completions → 10–12 active matches) should anchor all funder commitments and staffing decisions from day one. Overbuilding infrastructure around 40 and underdelivering 12 demoralizes staff and strains grant relationships.

### Calibrate Screening Depth to Actual Access Level (Convergent)

Both lenses draw the same lesson from adjacent domains: the relevant variable is supervision structure, not program type. Foster care's intensive screening is calibrated to 24/7 unsupervised custodial access and does not translate directly to weekly mentors who meet in supervised spaces. Gig platforms offer the right conceptual frame (higher bar for unsupervised access, lower bar for supervised settings) without the operational complexity. This distinction allows the program to preserve pipeline for group-activity mentors while applying stricter screening to community-location, one-on-one pairs. [recurring]

### The Contextual Review Double-Edged Sword (Divergent)

A feasibility-oriented case for a documented contextual review panel (program director plus board designee, written checklist, retained rationale) is that it creates accountability, a defensible precedent, and a learning record while avoiding the pipeline loss of categorical over-screening. A risk-oriented counterpoint notes that a written policy articulating nuanced criteria is a discoverable document. Every contextual approval is a potential exhibit in a wrongful supervision lawsuit, and the cure is legal counsel reviewing the policy language before adoption, not after an incident. The panel is workable only if insurance coverage for borderline approvals is confirmed in writing first and the reviewers have clearly documented indemnification.

### Screening Sequence Matters (Unique: Risk-Oriented)

Placing the background check before the interview (because it is administratively easier to schedule) creates a false sense of security. The interview frequently surfaces disqualifying information that background checks miss: undisclosed relationships with youth in the target population, concerning personal motivations, or boundary-setting deficits. Running a phone screen first, then advancing only interview-cleared candidates to background checks, saves money, reduces the pool of conditional-approval anxiety, and correctly positions the check as confirmatory rather than gatekeeping.

### Administrative Capacity as a Hidden Constraint (Convergent)

Neither adjacent-domain model (gig platforms or BBBS) was designed for an organization whose administrative staff is shared in-kind with a church running other programs simultaneously. A processing spike (all 40 applicants in the same month) creates bottlenecks that leave enthusiastic volunteers in awkward holding patterns. Both lenses recommend resolving this before launch: either designate a dedicated screening coordinator or formalize a capacity agreement with the church administrative staff that specifies turnaround times and overflow protocols.

# Summary: Mandatory Reporting, Escalation, and Concern-Raising

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension in this cluster is not whether mentors should be mandatory reporters. Both lenses agree they should, and that direct CPS contact (not internal routing first) is the legally and ethically correct default. The tension is about what happens in the space between "something feels off" and "I'm calling CPS now." A feasibility-oriented view argues for making that space explicit and navigable: a two-track escalation system, a consultation line, transparent intake language with teens. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues that any process requiring mentors to self-classify before escalating recreates the hesitation the law was designed to remove. The real danger, in this view, is a program culture of strategic incuriosity, where mentors stop asking questions because asking obligates them to act.

Both lenses converge on three high-confidence foundations: obtain a written legal opinion before training begins (not after), build training around scenario practice rather than compliance slides, and tell teens directly at intake that their mentor is a mandatory reporter and will inform them before filing where possible. These three points are load-bearing regardless of how the rest of the system is designed.

The sharpest disagreement concerns documentation. A feasibility-oriented view recommends layered documentation (signed training forms, incident logs) as the practical due-diligence package for a $15K budget. A risk-oriented counterpoint flags that thorough training records create a paper trail proving the organization knew the obligation, making a subsequent failure-to-report harder to defend as innocent ignorance. The resolution is scenario-based assessments that demonstrate comprehension (not just attendance), which satisfies both the documentation need and the evidence-of-understanding standard. A second disagreement concerns training cadence. The feasibility-oriented case for a minimum viable launch (single two-hour session) is credible for month one, but the risk-oriented point that knowledge decays significantly within six months argues for quarterly scenario refreshers embedded in existing mentor meetings rather than as a separate compliance event.

## Key Themes

### Direct CPS Contact Is the Non-Negotiable Default
Both lenses agree: the program must train mentors to contact the state CPS hotline directly, with internal supervisor notification happening in parallel, not as a prerequisite. An internal-first chain (however well-intentioned for coordination) risks introducing delay that harms the child and may violate the reporting window. Printing the hotline number on the mentor ID card eliminates friction at the moment of decision. [convergent]

### Scenario-Based Training Over Compliance Theater
Neither passive compliance videos nor sign-off sheets produce mentors who can recognize and act on grey-zone disclosures. Both lenses support training built around realistic vignettes drawn from this population (a teen mentioning something heard about a friend, a mentor sensing something is wrong without a concrete disclosure). A feasibility-oriented addition: a 60-day reinforcement session after mentors have real experience lands differently than onboarding-only training. A risk-oriented addition: include at least one scenario presenting a technically-policy-compliant but emotionally harmful relationship, so mentors learn protective judgment rather than just rule-following. [convergent with unique contributions]

### The Escalation Design Must Not Require Threshold Judgment from Mentors
A feasibility-oriented case for two explicit tracks (mandatory safety report vs. concern conversation) prevents mentors from treating every worry as a bureaucratic burden or a hair-trigger alarm. A risk-oriented counterpoint: requiring mentors to classify which track they are on before escalating places the threshold judgment on the person least equipped to make it. The better design inverts the default. Any disclosure touching potential harm goes immediately to the safeguarding lead, who holds the classification responsibility. The two tracks can coexist if it is clear that *the lead*, not the mentor, does the sorting. [trade-off]

### Faith Community Social Dynamics Create Specific Reporting Vulnerabilities
A mentor who attends the same congregation as the program director, or whose family are major donors, may face implicit discouragement from raising concerns about high-status members. Standard mandatory reporter training does not address this. An anonymous reporting mechanism (a dedicated phone line or third-party intake platform) is a structural remedy that does not depend on individual courage. [unique: risk-oriented]

### Matching Is a First-Line Safeguarding Tool
Grey-zone situations are less likely when matches are well-made. Pairing emotionally steady mentors with teens who have experienced significant relational trauma reduces the probability of the enmeshed-but-technically-compliant dynamic that no reporting framework can catch. A structured intake interview (not a complex instrument) achieves this within the existing volunteer pool. [unique: feasibility-oriented]

# Summary: Liability, Insurance, and Organizational Protections

## Executive Summary

The central tension in this cluster is not whether the program needs sound insurance and documentation (it does, and both lenses agree on the essentials) but whether the legal and insurance structures get built to serve the program or end up quietly running it. A feasibility-oriented view holds that the right sequence is straightforward: get a broker conversation, add a SAM rider, document volunteer scope, and build an annual review cadence. A risk-oriented counterpoint holds that even organizations following this sequence often discover they've handed program design authority to legal counsel and underwriters by default, with restrictions accumulating silently year over year until the program is technically intact but relationally hollow.

Both lenses converge on three high-confidence points: (1) the SAM rider is non-negotiable and must be confirmed before the first match activates, not assumed; (2) the Volunteer Protection Act shield is only as strong as the role-scope documentation underneath it, and that documentation must be training-integrated rather than a signature stack at orientation; (3) the organizational shield actually inverts when escalation paths are unclear. If staff hesitate to log concerns for fear of admitting organizational knowledge, youth are less protected, not more.

Where the lenses diverge most sharply is on governance design for activity approval. The feasibility view argues for a tiered activity framework (low/medium/high risk with proportional documentation requirements) as the practical mechanism for keeping meaningful activities available. The risk-oriented counterpoint flags that any tiered framework still requires program leadership to hold formal authority over legal counsel's recommendations, not merely receive them. Without that structural role, the tiered framework becomes another venue where legal caution wins by default. Both lenses identify legal review as a potential constraint on relationship-building, but one treats the solution as better process design, and the other as an explicit power structure that gives program staff standing to push back.

---

## Key Themes

### The SAM Rider Gap Is a Known Unknown
Both lenses converged here with unusual force. Standard CGL policies routinely exclude sexual abuse and molestation claims by endorsement, faith-based organizations frequently assume coverage exists without reading the exclusions schedule, and a one-on-one mentorship program is precisely the exposure pattern underwriters had in mind when writing those exclusions. The feasibility-oriented path is concrete: budget $500–$1,500 for a standalone SAM rider or confirm inclusion in a nonprofit umbrella product before the first match activates. The risk-oriented addition is that you should require the broker to submit a full program description (including the one-on-one structure and off-site activity pattern) and get written confirmation the rating reflects actual program design, not a classroom model.

### Documentation as Litigation-Ready Training Record, Not HR Paperwork
Both lenses treat volunteer role documentation and training records as the operational foundation of the VPA shield, but differ on how to build them. The feasibility-oriented view emphasizes low-cost infrastructure: a one-page role description, a timestamped Google Form for training attestation, and a one-page incident log template (all buildable in an afternoon, all audit-ready). The risk-oriented addition is that documentation comprehension matters as much as documentation existence. Volunteers who sign twelve pages they haven't meaningfully read provide weak legal cover. Scenario-based orientation exercises that require volunteers to demonstrate understanding produce both stronger legal records and lower risk of inadvertent boundary violations.

### Legal Review Can Narrow the Program It Was Meant to Protect
Both lenses identified the second-order risk: liability-conservative legal review applied without a counterweight restricts the activities (shared outings, community work, recreational engagement) that build genuine trust with at-risk youth, particularly those referred through juvenile justice who have fraught relationships with institutional settings. The feasibility-oriented mitigation is a pre-legal working session to define the "must-protect" activity list, shifting legal review's frame from "what is risky?" to "how do we safely enable these specific activities?" The risk-oriented counterpoint is structural: legal review should produce recommendations, not decisions. Program leadership's authority to evaluate trade-offs and document them must be built into governance explicitly, not assumed.

### Insurance Procurement Should Follow Program Design, Not Precede It
A unique risk-oriented contribution: organizations that let insurance availability define program scope are letting the insurer make program decisions. The correct sequence is to define the relational model that produces outcomes first, identify the coverage required to support it, and treat the premium delta as a required program cost. Coverage gaps between a conference-room tutoring model and a shared-outings mentorship model are addressable, but only if the insurance conversation starts with program ambition, not with what the cheapest available policy covers.

### Annual Review Cadence Prevents Invisible Drift
Both lenses flagged the cumulative narrowing risk: restrictions accumulate silently across years without any single decision being clearly wrong. The feasibility-oriented mechanism is an annual broker call and a standing agenda item in the program review asking whether current restrictions are limiting relationship quality without clear safety rationale. The risk-oriented framing adds that the annual review should explicitly log which activities have been restricted or discontinued since launch and what that has cost the mentoring relationship, making the drift visible rather than letting it compound.

# Summary: Consent Frameworks and Data Privacy

## Executive Summary

The core tension running through this cluster is between what rigorous consent and privacy protection demands (dynamic authority mapping, layered re-consent cycles, access-logged case management systems, firewalled data tiers) and what a $15K faith-based nonprofit can actually implement without drowning in administrative overhead before a single mentorship meeting occurs. A feasibility-oriented view consistently offers low-cost, practical proxies: a one-page custody flowchart, modular consent addenda, a locked cabinet plus password-protected spreadsheet as the two-tier data structure, and staff training scenarios instead of expensive software. A risk-oriented view accepts those proxies as inadequate half-measures in high-stakes situations. Spreadsheets provide no audit trail. Flowcharts go stale the moment custody shifts, and well-meaning staff will violate data firewalls through informal conversation regardless of training.

Both lenses converge on several high-confidence points: consent for program participation and consent for data sharing are legally distinct and must be documented separately; a one-time enrollment signature is not a consent framework; the intake process carries outsized weight in shaping whether a youth like Jordan experiences being protected or being processed; and data minimization (not collecting sensitive information in the first place) is the cheapest and most effective privacy protection available.

Where they diverge most sharply is on the firewall question. A feasibility-oriented view treats a documented "what mentors are told" policy and a two-tier folder structure as sufficient to prevent inadvertent disclosure. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues that in a faith community (a dense social network, not a clinical setting), informal information diffusion will defeat policy-based firewalls. Without access logs, no one will ever know a breach occurred. This is an inverted framing worth preserving. The same program coordinator who is the solution (training staff, enforcing the firewall) is also the risk source: they are the well-meaning person who tells a mentor "useful context" over coffee.

---

## Key Themes

### Consent Is a Process, Not a Signature Event

Both lenses agree that enrollment consent is a starting point, not a permanent authorization. Ongoing activities (off-site outings, photography, funder data sharing) each carry independent risk profiles and require discrete, revisable consent decisions. A modular addendum system (one page per activity category, obtained as activities arise) is the convergent practical answer; the risk-oriented view adds that annual re-confirmation of photo consent is warranted as teens' privacy preferences evolve with age.

### The Custody Mapping Problem Doesn't Stay Solved

A consent matrix that correctly identifies the legal signatory at enrollment will be wrong the moment custody shifts (a non-rare event for teens in juvenile justice or foster care). Both lenses converge on the need for a "consent hold" trigger when custody changes, and on partnering with referral agencies to identify the correct consent authority as part of their intake packet. The risk-oriented view flags an additional gap: grandparents and older siblings who are de facto caregivers but lack legal guardianship authority cannot validly consent, and accepting their signatures creates compliance paperwork without genuine informed consent.

### The Firewall Is Social, Not Just Structural

Both lenses agree on a two-tier data structure separating what mentors see from restricted referral and case background material. They diverge on what makes that firewall hold. A feasibility-oriented view emphasizes policy clarity (a written "what mentors are told" statement shared with referral partners and concrete staff training scenarios) as sufficient. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues that in a congregant social network, information travels through informal conversation. Spreadsheets and shared folders provide no audit trail, and the underlying breach may never surface until reputational damage has already occurred.

### The Age-18 Transition Is a Legal Cliff, Not a Gradual Handoff

Both lenses identify the 18th birthday as a hard boundary: guardian authority terminates and all existing data-sharing authorizations require re-execution in the youth's name. Without a documented transition protocol (notification 60 days out, self-consent form, brief review meeting, archive-and-activate sequence), the organization risks retaining and using data on an adult without valid authorization. This item was convergent and is one of the highest-confidence action items in the cluster.

### Jordan's First Five Minutes Are Determinative

Both lenses treat the intake experience for court-referred youth as a trust-building event, not an administrative procedure. They converge on structural separation: paperwork and relationship conversation should not happen simultaneously, and should be conducted by different staff if possible. A feasibility-oriented view adds a concrete intake script opening with what the program will *not* share, and a "you decide what your mentor knows" choice that gives the youth agency over their own narrative. The risk-oriented view adds that abstract assurances of privacy are insufficient for youth who have learned that institutional promises are unreliable. Concrete specificity about who sees which information proves more credible than "your information is safe."

### Immediate (0-3 months)

[convergent]
- Define the minimum data set required to make a safe match. Convene a 30-minute meeting with the program director and case manager to answer "what does a mentor actually need to know?" and design the intake form around that floor, not around what referral agencies want to share. [convergent]

A risk-oriented view holds these are insufficient without access logs, and the program should plan a transition to a purpose-built case management tool within 12–18 months (though longer if budget constraints persist). [trade-off]
- Redesign the intake meeting to open with an explicit statement of what the program will *not* share (not with the mentor, not with the congregation, not with other participants), separate paperwork from the relationship conversation, and offer youth an active choice about what they share with their mentor. ### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Implement annual re-consent for photography and program materials use (separate from program participation consent) to respect the youth's evolving privacy preferences as they move from 13 toward adulthood. [unique: risk]

# Summary: Matching, Power, and Youth Agency

## Executive Summary

The cluster's most important tension is not whether to include youth voice in matching and safety design. Both lenses endorse it. The real question is an inverted one: a feasibility-oriented view treats youth advisory panels, preference forms, and structured intake conversations as the practical mechanism for giving teens genuine agency. A risk-oriented counterpoint flags that these same mechanisms systematically attract and represent only the teens with enough social capital and institutional comfort to participate in organized processes. The teens most likely to experience coercion, exploitation, or poor matching have the deepest trauma histories and the most distrust of systems. They are the least likely to show up to a panel, fill out a form honestly, or use a text line to request a rematch. The co-design process may produce legitimacy without protection where it is most needed.

Both lenses converge on two high-confidence points: mentor reliability and follow-through outpredict demographic similarity as retention variables, and treating rematch requests as success indicators rather than failures is operationally necessary. A feasibility-oriented case makes these points constructively. Build a four-week provisional match, give teens a confidential text line to request rematch, and target 10–15% early match adjustment as a healthy program signal. The risk-oriented counterpoint does not dispute any of this but raises the structural floor. If the only exit route is a formal escalation process, most teens will disengage silently rather than use it, producing dropout statistics that look like low motivation rather than bad match design.

On power dynamics for court-involved youth, the two lenses land at the same destination: a written firewall with JJ partners specifying that participation status will not be reported. But they arrive from opposite directions. A feasibility view frames this as a negotiable logistics item to finalize before accepting referrals. A risk-oriented view frames it as the difference between a voluntary program and a legal fiction. Even with the firewall in place, the structural power asymmetry between an adult volunteer and a referred teen cannot be dissolved by warmth or "non-judgmental" framing. Teens deserve honest, age-appropriate information about what the actual power structure is.

## Key Themes

### Mentor Reliability Over Demographic Match

Both lenses converge on this. Research from programs like BBBS shows that mentor commitment and follow-through outpredict demographic similarity, shared hobbies, and even race concordance as 12-month retention variables. A feasibility-oriented view translates this into a practical intake question for returning mentors: "What was the hardest moment in your last match, and how did you handle it?" Add a four-week provisional match period that surfaces mismatches early. A risk-oriented view notes that optimizing for surface-level similarity is often a staffing-efficiency choice disguised as a best-practice claim. A matching rubric weighted toward interests may screen out the most reliable volunteers who happen to share nothing obvious with the teen.

### The Rematch Pathway as a Structural Test of Youth Agency

Both lenses agree that a zero rematch rate is a failure signal, not a success signal. A feasibility-oriented view proposes a named confidential text channel that lets teens signal "this isn't working" without explanation, with the coordinator managing the mentor-side conversation. A risk-oriented counterpoint sharpens the stakes. When match dissolution requires a formal escalation event (meeting with staff, a form, an explanation), teens who have learned that voicing complaints leads to consequences will silently disengage rather than use it. The asymmetry of a bad match falls on the teen, not the mentor. The mentor returns to their regular life, while the teen may internalize the failure as evidence they are unlovable or not worth investment.

### "Voluntary" for Court-Involved Youth Requires a Written Firewall, Not Just Good Intentions

Both lenses converge: a referral pathway that allows case workers to know participation status (even implicitly) converts a voluntary program into a coercive one. A feasibility-oriented case treats this as a pre-launch logistics item. Negotiate the written non-reporting agreement with JJ partners before accepting any referrals. A risk-oriented view raises the structural point that warmth and good intentions leave the power asymmetry untouched. The mentor still has mandatory reporting obligations, still has access to staff if the relationship becomes difficult, and faces essentially no consequences if it ends. Teens deserve honest language about who reports to whom, not the framing of a friendship or equal partnership.

### Drift Toward Easy Cases is a Metrics and Staffing Design Problem

Both lenses agree the drift is real and structural. If program success is measured by aggregate retention rates without breaking them down by referral source, the drift will be invisible until it has already happened. A feasibility-oriented view proposes a concrete threshold: if JJ-referred teens show a retention rate more than 15 percentage points below school-referred teens at 6 months, that triggers a program review. A feasibility-oriented case argues that compensating five to seven teens ($300–400 within the existing budget) to review the draft boundary framework and then treating their output as the primary document rather than a supplement is both affordable and operationally superior to adult-designed compliance checklists. A risk-oriented counterpoint notes two structural risks: the teens available to participate in an organized panel are not the teens most likely to experience boundary violations, and the staff facilitator holds enormous agenda-setting power that can quietly steer the panel away from conclusions inconvenient for the organization. # Summary: Safety Protocols and the Relational Core

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension of this cluster is not "how much safety is enough" but something sharper: the same protective systems the program installs to safeguard teens may signal to those teens that they are surveillance subjects rather than trusted people. The teens most likely to read it that way are exactly the juvenile-justice-referred youth the program is designed to serve. Both lenses recognized this contradiction, but arrived at different conclusions about what to do with it.

A feasibility-oriented view argues the solution is reframing and sequencing: call the session log a "connection note," back-load administrative requirements to week six, replace blanket location rules with a tiered policy that expands flexibility as the match matures, and pilot the protocol package with five pairs before scaling. These moves preserve oversight while reducing the surveillance feel. A risk-oriented counterpoint holds that reframing alone is insufficient. A check-in log, however warmly named, still exists primarily to create a paper trail for liability purposes. Honesty about this distinction (separating organizational-protection protocols from teen-protection protocols) may itself be a form of respect.

Both lenses converged on one high-confidence point: the public-only meeting rule is a blunt instrument that may backfire with trauma-affected youth for whom crowded public spaces carry their own threat signals. Both independently proposed a tiered location policy with staged flexibility. Both also agreed that faith-based organizational identity requires explicit, not implicit, handling. They both stressed that mentor training must address how teens who have been processed by courts and schools read adult authority before the first match is made. Where they diverged most sharply was on the first-meeting design. The feasibility view favored structured-but-light orientation tools and shared activities to reduce pressure. The risk view warned that any program-designed ritual would read as another intake procedure and proposed instead that mentors let the teen set the agenda entirely, treating Marcus as a co-designer of the safety protocols rather than a subject of them.

## Key Themes

### Tiered Location Flexibility Over Blanket Rules
Both lenses converged on this, making it the cluster's highest-confidence structural recommendation. A public-only rule may actively harm the most vulnerable participants by associating the program with surveillance environments. A three-tier policy (public for the first three months, supervised semi-private after that, further flexibility as match quality is demonstrated) preserves protection without permanently capping relational depth. [convergent]

### Organizational Protection vs. Teen Protection Are Different Things
A risk-oriented view surfaced this as the cluster's most uncomfortable structural question. Most installed protocols (check-in logs, location rules, communication sign-off policies) were designed by insurance teams to create liability paper trails, not by youth-development researchers to protect teens. Conflating these two goals produces a protocol system optimized for neither. Naming them separately and designing the teen-protection layer from the teen's perspective is a redesign that no reframing exercise can substitute for. [unique: risk]

### Faith-Based Context Requires Explicit, Not Implicit, Inclusivity Commitments
Both lenses flagged that the organizational setting pre-sorts participants. Teens whose identities may conflict with congregational norms (LGBTQ+ youth, teens from religiously traumatic backgrounds) face a conditional welcome that training rhetoric alone cannot address. The feasibility view calls for a signed one-page covenant stating mentors will not proselytize and religious participation is not required. The risk view adds that recruitment materials and guardian consent documents must make inclusivity commitments explicit (not just training manuals) so the signal reaches families before enrollment. [convergent on problem; divergent on where the explicit signal must appear]

### The First Meeting as a Trust-Building Design Problem
The first session with a teen like Marcus is where the program's relational philosophy either manifests or collapses. A feasibility-oriented view recommends structured-but-light scaffolding: a one-page mentor guide, a shared low-stakes activity (choosing from a menu, filling out a favorites card), a brief three-way coordinator introduction that models how to talk with (not at) the teen, and a no-questions-asked exit option that both parties know exists. A risk-oriented counterpoint warns that any program-designed ritual signals institutional processing to a teen already experienced in being processed. The more powerful intervention may be to tell Marcus plainly what the safety requirements are and ask how he'd like to handle them, reframing protocols from management to mutual agreement. [divergent. structured scaffolding vs. teen-led agenda and co-design]

### Protocol Density in the First 90 Days Is the Highest-Risk Variable
Both lenses identified the early match period as fragile. The feasibility view provided the most operational specificity: push the first formal check-in report to week six, hold the first coordinator review call at month two, and pilot the full protocol package with five pairs before scaling. The risk view reinforces this observation: teens are watching whether the program trusts its mentors. Demonstrating trust at the outset shapes the entire relational arc. [convergent]

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)
- Adopt a three-tier location policy: public-only for the first three months, semi-private (church room with windowed door) after three completed months with coordinator sign-off, and further flexibility tied to demonstrated match quality, not just time elapsed. [convergent]
- Commission a one-day protocol audit. Map each existing rule to the specific harm it prevents. Distinguish protocols that protect the organization from those that protect the teen and name them as such in the policy manual. [unique: risk]
- Draft and circulate a one-page program covenant signed by mentors, teens, and guardians stating that mentors will not proselytize and religious participation is not required. Include this language in recruitment materials before enrollment, not only in orientation packets. [convergent]
- Back-load administrative requirements: push the first formal session log to week six and the first coordinator review call to month two. During the first four weeks, require only brief meeting-start and meeting-end confirmation. [convergent]

### Near-term (3-12 months)
- Pilot the full protocol package with five pairs in month one. Collect qualitative teen feedback via informal check-in ("Did anything about how the program works feel weird or uncomfortable?") and use responses to redesign the intake experience before scaling to all 40 pairs. [unique: feasibility]
- Replace session logs with "connection notes" (a five-field, two-minute form framed as a mentor reflection tool, not a compliance report) and train mentors to explain the purpose to their teen naturally. [trade-off: the feasibility view endorses reframing as sufficient; the risk view holds that reframing a liability log does not change what it is, and that teens referred through juvenile justice are more likely to perceive the distinction than program designers expect]
- Add a post-month-one numeric check-in from the program coordinator to all teens (1–5 comfort rating via text). Scores of 1 or 2 trigger a follow-up call. Build a no-questions-asked rematch option into the first meeting and name it explicitly to both parties before they meet. [convergent]
- Conduct active mentor recruitment outside the congregation by partnering with organizations, neighborhood associations, and alumni networks before match assignments. This reduces cultural mismatch, which is a documented predictor of early match dissolution. [unique: risk]

### Long-term (1+ years)
- Build a mid-year anonymous youth-perception survey that separately measures how safe teens feel and how watched they feel. Use these two scores (not adult satisfaction or session completion rates) as the primary protocol-effectiveness metrics, revising the safety architecture annually based on results. [unique: risk]
- At the twelve-month mark, analyze which meeting formats (public, semi-private, activity-based) correlate with longer match durations and use that data to update the default location policy. [unique: feasibility]

What does it feel like to be a teen who has been let down by institutions while slowly extending trust to an adult? How can the program's protocol design actively support that emotional process over time?

How does the program design the relationship's conclusion so that a match termination, especially an involuntary one, does not become another institutional abandonment event for a teen with a long history of them?

# Summary: Mentor Support, Retention, and Burnout

## Executive Summary

The program faces an inversion at the heart of its mentor support question: the support structures most likely to retain mentors (peer cohorts, proactive coordinator outreach, a named "month four" moment) are also the structures most likely to be underfunded and underbuilt when a $15K seed-funded program is racing toward launch. A feasibility-oriented view holds that lightweight, low-cost interventions (a weekly Y/N text check-in, a "month four survival kit" distributed at month three, peer pods of 6–8 mentors) can meaningfully reduce attrition without requiring additional staff. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues that programs built around mentor-initiated distress signals are not support structures at all. The mentors most likely to quietly exit are precisely those least likely to ask for help. Designing for the virtuous cycle of early engagement obscures the more probable starting state: skepticism from teens who have learned not to trust adults who eventually disappear.

Both lenses converge strongly on several points. Institutional knowledge about active matches must not live in one coordinator's head or spreadsheet. A shared match-tracking file with last-contact dates is a non-negotiable risk control, not an administrative nicety. Peer cohorts for mentors (groups of 4–8 meeting monthly) are endorsed by both as the highest-leverage, lowest-cost retention mechanism the program can build. Both also agree that the 6–9 month motivational trough is predictable enough that failing to pre-brief mentors about it is a design failure rather than a support gap.

The lenses diverge most sharply on check-in design and attrition math. A feasibility case supports structured biweekly coordinator calls in the first six months, supplemented by a simple weekly text check-in, as the backbone of early retention. A risk-oriented counterpoint notes that mandatory check-ins can feel like performance monitoring to autonomy-driven volunteers, potentially accelerating the exits the program is trying to prevent. On attrition, a feasibility view plans for 20–30% year-one loss and designates 5–8 reserve mentors accordingly. A risk-oriented view pushes harder. Research suggests 30–50% is structurally normal. Designing as though retention is primarily achievable may distort priorities. The program should invest as much in graceful churn protocols as in retention mechanics.

---

## Key Themes

### Proactive Outreach at Predictable Vulnerability Points
Both lenses agree that reactive support (waiting for mentors to signal distress) will systematically miss the highest-risk volunteers: conscientious, high-commitment adults who interpret their struggle as personal failure and disengage quietly. Proactive outreach timed to known trough windows (month two, month four, month seven) is a convergent recommendation, with specific language that normalizes difficulty ("Most mentors hit a rough patch around now. What's yours?") rather than prompting for problems. A feasibility-oriented view adds concrete artifacts: a "month four survival kit" delivered at month three. It contains a one-page reframe, low-pressure activity ideas, and the coordinator's direct number.

### The Peer Layer as the First Line of Response
Both lenses endorse organizing mentors into small peer cohorts (4–8 people, meeting monthly) and treat this as the structural mechanism that makes mentor sustainability possible. A feasibility-oriented view is specific: these groups require only a room and a volunteer facilitator and can absorb routine doubt before it reaches the coordinator. A risk-oriented view draws the design rationale from the sponsor/sponsee model in recovery communities. Peer sustainability works not because institutions support individuals but because other helpers hold each other. This view warns that borrowing the AA framework without building the horizontal community infrastructure imports the scaffolding without the building itself.

### Coordinator as Single Point of Failure
Both lenses flag the coordinator concentration risk with unusual agreement on the remedy: structured match notes in a shared system, accessible to program leadership, updated weekly. A risk-oriented view extends the concern to the teens themselves. If the coordinator is also the safety anchor teens contact when something goes wrong with their mentor, coordinator departure is not just a logistics problem but a trust rupture for the young people in the program. Transition protocols for coordinator succession should include direct, in-person introductions between the outgoing and incoming coordinator and each teen.

### Training Built from Internal Wisdom, Fortified with External Protocols
Both lenses reject a purely imported generic curriculum. A feasibility-oriented view offers a concrete alternative: interview 5–6 people in the congregation who have already done informal mentoring, use those practices as the training backbone, and add a narrow set of program-specific modules (mandatory reporting, boundary protocols, documentation). A risk-oriented counterpoint flags that 20 years of internal practice may also carry 20 years of unexamined assumptions about what adolescent struggle looks like, particularly for teens in the juvenile justice system whose trauma may present as defiance. At least one external practitioner (a trauma-informed educator or juvenile-justice counselor) should stress-test the internal curriculum before finalization.

### Graceful Churn vs. Retention Optimization
This is the cluster's sharpest divergence. A feasibility-oriented case designs around 20–30% attrition: identify reserve mentors, run quarterly new-mentor orientations, and designate a pipeline owner in the congregation. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues this estimate is too optimistic. 30–50% is structurally normal in volunteer-based mentoring. The program may be over-investing in retention while under-investing in graceful exit protocols. Many departures are not emotional but logistical (a job change, a move). Without a designed off-ramp, mentors ghost rather than triggering a formal exit that feels like abandoning a vulnerable teen. Both agree continuous recruitment must be embedded from launch rather than treated as a year-two project.

---

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)

- Build a shared match-tracking file (accessible to program leadership, not only the coordinator) capturing: match start date, last contact date, any flags. Assign the coordinator 10 minutes/week to update it. [convergent]
- Designate 2–3 lead mentors from within the 40 volunteers, each covering 10–12 pairs as a backup point of contact. Name this role before launch, not after the first coordinator absence. [convergent]
- Interview 5–6 congregants who have done informal mentoring; use their responses as the backbone of training curriculum design. Supplement with at least one external practitioner to stress-test the result against juvenile-justice trauma presentations. [convergent]
- Designate a "pipeline owner" volunteer responsible for 2–4 new mentor candidates per quarter. Establish a standing quarterly orientation cadence even if only 2–3 people attend — the on-ramp must always be open. [convergent]
- Draft a crisis communications protocol for a mentor accusation scenario *before* any incident occurs. A faith-based volunteer cohort is especially vulnerable to social-trust contagion; this protocol pays off only if something goes wrong, which is why it tends not to get funded. [unique: risk]

### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Form mentor peer cohorts of 6–8, meeting monthly, with a rotating volunteer facilitator. Provide a simple 60-minute agenda: one challenge shared per mentor, group input, one specific next step. No coordinator required. [convergent]
- Distribute a "month four survival kit" proactively at the three-month mark: a one-page normalization reframe, two low-pressure activity ideas, the coordinator's direct number, and a simple re-engagement script ("I've been thinking about you — want to grab food this week?"). [convergent]
- Design coordinator check-in cadence with a gradual taper: biweekly calls in months 1–3, monthly in months 4–6, then mentor-cohort-primary thereafter. This avoids the cliff effect of abrupt handoff from intensive onboarding to "you're on your own." [trade-off: a feasibility case for structured biweekly calls as the retention backbone; a risk-oriented counterpoint that mandatory check-ins can feel like performance monitoring to autonomy-driven volunteers, which may accelerate attrition]
- Build a teen-facing coordinator touchpoint into the program: a low-stakes regular check-in that gives teens a channel to signal discomfort without confronting their mentor directly. This is not a bonus feature — it is the accountability layer that surfaces failures the mentor self-report system will miss. [unique: risk]
- Develop a monthly peer "round table" where mentors briefly name one challenge and receive group input. This builds shared language for hard moments (e.g., "showing up is the intervention," "silence is a test of consistency") without requiring a trained facilitator or additional budget. [convergent]

### Long-term (1+ years)

- Size the reserve mentor pool for 30–50% year-one attrition, not 20–30%. Build graceful exit protocols (designed off-ramps that let a departing mentor leave without feeling they are abandoning a teen) with as much care as retention mechanics. [trade-off: a feasibility case for 20–30% planning and a designated reserve cohort; a risk-oriented counterpoint that 30–50% is structurally normal and under-designing for churn distorts priorities]
- Commission a developmental map of what a healthy match looks like month by month (not outcome metrics but relational markers) so mentors can locate themselves on a progress arc rather than against a binary of success or failure. This is the prerequisite to Deb being able to understand why a silent car ride at month four is progress. [convergent]

# Summary: Outcome Measurement and Success Definition

## Executive Summary

The central measurement tension runs in two directions at once. The risk-oriented lens warns that minimizing survey burden produces data too thin to detect program failure—a program in which mentors and teens sit in silence for an hour still satisfies the metric. The feasibility-oriented lens argues that over-instrumenting a population already navigating school and justice-system demands is the surest way to get safe, misleading answers instead of honest ones. Both stances are correct in their own domain. This is a genuine trade-off, not a planning error.

Both lenses converge on a small number of high-confidence design decisions. Baseline data should be pulled from existing school counselor relationships rather than administered fresh. Data collection must be structurally separated from match status (teens must understand that honest negative answers cannot jeopardize their pairing). Privacy terms should be written in plain language at intake specifying what data cannot flow to juvenile justice or schools. Both also converge on maintaining two parallel tracks: funder-visible lagging indicators (attendance, grades, match retention) and an internal qualitative layer. The explicit acknowledgment is that the former will be flat in year one as a structural feature of youth development timelines, not program failure.

The sharpest divergence concerns teen-defined success and absenteeism framing. A feasibility-oriented view holds that capturing teen-defined goals at match initiation (a brief "what does a good year look like to you?" sheet) is a 20-minute, zero-cost anchor that meaningfully separates program impact from funder metrics. A risk-oriented counterpoint: teens with repeated institutional failures may lack a vocabulary for success or will give back the answer they think adults want. Authentic youth-defined outcomes are a relationship-depth problem, not a data-collection problem, and eliciting them too early produces socially desirable noise. On absenteeism, the risk-oriented lens pushes hardest. Embedding school attendance as a success metric accepts without examination that the schools these teens attend are worth attending and may filter toward mentors with conventional academic orientations, reducing the program's credibility with the teens most skeptical of those institutions. The feasibility-oriented lens redirects this toward structural design: don't make attendance an intervention goal, make teen-reported wellbeing the goal, and give mentors written permission to support alternatives (GED pathways, vocational programs) when that's what a teen actually needs.

---

## Key Themes

### Two Parallel Measurement Tracks Are Both Necessary

Both lenses endorse a dual-track approach: Track A (funder-legible metrics: attendance, grades, match retention) reported faithfully, and Track B (internal qualitative layer: mentor narrative logs, teen-defined goals, self-efficacy check-ins) reviewed by staff but not shared in formal reports. The risk-oriented view adds a specific mechanism: a "shadow metrics" quarterly review to keep staff anchored to the relational work that Track A will not capture and cannot reward. This is convergent and high-confidence.

### Baseline Data Should Exploit Existing Infrastructure, Not Add New Burden

Both lenses agree on four baseline data points collectable at match initiation: prior-semester attendance rate, current GPA or credit status, a brief self-efficacy item, and current justice-system obligations. Both agree these should be pulled via school counselor data-sharing agreements (a formal MOU, reviewed by legal counsel once, applied to all partner schools) rather than administered independently. The risk-oriented lens adds a warning: those counselor relationships are individual, not institutional, and can evaporate with staff turnover. District-level agreements are the mitigation.

### "Healthy Graduation" Taxonomy Must Be Defined Before Launch

Both lenses independently converged on the need for a formal exit taxonomy distinguishing healthy graduation from premature exit. Both agree criteria must be established before launch, not after the first difficult exit. The risk-oriented lens adds the specific risk that the "healthy graduation" category, once it exists, will be applied liberally to protect metrics. The mitigation is supervisor approval plus documented criteria (teen articulates specific future goals; mentor provides growth narrative) rather than mentor self-report.

### Meeting Consistency Is the Single Most Actionable Leading Indicator

Both lenses treat meeting regularity as the primary early-warning signal, consistent with mentorship evaluation research showing that match duration and meeting frequency are stronger predictors of outcomes than program content. A practical threshold both lenses support is fewer than two meetings in a month, which triggers a coordinator check-in call. This is observable, actionable, and requires only coordinator time.

### Absenteeism Framing Has Organizational Values Stakes, Not Just Metric Stakes

The risk-oriented lens pushes this farthest: whether to treat attendance improvement as a program goal is an organizational values question about whether the schools these teens attend deserve their presence. Hiding that question inside attendance metrics is dishonest both to funders and to teens. A feasibility-oriented view agrees on the structural fix but frames it operationally: separate program-level funder reporting (attendance trends, tracked because required) from individual match goals (set by the teen, not defaulting to attendance). Writing explicit permission for mentors to support non-conventional pathways into training materials is the low-cost design move that prevents the metric from reshaping mentor behavior over time.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)

- Draft and legally review a one-page school data-sharing MOU template; negotiate at the district level, not through individual counselors. [convergent]
- Define the four-category exit taxonomy (natural graduation, life transition, program-initiated close, premature exit) and the specific criteria for healthy graduation designation, before training or launch. [convergent]
- Write a one-page plain-language data privacy disclosure (middle-school reading level) specifying what is collected, who sees it, and what it cannot be used for — including an explicit list of parties who cannot access it (probation officers, school administrators). Include it in the intake packet. [convergent]
- Write a mentor training module (20 minutes) that presents the 35% absenteeism context, prompts mentors to examine their own school-attendance assumptions, and explicitly grants permission to support non-conventional paths (GED, vocational, community college) when that serves the teen's actual goals. [unique: feasibility]

### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Establish a 90-day early-indicator review per match: coordinator checks meeting frequency, mentor log quality, and a single follow-up self-efficacy question. Flag at-risk matches for support before dissolution. [convergent]
- Begin teen-defined success elicitation no earlier than month three (risk-oriented recommendation) or at match initiation (feasibility-oriented recommendation) — the trade-off is between data authenticity and measurement timing; programs with more trust-building infrastructure at intake can move earlier. [trade-off]
- Launch a quarterly internal "shadow metrics" review tracking qualitative relationship depth indicators that do not appear in funder reports, to keep staff anchored to work Track A will miss. [unique: risk]
- In every funder progress report, include an explicit paragraph naming the developmental timescale lag: "Youth development outcomes at the relationship level typically manifest over 3–5 years; we are reporting leading indicators as agreed for Year 1." [convergent]

### Long-term (1+ years)

- At 12-month match close, conduct a structured comparison of baseline intake data against current status on the four agreed metrics — this is the primary funder deliverable, even if the full outcome story takes longer. [convergent]
- Build a 30-day post-exit check-in (coordinator call, three questions, five minutes) into standard protocol for all match closures, including early exits. [unique: feasibility]
- Convene a listening session with teens and families specifically about what school does and does not do for them before finalizing any second-year outcome framework that includes school-related metrics. [unique: risk]

Intake data collection may trigger anxiety, distrust, or shutdown regardless of the privacy disclosure's content. A trauma-informed framing of the data collection encounter itself (pacing, who administers it, verbal vs. written options) is entirely absent.

# Summary: Sustainability, Endings, and Long-Term Horizon

## Executive Summary

The core tension in this cluster is not between ambition and caution, but between obligation and capacity. The moment the program matches a vulnerable teen, it takes on a debt that doesn't disappear if the funding does. Both the feasibility and risk perspectives agree most programs underestimate this. The disagreement is about what to do with that recognition. A feasibility-oriented view argues that the debt is manageable with the right structures: a graduated exit ladder, a wind-down protocol written before the first match is opened, cost modeled at 15/25/40 pairs before the first dollar is spent. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues that the structures themselves are often a polite fiction designed for clean scenarios. They remain untested under pressure and are funded by the same grant that evaporates when they're needed most.

Both perspectives identify the same coordinator mechanism but from opposite directions. The feasibility view treats coordinators as the solution: biweekly mentor check-ins, structured 30-day wind-downs, exit interviews, pilot review time. The risk view flags that coordinators are also the first people to lose their jobs when funding collapses, meaning the wind-down executor cannot be a staff role. This inversion (coordinator as load-bearing structure; coordinator as single point of failure) is the organizing tension of the sustainability question.

On the long horizon, both perspectives agree that a 20-year intergenerational institution is possible but requires cheap structural decisions made in year one: distinct program identity, disciplined record-keeping, a governance seat for community members who are not congregation members. The divergence is about ambition sequencing. The feasibility view proposes a concrete alumni-to-mentor pipeline (a written Junior Mentor job description with a modest stipend in Year 3 or 4), while the risk view cautions that publicizing the 20-year vision too early attracts legacy-minded board members poorly suited to the unglamorous work that makes year one survive.

## Key Themes

### Match endings carry asymmetric risk for juvenile-justice-referred teens
Both perspectives flagged this, making it the cluster's highest-confidence signal. Teens referred through probation or counselor expectations may not feel free to voice that a match isn't working. Post-termination harm data also lags the event by months. The feasibility view recommends routing post-termination check-ins through a neutral party (school counselor, not program staff) and tracking three concrete indicators at close: school attendance in the 60 days following termination, new juvenile justice contact, and whether the teen has at least one other identified adult support. The risk view adds that the feedback channel for voicing problems must be fully separated from case management reporting from the start, not patched in after the first complaint.

### The one-year commitment functions as a ceiling, not a floor
Both perspectives identified this failure mode. A mentor who hears "one year" mentally marks an exit date. A teen who has built genuine trust by month eleven is effectively counting down to a loss. The feasibility-oriented solution is a simple administrative intervention: a "continuation form" at month eleven with three options (renew formally, continue informally, close with a celebration), normalizing continuation without requiring policy overhaul. The risk-oriented view frames the issue more sharply: the reframe must happen in training, not paperwork. Mentors need to actively surface the continuation question at month ten, not wait for the teen to wonder.

### The seed-to-sustaining gap is structurally mismatched to the relationship timeline
Both perspectives identify a specific danger: the $15K grant will be substantially consumed by setup costs (background checks, training, insurance) before any relationship has had time to prove its value. The 12-month report date and the point at which relationships generate compelling evidence are not the same date. The feasibility view proposes building a 6-month proof-of-concept report at months 8–9 to give funders early signal. The risk view notes that comparable faith-based programs bridge to sustained funding through congregation relationships and major-donor pipelines. These took years to cultivate and cannot be replicated on a grant timeline. The honest 18-month survival probability, absent a parallel fundraising infrastructure built from month two, is roughly 50–60%.

### Orderly wind-down requires pre-funding and non-staff ownership
This is the cluster's sharpest divergence in practical terms. Both perspectives agree a wind-down protocol is necessary. The feasibility view specifies what it should contain: 90-day notice to all families and partners, systematic handoff of each teen to at least one other identified adult support, final documentation of all match records, communication to referring schools and juvenile justice partners. The risk-oriented view argues that this protocol is meaningless unless it is pre-funded and governed by a volunteer board member (not staff) as the designated wind-down executor. Staff are the first to go when funding collapses, so wind-down ownership must sit elsewhere.

### Intergenerational institution-building requires cheap structural decisions made early
Both perspectives agree on a set of low-cost year-one decisions that compound over 20 years: distinct program identity not tied to any individual pastor or staff member, formal alumni tracking (not just social media contact), and a governance structure that includes at least one community member who is not a congregation member. The feasibility view adds the specific mechanism for alumni re-entry: a written Junior Mentor or Peer Navigator role with a modest stipend ($200–300/month) in Year 3 or 4. The risk-oriented view cautions against publicizing the 20-year vision until the program has at least two full cohort cycles completed, avoiding the attraction of board energy better suited to legacy-building than year-one rigor.

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- Teens referred through juvenile justice may not feel free to voice that a match isn't working; the feedback and termination protocols must account for non-voluntary participants from the start.
- Post-termination harm is real and trackable: school attendance, new juvenile justice contact, and presence of at least one other adult support are the right indicators, routed through a neutral party.
- The $15K grant timeline and the relationship evidence timeline are mismatched; a proof-of-concept report at months 8–9 is necessary to avoid arriving at the Year 2 funding conversation with no usable data.
- A wind-down protocol is meaningless without pre-funding and a non-staff executor; both need to be designated before the first match opens.
- A 20-year intergenerational institution is achievable but requires year-one structural decisions: distinct program identity, formal alumni tracking, and a governance seat for community members outside the congregation.

**Trade-offs** (the two perspectives disagreed):
- A feasibility case that the continuation form at month eleven is the key intervention (normalizing continuation through a simple administrative step) versus a risk-oriented view that the form is insufficient. The reframe must happen in mentor training, with coordinators actively surfacing the continuation question at month ten rather than waiting for the paperwork deadline.
- A feasibility case that building five strong pilot matches with extended intake protocols produces a replicable template and Year 2 funder evidence, versus a risk-oriented view that the five easiest cases are systematically unrepresentative of the harder intake that will dominate once word spreads. The founding cohort should deliberately include at least one high-challenge match to generate transferable learning.
- A feasibility case for publishing the 20-year institutional vision as a recruiting and alignment tool in year one, versus a risk-oriented view that early publication of the legacy vision attracts board energy poorly matched to unglamorous operational work. That unglamorous work is what actually makes year one survive.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one perspective raised):
- The "replacement mentor" promise is often unfulfilled. Programs promise warm handoffs that require a reserve pool of trained-but-unmatched mentors to actually execute. Without a standing reserve of three to five such mentors, the promise produces a "teen waits six months" gap. [feasibility-oriented]
- The neighborhood system bears the downstream cost of program failure. If the program recruits a vulnerable teen, builds real trust, and then closes abruptly, the measurable harm (increased disengagement, reinforced distrust of institutions) falls on the school and juvenile justice systems, not on the program. A formal conversation with referring counselors and liaisons asking "if we fold in year two, what does that mean for the kids you sent us?" should happen before launch, not after. [risk-oriented]
- Early-success narrative risk: if the founding five matches produce compelling stories, funders and leadership will anchor to those stories and create pressure to replicate the feel of the easy early matches rather than honestly serving the harder population. Communicating early-stage results explicitly as "learning phase" outputs (not proof of concept) is a strategic framing decision. That decision needs to be made before results are reported. [risk-oriented]

**Neither-perspective gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- **Emotional/phenomenological**: Neither perspective addressed what teens themselves experience when a match ends. Not the measurable outcomes, but the phenomenology of being "transitioned" through a structured protocol. For a teen referred through juvenile justice who has never had a reliable adult relationship, the experience of a "facilitated closure meeting" may carry meaning (positive or negative) that the intake process, match criteria, and termination protocol all miss. A participatory design element (even one conversation with two or three current teens about what a good ending would feel like) would surface this.
- **Equity/distributional**: Both perspectives analyzed the program's obligations to teens in aggregate. Neither addressed whether the wind-down and continuation protocols distribute risk and protection equally across the teen population. Specifically: Are teens with less stable home environments, more complex juvenile justice involvement, or fewer alternative adult supports systematically more exposed to harm when a match ends than teens with stronger baseline support networks?
- **Political-economy/institutional**: Neither perspective addressed the referring ecosystem's incentive structure. School counselors and juvenile justice liaisons refer teens to this program because it serves their caseloads. If the program closes abruptly, those referrers absorb the fallout and become less likely to refer to the next program. The program has leverage here. It is providing a service those systems need. It could use that leverage to negotiate formal partnership agreements (not just informal relationships) that give the program more institutional stability and give the referring systems a formal stake in its survival.
