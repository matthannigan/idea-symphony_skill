# Summary: Consent Frameworks and Data Privacy

## Executive Summary

The core tension running through this cluster is between what rigorous consent and privacy protection demands (dynamic authority mapping, layered re-consent cycles, access-logged case management systems, firewalled data tiers) and what a $15K faith-based nonprofit can actually implement without drowning in administrative overhead before a single mentorship meeting occurs. A feasibility-oriented view consistently offers low-cost, practical proxies: a one-page custody flowchart, modular consent addenda, a locked cabinet plus password-protected spreadsheet as the two-tier data structure, and staff training scenarios instead of expensive software. A risk-oriented view accepts those proxies as inadequate half-measures in high-stakes situations. Spreadsheets provide no audit trail. Flowcharts go stale the moment custody shifts, and well-meaning staff will violate data firewalls through informal conversation regardless of training.

Both lenses converge on several high-confidence points: consent for program participation and consent for data sharing are legally distinct and must be documented separately; a one-time enrollment signature is not a consent framework; the intake process carries outsized weight in shaping whether a youth like Jordan experiences being protected or being processed; and data minimization (not collecting sensitive information in the first place) is the cheapest and most effective privacy protection available.

Where they diverge most sharply is on the firewall question. A feasibility-oriented view treats a documented "what mentors are told" policy and a two-tier folder structure as sufficient to prevent inadvertent disclosure. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues that in a faith community (a dense social network, not a clinical setting), informal information diffusion will defeat policy-based firewalls. Without access logs, no one will ever know a breach occurred. This is an inverted framing worth preserving. The same program coordinator who is the solution (training staff, enforcing the firewall) is also the risk source: they are the well-meaning person who tells a mentor "useful context" over coffee.

## Key Themes

### Consent Is a Process, Not a Signature Event

Both lenses agree that enrollment consent is a starting point, not a permanent authorization. Ongoing activities (off-site outings, photography, funder data sharing) each carry independent risk profiles and require discrete, revisable consent decisions. A modular addendum system (one page per activity category, obtained as activities arise) is the convergent practical answer; the risk-oriented view adds that annual re-confirmation of photo consent is warranted as teens' privacy preferences evolve with age.

### The Custody Mapping Problem Doesn't Stay Solved

A consent matrix that correctly identifies the legal signatory at enrollment will be wrong the moment custody shifts (a non-rare event for teens in juvenile justice or foster care). Both lenses converge on the need for a "consent hold" trigger when custody changes, and on partnering with referral agencies to identify the correct consent authority as part of their intake packet. The risk-oriented view flags an additional gap: grandparents and older siblings who are de facto caregivers but lack legal guardianship authority cannot validly consent, and accepting their signatures creates compliance paperwork without genuine informed consent.

### The Firewall Is Social, Not Just Structural

Both lenses agree on a two-tier data structure separating what mentors see from restricted referral and case background material. They diverge on what makes that firewall hold. A feasibility-oriented view emphasizes policy clarity (a written "what mentors are told" statement shared with referral partners and concrete staff training scenarios) as sufficient. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues that in a congregant social network, information travels through informal conversation. Spreadsheets and shared folders provide no audit trail, and the underlying breach may never surface until reputational damage has already occurred.

### The Age-18 Transition Is a Legal Cliff, Not a Gradual Handoff

Both lenses identify the 18th birthday as a hard boundary: guardian authority terminates and all existing data-sharing authorizations require re-execution in the youth's name. Without a documented transition protocol (notification 60 days out, self-consent form, brief review meeting, archive-and-activate sequence), the organization risks retaining and using data on an adult without valid authorization. This item was convergent and is one of the highest-confidence action items in the cluster.

### Jordan's First Five Minutes Are Determinative

Both lenses treat the intake experience for court-referred youth as a trust-building event, not an administrative procedure. They converge on structural separation: paperwork and relationship conversation should not happen simultaneously, and should be conducted by different staff if possible. A feasibility-oriented view adds a concrete intake script opening with what the program will *not* share, and a "you decide what your mentor knows" choice that gives the youth agency over their own narrative. The risk-oriented view adds that abstract assurances of privacy are insufficient for youth who have learned that institutional promises are unreliable. Concrete specificity about who sees which information proves more credible than "your information is safe."

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)

- Create a one-page custody-authority flowchart mapping custody status to correct consent signatory; train all intake staff to complete it before presenting any forms; include a clause in enrollment documents acknowledging that consent transfers with guardianship changes and that new consent will be sought within 10 business days of a reported change. [convergent]
- Define the minimum data set required to make a safe match. Convene a 30-minute meeting with the program director and case manager to answer "what does a mentor actually need to know?" and design the intake form around that floor, not around what referral agencies want to share. [convergent]
- Implement a two-tier access structure from day one: a "program file" (contact info, meeting logs, goals) mentors can see, and a "restricted file" (referral source, case background) accessible only to the program director and designated case manager, stored separately with physical or password-based access control. A locked cabinet and password-protected spreadsheet meet minimum viability. A risk-oriented view holds these are insufficient without access logs, and the program should plan a transition to a purpose-built case management tool within 12–18 months (though longer if budget constraints persist). [trade-off]
- Redesign the intake meeting to open with an explicit statement of what the program will *not* share (not with the mentor, not with the congregation, not with other participants), separate paperwork from the relationship conversation, and offer youth an active choice about what they share with their mentor. [convergent]

### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Build a modular consent addendum system: one page per non-standard activity category (off-site outings, photography, funder data sharing), obtained in concrete context as activities arise rather than front-loaded at enrollment. Tie data-sharing consent to specific funder reporting dates rather than vague "program evaluation purposes" language. [convergent]
- Write and distribute a one-paragraph "what mentors are told" policy to all referral partners, and conduct a two-hour annual staff training with concrete scenarios (e.g., "a mentor asks why Jordan was referred; what do you say?") to reduce inadvertent disclosure risk. [unique: feasibility]
- Document a formal age-18 transition checklist: (1) notify youth 60 days before birthday that consent transfers, (2) send self-consent form for review, (3) schedule a brief meeting to answer questions, (4) archive guardian consent and activate youth's own consent as governing document. [convergent]
- Execute data-sharing agreements with each institutional partner (school counselors, juvenile justice agencies) that define permissible uses and prohibit re-disclosure; without these, the school counselor's referral may itself constitute a FERPA violation by the sending institution. [unique: risk]

### Long-term (1+ years)

- Adopt a data retention and destruction schedule: case background materials destroyed after program completion or two years of inactivity; program files retained for seven years for funder audit purposes. [unique: feasibility]
- Transition from spreadsheet-based case management to a purpose-built system with role-based access control and access logging; the access log is what makes a firewall enforceable rather than aspirational. [unique: risk]
- Implement annual re-consent for photography and program materials use (separate from program participation consent) to respect the youth's evolving privacy preferences as they move from 13 toward adulthood. [unique: risk]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- Consent for program participation and consent for data use (funder reporting, photography, off-site outings) are legally and ethically distinct and must be documented separately. Bundling them into a single signature creates future liability.
- Data minimization at intake is the most cost-effective privacy protection: not collecting what you don't need eliminates the liability before it exists.
- The age-18 transition requires a documented re-consent protocol; operating on guardian authorization after a participant's birthday creates legal exposure.
- The intake experience for court-referred youth is a trust-critical event; separating relationship conversation from administrative paperwork is a feasible structural intervention that costs nothing.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility-oriented case that a locked cabinet and password-protected spreadsheet provide adequate two-tier data separation for a small nonprofit's current scale. A risk-oriented counterpoint holds that this structure provides no audit trail, making firewall breaches invisible and unenforceable after the fact. The congregation's social network will route information around structural firewalls regardless.
- A feasibility-oriented case that a written "what mentors are told" policy plus concrete staff training scenarios is sufficient to prevent inadvertent disclosure. A risk-oriented counterpoint argues that informal information-sharing in a faith community is driven by social norms and relational trust rather than policy compliance, and will defeat the policy over time.
- A feasibility-oriented case for verbal consent (witnessed, date-stamped) as a temporary bridge when the appropriate guardian is unavailable at intake. A risk-oriented counterpoint holds that proceeding without written consent for court-involved youth, even for limited activities, creates legal exposure beyond what a convenience argument can justify.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- Non-traditional guardians (older siblings, grandparents without formal custody) cannot legally consent and must not be treated as valid signatories. The program needs a legal pathway for these cases, not workaround signatures. [unique: risk]
- FERPA does not apply to the program directly. Data-sharing agreements with school partners are needed to legalize the referral pipeline itself, not simply to govern what happens once data is received. [unique: risk]
- A data retention and destruction schedule is achievable at minimal cost and protects against claims that sensitive juvenile records were held longer than necessary. [unique: feasibility]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- *Emotional/phenomenological*: Both lenses addressed Jordan's intake experience procedurally, but neither examined what happens in the weeks following enrollment, when a court-involved teen decides whether the trust the intake conversation offered actually holds. The gap is a post-intake experience design. How does Jordan learn, through program behavior, that the firewall is real?
- *Equity/distributional*: Both lenses treated the consent complexity of juvenile-justice referrals as a compliance problem. Neither examined the distributional effect: teens referred through juvenile justice face a higher documentation burden, more custodial instability, and more privacy risk than self-referred or school-referred youth in the same program. This means the youth most disadvantaged by prior system involvement are also the most burdened by the program's own intake process. A youth-equity lens would ask whether intake procedures can be calibrated to reduce that disparity.
