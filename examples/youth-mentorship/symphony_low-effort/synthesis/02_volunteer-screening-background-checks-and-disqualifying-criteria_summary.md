# Summary: Volunteer Screening, Background Checks, and Disqualifying Criteria

## Executive Summary

The core tension in designing this program's screening framework is not whether to do background checks (both lenses agree on that) but whether a contextual review process for borderline cases is an asset or a liability. A feasibility-oriented view argues that a two-tier policy (categorical disqualifiers plus a documented contextual review panel) expands the volunteer pipeline, borrows legally tested precedent from foster care and BBBS, and provides the paper trail that protects the organization if harm occurs. A risk-oriented counterpoint flags that every contextual approval creates a discoverable written record. If a borderline-approved volunteer later causes harm, plaintiffs' counsel will subpoena the policy and ask why someone with that history was cleared. A nuanced, well-intentioned criteria document may invite more scrutiny than a conservative categorical rule, unless it is drafted with legal counsel reviewing the specific language before board adoption.

Both lenses converge on two high-confidence points: funnel attrition is severe and predictable, and the raw 40-volunteer headcount should not drive capacity commitments. A realistic planning assumption of 10–12 active matches at launch is far more defensible than building infrastructure around a pool of 40. Both lenses also agree that simplicity in screening architecture is a feature at this scale. The gig-platform tiered model offers a useful conceptual frame (calibrate screening depth to actual supervision level) but its operational machinery is over-engineered for a faith org with in-kind administrative staff.

The most important unique contribution from the risk lens is the insurance gap. Many volunteer liability policies explicitly exclude harm caused by volunteers approved over standard criteria. This is not a theoretical risk but a potentially catastrophic one. Confirming coverage for borderline approvals with the insurer in writing must precede any contextual review policy going live. The most important unique contribution from the feasibility lens is practical pipeline management: interview before background check, a low-friction digital application that captures interest immediately, and a simple stage-tracking spreadsheet that turns the first cohort into institutional learning for the second.

## Key Themes

### Funnel Reality vs. Headcount Assumptions (Convergent)

Both lenses independently arrive at the same conclusion: the 40-volunteer headcount is not a stable planning baseline. Interest expressed at a congregational announcement is social commitment, not considered commitment. A realistic funnel (40 expressions → 25 applications → 20 cleared checks → 15 training completions → 10–12 active matches) should anchor all funder commitments and staffing decisions from day one. Overbuilding infrastructure around 40 and underdelivering 12 demoralizes staff and strains grant relationships.

### Calibrate Screening Depth to Actual Access Level (Convergent)

Both lenses draw the same lesson from adjacent domains: the relevant variable is supervision structure, not program type. Foster care's intensive screening is calibrated to 24/7 unsupervised custodial access and does not translate directly to weekly mentors who meet in supervised spaces. Gig platforms offer the right conceptual frame (higher bar for unsupervised access, lower bar for supervised settings) without the operational complexity. This distinction allows the program to preserve pipeline for group-activity mentors while applying stricter screening to community-location, one-on-one pairs. [recurring]

### The Contextual Review Double-Edged Sword (Divergent)

A feasibility-oriented case for a documented contextual review panel (program director plus board designee, written checklist, retained rationale) is that it creates accountability, a defensible precedent, and a learning record while avoiding the pipeline loss of categorical over-screening. A risk-oriented counterpoint notes that a written policy articulating nuanced criteria is a discoverable document. Every contextual approval is a potential exhibit in a wrongful supervision lawsuit, and the cure is legal counsel reviewing the policy language before adoption, not after an incident. The panel is workable only if insurance coverage for borderline approvals is confirmed in writing first and the reviewers have clearly documented indemnification.

### Screening Sequence Matters (Unique: Risk-Oriented)

Placing the background check before the interview (because it is administratively easier to schedule) creates a false sense of security. The interview frequently surfaces disqualifying information that background checks miss: undisclosed relationships with youth in the target population, concerning personal motivations, or boundary-setting deficits. Running a phone screen first, then advancing only interview-cleared candidates to background checks, saves money, reduces the pool of conditional-approval anxiety, and correctly positions the check as confirmatory rather than gatekeeping.

### Administrative Capacity as a Hidden Constraint (Convergent)

Neither adjacent-domain model (gig platforms or BBBS) was designed for an organization whose administrative staff is shared in-kind with a church running other programs simultaneously. A processing spike (all 40 applicants in the same month) creates bottlenecks that leave enthusiastic volunteers in awkward holding patterns. Both lenses recommend resolving this before launch: either designate a dedicated screening coordinator or formalize a capacity agreement with the church administrative staff that specifies turnaround times and overflow protocols.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)

- Confirm in writing with the liability insurer whether borderline-case contextual approvals void volunteer-related coverage before drafting any contextual review criteria. Do not publish a tiered policy until this is resolved. [unique: risk]
- Verify the state's specific mandatory disqualifying-offense list for youth-serving programs and build a two-tier policy document (categorical disqualifiers with no discretion, plus contextual review criteria) reviewed by legal counsel before board adoption. [convergent]
- Build the background check cost ($1,000–$2,000 for 40 volunteers at $20–$50 each) explicitly into the $15K seed grant budget as a line item, not as overhead. [unique: feasibility]
- Prepare a low-friction digital application (one page, under 10 minutes) and deploy it before any public recruitment announcement so interest is captured before it cools. [unique: feasibility]

### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Adopt BBBS's two-check minimum (national sex offender registry + state criminal history) as the baseline, adding fingerprinting if state law mandates it; use an established vendor (Checkr, Sterling, or a BBBS-affiliated provider) so compliance and registry checks run automatically without staff overhead. [convergent]
- Flip the screening sequence: run a structured phone screen before initiating background checks; only advance interview-cleared candidates to the check step. [unique: risk]
- Build a simple stage-tracking spreadsheet from day one — applied / background check initiated / cleared / interview complete / training complete / matched — and use first-cohort conversion data to set realistic capacity commitments for cohort two. [unique: feasibility]
- Set active-match capacity targets at 10–12 for year one in all funder communications, with explicit notation that this is a funnel estimate, not a headcount projection. [convergent]

### Long-term (1+ years)

- Schedule an annual policy review — timed to the board calendar — that asks whether incidents occurred, whether comparable programs updated criteria, and whether state law changed; document the review outcome as a governance record. [unique: feasibility]
- After the first full cohort cycle, assess whether contextual review panel decisions improved or degraded match outcomes, and use that data to refine categorical thresholds before cohort two. [trade-off: whether contextual review should narrow toward categorical over time or expand as evidence accumulates]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- The 40-volunteer expression of interest is not a headcount; plan active matches around 10–12 after funnel attrition, and communicate that figure to funders from the start.
- Calibrate screening depth to supervision structure: higher bar for unsupervised community-location pairs, lower bar for supervised in-church group settings. This preserves pipeline without reducing rigor where it matters most.
- Simple screening architecture is a feature at this scale; use an established background check vendor rather than building a custom system the organization cannot consistently operate.
- Administrative bottleneck risk is real: resolve staff capacity for the screening workload before launch, either with a dedicated coordinator or a formal agreement with church admin staff.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility case for a documented contextual review panel as a workable, legally defensible middle path between categorical over-screening and under-screening. A risk-oriented counterpoint notes that the panel's written rationale is discoverable and may amplify liability rather than contain it. The trade-off turns on whether legal counsel can draft criteria language that withstands adversarial scrutiny and whether insurance coverage for borderline approvals is confirmed before any contextual decision is made.
- A feasibility case for conducting the interview after the background check to reduce the number of people who complete a costly check before basic suitability is assessed. A risk-oriented counterpoint notes that background-check-first creates a false completion signal that suppresses more probing interview questions. Flipping the order improves both safety and cost.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- Insurance exclusion for borderline approvals: many volunteer liability policies explicitly exclude harm by volunteers approved over standard criteria. This must be confirmed in writing before any contextual review policy goes live. [unique: risk]
- Self-disclosure gap for expunged records: the program's written policy needs to state explicitly whether it relies on self-disclosure for expunged records and what the non-disclosure consequence is. Treating expungement as a clean slate without addressing this gap leaves a visible hole. [unique: risk]
- Low-friction application timing: volunteers are most motivated immediately after raising their hand. A multi-week lag between expressed interest and application submission is where the funnel loses the most recoverable attrition. [unique: feasibility]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- Equity/access/distributional: neither lens examined whether the screening criteria disproportionately disqualify volunteers from the same community as the mentees, particularly for old non-violent offenses common in high-poverty urban neighborhoods. A community member with a 15-year-old drug conviction who grew up in the same neighborhood and has demonstrated stability may be the most effective mentor for a referred teen. A screening policy calibrated to middle-class volunteer profiles could structurally exclude exactly that person.
- Relational/social: neither lens addressed how the screening process itself shapes volunteer motivation. Volunteers referred through a faith community carry social expectations. A heavy or bureaucratic screening experience may signal distrust and cool commitment in ways that a clean background check rate will not reveal. The tone and communication of the screening process (how rejection is communicated, how long waits are explained) is a program design question neither lens treated as such.
