# Summary: Matching, Power, and Youth Agency

## Executive Summary

The cluster's most important tension is not whether to include youth voice in matching and safety design. Both lenses endorse it. The real question is an inverted one: a feasibility-oriented view treats youth advisory panels, preference forms, and structured intake conversations as the practical mechanism for giving teens genuine agency. A risk-oriented counterpoint flags that these same mechanisms systematically attract and represent only the teens with enough social capital and institutional comfort to participate in organized processes. The teens most likely to experience coercion, exploitation, or poor matching have the deepest trauma histories and the most distrust of systems. They are the least likely to show up to a panel, fill out a form honestly, or use a text line to request a rematch. The co-design process may produce legitimacy without protection where it is most needed.

Both lenses converge on two high-confidence points: mentor reliability and follow-through outpredict demographic similarity as retention variables, and treating rematch requests as success indicators rather than failures is operationally necessary. A feasibility-oriented case makes these points constructively. Build a four-week provisional match, give teens a confidential text line to request rematch, and target 10–15% early match adjustment as a healthy program signal. The risk-oriented counterpoint does not dispute any of this but raises the structural floor. If the only exit route is a formal escalation process, most teens will disengage silently rather than use it, producing dropout statistics that look like low motivation rather than bad match design.

On power dynamics for court-involved youth, the two lenses land at the same destination: a written firewall with JJ partners specifying that participation status will not be reported. But they arrive from opposite directions. A feasibility view frames this as a negotiable logistics item to finalize before accepting referrals. A risk-oriented view frames it as the difference between a voluntary program and a legal fiction. Even with the firewall in place, the structural power asymmetry between an adult volunteer and a referred teen cannot be dissolved by warmth or "non-judgmental" framing. Teens deserve honest, age-appropriate information about what the actual power structure is.

## Key Themes

### Mentor Reliability Over Demographic Match

Both lenses converge on this. Research from programs like BBBS shows that mentor commitment and follow-through outpredict demographic similarity, shared hobbies, and even race concordance as 12-month retention variables. A feasibility-oriented view translates this into a practical intake question for returning mentors: "What was the hardest moment in your last match, and how did you handle it?" Add a four-week provisional match period that surfaces mismatches early. A risk-oriented view notes that optimizing for surface-level similarity is often a staffing-efficiency choice disguised as a best-practice claim. A matching rubric weighted toward interests may screen out the most reliable volunteers who happen to share nothing obvious with the teen.

### The Rematch Pathway as a Structural Test of Youth Agency

Both lenses agree that a zero rematch rate is a failure signal, not a success signal. A feasibility-oriented view proposes a named confidential text channel that lets teens signal "this isn't working" without explanation, with the coordinator managing the mentor-side conversation. A risk-oriented counterpoint sharpens the stakes. When match dissolution requires a formal escalation event (meeting with staff, a form, an explanation), teens who have learned that voicing complaints leads to consequences will silently disengage rather than use it. The asymmetry of a bad match falls on the teen, not the mentor. The mentor returns to their regular life, while the teen may internalize the failure as evidence they are unlovable or not worth investment.

### "Voluntary" for Court-Involved Youth Requires a Written Firewall, Not Just Good Intentions

Both lenses converge: a referral pathway that allows case workers to know participation status (even implicitly) converts a voluntary program into a coercive one. A feasibility-oriented case treats this as a pre-launch logistics item. Negotiate the written non-reporting agreement with JJ partners before accepting any referrals. Hold intake at the church rather than institutional settings, and build opt-out check-ins at 60 and 120 days. A risk-oriented view raises the structural point that warmth and good intentions leave the power asymmetry untouched. The mentor still has mandatory reporting obligations, still has access to staff if the relationship becomes difficult, and faces essentially no consequences if it ends. Teens deserve honest language about who reports to whom, not the framing of a friendship or equal partnership.

### Drift Toward Easy Cases is a Metrics and Staffing Design Problem

Both lenses agree the drift is real and structural. If program success is measured by aggregate retention rates without breaking them down by referral source, the drift will be invisible until it has already happened. A feasibility-oriented view proposes a concrete threshold: if JJ-referred teens show a retention rate more than 15 percentage points below school-referred teens at 6 months, that triggers a program review. It also recommends designating one coordinator as the high-needs specialist rather than distributing complex matches evenly. The risk-oriented counterpoint notes that well-intentioned training on "difficult cases" can backfire by priming mentors to scan for expected warning signs in JJ-referred teens, producing the difficulty they were trained to anticipate.

### Youth-Designed Safety Protocols are Most Valuable When the Design Process Has Its Own Power Audit

A feasibility-oriented case argues that compensating five to seven teens ($300–400 within the existing budget) to review the draft boundary framework and then treating their output as the primary document rather than a supplement is both affordable and operationally superior to adult-designed compliance checklists. A risk-oriented counterpoint notes two structural risks: the teens available to participate in an organized panel are not the teens most likely to experience boundary violations, and the staff facilitator holds enormous agenda-setting power that can quietly steer the panel away from conclusions inconvenient for the organization. Both risks argue for supplementing the panel with anonymous input mechanisms and an external facilitator with youth participatory design experience.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)

- Build a two-column referral-source tracking field into the intake spreadsheet from day one (school-referred vs. JJ-referred), and set an explicit 6-month review trigger if retention rates diverge by more than 15 percentage points. [convergent]
- Negotiate a written non-reporting agreement with juvenile justice partners — specifying that participation status, attendance, and program engagement will not be shared with case workers or used in case reviews — before accepting any JJ referrals. Communicate this commitment to teens and families explicitly at intake. [convergent]
- Create a confidential text line for teens to request rematch without explanation; train coordinators that 10–15% early match adjustment requests in the first 60 days is a healthy program signal, not a failure indicator. [convergent]
- Before finalizing the matching intake instrument, run at least two unstructured focus groups with teens from the target population to generate verbatim teen language for preference menus — not staff-translated summaries. [unique: risk]

### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Run a four-week provisional match period with a 30-day check-in before confirming permanent pairings; document the matching rubric explicitly enough that any trained volunteer can apply it, eliminating single-coordinator dependency. [convergent]
- Recruit and compensate a youth advisory panel (five to seven teens not yet enrolled, $15–20/hour for three sessions) to draft boundary frameworks and meeting-location norms; use their output as the primary safety document with staff legal annotations in a separate section; bring in an external facilitator with youth participatory design experience for the sessions. [trade-off: a feasibility case that this is affordable and produces legitimacy; a risk-oriented counterpoint that panel composition will underrepresent the most vulnerable teens unless supplemented by anonymous written input from non-panel participants]
- Build explicit opt-out check-ins at 60 and 120 days into the standard coordinator workflow for all participants, with JJ-referred teens given private, coordinator-only conversations separate from mentor relationship tracking. [convergent]
- Run a 90-minute referral-source bias session for all mentors before they receive teen profiles — using the two identical profiles (one labeled school, one labeled JJ) scenario to make differential expectations visible without requiring personal admission of bias. [convergent]

### Long-term (1+ years)

- Designate a high-needs specialist coordinator role to manage all JJ-referred matches and any match flagged high-complexity in the first 30 days, rather than distributing complex matches evenly across coordinator caseloads. [feasibility: one person's partial focus at current scale; risk-oriented note: separating intake/matching from ongoing support prevents the coordinator from managing toward stability at the cost of harder matches]
- Design an explicit relationship-closure protocol that begins conversation about future contact and transition well before the formal program period ends — especially critical for youth with histories of repeated adult departures. [unique: risk]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- Mentor reliability and follow-through outpredict demographic similarity and shared interests as 12-month retention variables. Weight intake accordingly.
- A zero rematch rate signals teens have no real exit options, not that all matches are working.
- "Voluntary" for court-involved youth requires a written non-reporting firewall with JJ partners, negotiated before the first referral is accepted.
- Breaking down retention metrics by referral source from month one is the minimum required to detect drift toward easier cases.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility case treats the youth advisory panel as the primary mechanism for centering teen voice in safety design. A risk-oriented counterpoint: the teens most likely to experience harm are systematically underrepresented in organized co-design processes, so panel legitimacy must be supplemented by anonymous input channels targeting non-participating youth.
- A feasibility case for holding intake at the church with low-institutional-cue logistics as sufficient to reduce the coercion signal for JJ-referred teens. A risk-oriented counterpoint: structural power asymmetries (mandatory reporting obligations, mentor access to staff, asymmetric exit consequences) persist regardless of setting and require honest disclosure to teens rather than a relational framing that implies a partnership that does not structurally exist.
- A feasibility case for using mentor training on trauma and difficult behaviors to prepare mentors for higher-need teens. A risk-oriented counterpoint: leading training with risk profiles primes mentors to confirm predictions, and individual youth circumstances should be disclosed only to specifically matched mentors who have teen consent.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- The multi-year attachment dependency risk: a teen who builds genuine trust over 12+ months with a mentor who then exits the program may experience net harm by reopening prior attachment wounds. Design the relationship's ending as carefully as its beginning. [unique: risk]
- Travel time as a hidden retention variable: any pairing requiring more than 20 minutes of teen travel should be flagged at intake. Prioritize school-adjacent or on-site alternatives before confirming the match. [unique: feasibility]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):
- *Emotional/phenomenological*: Neither lens examined what the matching and onboarding process feels like from the inside for a teen who has already been through multiple institutional referrals. The subjective experience of being "assessed," profiled, and assigned (even via a teen-preference form) may carry its own relational charge that shapes whether the teen enters the relationship with genuine openness or practiced performance of what adults want to hear.
- *Lifecycle/temporal-stage*: Neither lens addressed how the matching calculus changes as teens age through the 13–17 range. A 13-year-old's developmental needs, relational capacities, and tolerance for structured programming differ substantially from a 17-year-old approaching adulthood. Matching criteria and mentor preparation may need to be age-tiered rather than applied uniformly across the full range.
