# Summary: Program Identity, Framing, and Hidden Assumptions

## Executive Summary

The organizing tension across this cluster is that the faith organization's 20-year neighborhood presence operates as both the program's core asset and its primary filter. It is trusted by families who already trust it, and invisible or actively off-putting to families who don't. A feasibility-oriented view holds that this existing trust infrastructure is precisely what makes the program buildable on a $15K seed budget. It supplies volunteer pipeline, meeting space, and institutional relationships with schools and juvenile justice that a startup program couldn't otherwise establish. A risk-oriented counterpoint is that the program's current design (mediated entirely through the church building, the congregational volunteer pool, and faith-affiliated administrative staff) treats that institutional trust as universal when it is demonstrably selective. Both lenses point at the same element from opposite directions. The 20-year presence is simultaneously named as the solution vector (leverage what already works) and the risk source (the institution's identity quietly shapes who enrolls). This inverted framing represents the highest-stakes design question in this cluster.

Beyond the institutional identity question, both lenses converged on deficit framing as a high-confidence problem requiring structural intervention, not just cultural awareness. The "at-risk" label travels into the match before trust is built through intake forms, referral documentation, and mentor orientation. It shapes how mentors perceive their mentees from the first meeting. Both perspectives recommended making teens' own goals the operative frame at intake, stripping referral source from mentor-facing materials, and establishing a staff language protocol with specific phrase substitutions. The lenses diverged on formal referral pipeline design. A feasibility-oriented view argues for soft caps on any single referral source and community-level outreach cards as a low-cost expansion. A risk-oriented counterpoint presses harder on whether the entire pipeline is pre-filtering toward teens whose families are already somewhat institutionally engaged, and whether the program is reaching highest-need youth or simply the most accessible subset.

The volunteer motivation question produced a similar convergence-then-divergence structure. Both perspectives agreed that the 40 interested adults represent a waiting room, not a vetted pool, and that misaligned motivations are most dangerous precisely when a match is most at risk. A feasibility-oriented frame focuses on practical screening tools: a short intake survey, a mutual fit conversation, a graceful 90-day exit protocol. A risk-oriented lens pushes a harder point. The weekly-meeting-for-a-year commitment structure may be self-selecting for a volunteer demographic that is helpful and well-intentioned but structurally distant from the teens' own communities, limiting relational resonance where it matters most.

## Key Themes

### Deficit Framing Requires Structural, Not Just Cultural, Intervention

Both lenses converged that the "at-risk" label is an operational problem, not merely a language sensitivity issue. Intake forms, mentor orientation scripts, and referral documentation all transmit the deficit frame into the relationship before trust is built. The feasibility-oriented view identified specific, zero-cost fixes: a staff language cheat sheet, a teen asset-mapping activity in session one, a structured mentor script for "why am I in this program?" The risk-oriented view flagged that even a well-launched vocabulary will drift back within 12 months. A designated language-audit mechanism and supervision practices that surface tacit staff categorizations are needed to prevent this drift.

### The Referral Pipeline Is Pre-Filtering, and Speed Pressure Will Make It Worse

Both lenses flagged that school counselors and juvenile justice systems identify teens after something has already gone wrong, meaning the most isolated, quietly struggling teens may never surface in either channel. The risk-oriented view pushed hardest on this point. The $15K seed budget creates utilization pressure that will favor easy, paperwork-complete referrals over slower informal outreach. The program should set a deliberate cap (no more than 70% from formal channels) before launch, not after. The feasibility-oriented view offered the operational path forward: 30-minute conversations with school counselors to map coverage gaps, low-cost referral cards to two or three community anchors, and referral source tracked as a data field from day one. This enables a year-one analysis of pipeline effectiveness.

### The Faith Identity Is a Trust Asset With an Unacknowledged Admission Filter

The faith organization's presence opens doors with families who already trust it and quietly closes them with families who don't. This includes secular households, different-faith families, or families for whom the building itself carries social risk in a dense urban neighborhood. Both lenses agreed that a written non-discrimination policy, separated program branding, and at least one off-site meeting option should be in place before the first referral is accepted. The risk-oriented view went further. The program needs a written policy on values-tension scenarios (a teen's identity or family structure in tension with the host institution's values) before the first match is formed. Those cases will arrive unpredictably, and ad hoc responses will damage trust faster than any other failure mode.

### Volunteer Motivations Are Most Dangerous When the Match Is Most At Risk

Both lenses converged that faith-based mentorship contexts draw volunteers motivated by visible transformation, a misalignment that becomes harmful precisely when a teen is in crisis, disengaged, or simply unavailable. A feasibility-oriented frame pointed to practical mitigation: a structured intake survey, an explicit written commitment document, and a 90-day check-in with a non-stigmatizing exit path. The risk-oriented lens added that the program should pressure-test the full 40-volunteer pool with a specific scenario: "your mentee has missed three meetings and isn't responding; what do you do?" That answer reveals whether a volunteer's motivation will cause them to disengage or stay. The program needs to know this before matches are formed.

### The Organization's 20-Year Track Record Is Fragile and Partially Unmined

Both lenses converged that the relational equity built over 20 years is concentrated in a small number of long-tenured staff and congregants, not institutionalized in the program design. The feasibility-oriented view identified a concrete first step: a 90-minute facilitated conversation with three to five long-tenured leaders to surface informal mentors already operating in the community, successful prior relationships, and prior failures worth designing against. The risk-oriented view emphasized that the people with genuine relational gifts may not be among the 40 who raised their hand for a formal program. People who mentor through relationship tend to find formal programs bureaucratic. A training curriculum built on generic best practices rather than what proven informal mentors already do risks replacing working instinct with institutional bureaucracy.

## Recommended Actions

### Immediate (0-3 months)

- Before the first cohort, remove "at-risk" and all deficit-framing language from every youth-facing document; create a one-page staff language cheat sheet with specific substitute phrases and approved mentor scripts for common intake questions. [convergent]
- Strip referral source from the mentor-facing intake packet; retain it in a separate administrative record accessible only to the program director for legal compliance. [convergent]
- Write a non-discrimination policy (one page, attorney-reviewed) stating that program participation is open regardless of religious background, family structure, or identity, and a two-sentence staff script for "do I have to be religious?" before accepting the first referral. [convergent]
- Conduct a 90-minute facilitated conversation with three to five long-tenured staff or congregation leaders to identify informal neighborhood mentors, prior program successes and failures, and families with different religious backgrounds who have been successfully served — before finalizing any program design elements. [convergent]
- Run a structured 10-question volunteer intake survey and build a written commitment document (year, weekly meetings, quarterly check-ins) that forces self-selection before the matching process begins. [convergent]

### Near-term (3-12 months)

- Set a soft cap (no more than 70% of participants from formal referral channels) and require staff to cultivate at least two informal community referral points (a barbershop owner, a youth sports coach, a community anchor) before the program opens enrollment. [trade-off: a feasibility-oriented case that a 50% cap is achievable without disrupting the formal pipeline; a risk-oriented counterpoint that 70% still leaves the program structurally shaped by institution-filtered teens]
- Develop a separate program name, logo, and outreach materials that do not feature the church's name prominently, and pilot distribution through at least two secular channels before the first cohort to test whether institutional identity is narrowing reach. [convergent]
- Build a structured mutual-fit conversation into the matching process — a 30-minute facilitated meeting between prospective mentor and teen, with staff observing and making the final call — before scaling to the full 40-volunteer pool. [convergent]
- Write a values-tension policy (one page, reviewed by leadership) specifying how the program handles cases where a teen's identity or family structure is in tension with the host institution's values, before the first match is formed. [unique: risk]
- Add `referral_source` as a tracked field in program data from launch so a year-one analysis can determine whether referral channel correlates with match durability or outcome quality. [unique: feasibility]

### Long-term (1+ years)

- Negotiate a written memorandum of understanding with the highest-referral school defining data-sharing boundaries, referral criteria, and each party's obligations — formalizing the intermediary role in a document that survives individual relationship changes. [convergent]
- After year one, conduct a deliberate retrospective on which volunteer motivations and referral sources produced the most durable matches, and use those findings to revise screening criteria, commitment structures, and referral channel mix for the second cohort. [convergent]

## Key Considerations

**High-confidence items** (both lenses agreed):
- The "at-risk" label is an operational problem, not just a language issue — it travels into the match through intake forms and referral documentation and shapes mentor perception before trust is built.
- The 40-volunteer pool is unvetted; misaligned motivations are most damaging when matches are most at risk, and screening must happen before matches are formed.
- The organization's 20-year relational equity is fragile, concentrated in a small number of people, and needs to be deliberately audited and documented before launch.
- A non-discrimination policy and a written values-tension protocol must exist before the first referral is accepted — ad hoc responses to hard cases will damage trust faster than any other failure mode.

**Trade-offs** (the two lenses disagreed):
- A feasibility-oriented case for setting a soft referral-source cap (50%) as an achievable pipeline diversification measure that keeps formal channels as the operational backbone; a risk-oriented counterpoint that even a 50% cap may still be pre-filtering toward institutionally engaged families and that the program should ask whether it is reaching highest-need youth or the most accessible subset.
- A feasibility-oriented case for leveraging the church's brand recognition and built trust as the lowest-cost path to volunteer recruitment and family outreach; a risk-oriented counterpoint that the current design — building-mediated, congregationally staffed, faith-branded — makes no version of the program legible to families for whom the institution is a barrier, and that functional invisibility for those families is worth piloting as a satellite model before assuming reach.
- A feasibility-oriented case for piloting one co-designed program element (the matching questionnaire or first-session activity) as an achievable step toward teen agency; a risk-oriented counterpoint that any co-creation framing requires distributing actual decision-making authority over match, agenda, and metrics — and that if the program always defers to the adult when teen and mentor goals conflict, it is not a co-creation model.

**Blind-spot flags** (only one lens raised):
- Language drift over the program lifetime — even a well-launched vocabulary will revert within 12 months without a designated quarterly language-audit mechanism and supervision practices that surface tacit staff categorizations. [unique: risk]
- The weekly-in-person-for-a-year commitment structure may be self-selecting for a volunteer demographic that is helpful but structurally distant from teens' own communities, limiting relational resonance. [unique: risk]
- Tracking `referral_source` as a data field from day one costs 30 minutes of spreadsheet setup and enables a year-one analysis of whether referral channel shapes match durability — without it, adjustments will be based on intuition rather than evidence. [unique: feasibility]
- Using long-tenured congregation members to personally hand-deliver consent packets to families in their social networks leverages existing trust at zero cost to accelerate early enrollment. [unique: feasibility]

**Neither-lens gaps** (what a different angle would have surfaced):

- **Emotional/phenomenological.** The teen's experience of being identified and referred. Neither lens addressed what it feels like to a 13-17 year-old to be told by a school counselor or juvenile justice officer that they've been referred to a mentorship program. That moment of identification carries its own stigma load before the program's framing choices ever reach the teen. How that referral conversation is conducted (by whom, with what language, with what framing of the teen's agency in accepting) is a program design question neither the risk nor the feasibility view touched.
- **Equity/distributional.** Whose families can afford the participation requirements. Both lenses focused on volunteer demographics and institutional reach, but neither examined whether the program's participation structure (weekly meetings, parental/guardian consent process, enrollment paperwork) creates barriers for the specific families it most needs to reach: undocumented families, single-parent households managing multiple jobs, families in active legal proceedings for whom any institutional paperwork carries risk.
- **Lifecycle/temporal.** What happens after the one-year commitment ends. Neither lens addressed match closure: what happens to teens and mentors when the formal year concludes, how the program handles cases where a relationship has become genuinely important to both parties, and whether ending the formal structure at 12 months creates a loss experience for teens who may have had few stable adult relationships. This design question carries equity implications that neither the risk nor the feasibility framing surfaced.
