# Brainstorming Questions from The Audience Advocate: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## How Teens Will Actually Experience This Program

1. **First impression and emotional entry**: When a 15-year-old arrives for their first mentor meeting — referred through juvenile justice, with no say in being here — what does that moment feel like from their perspective, and what would need to be true about the environment for them to walk out willing to come back?

2. **Hidden barriers to showing up**: Beyond scheduling logistics, what emotional or social barriers might prevent teens from consistently attending — fear of being seen as needing help, stigma from peers, distrust of adults in authority — and how might the program design inadvertently reinforce rather than reduce those barriers?

3. **Agency and voice in the match**: The people we serve might experience a mentor assignment as something done *to* them rather than *with* them — how can teens meaningfully shape who they're paired with, and what happens when the match feels wrong to them?

---

## Trust, Power, and the Mentor Relationship

4. **The trust gap with faith-based framing**: From the community's perspective, a faith-based organization carries strong associations for some teens and families — some welcoming, others alienating or coercive — how does the program's faith identity show up in day-to-day interactions, and what's the honest answer for families who aren't part of that faith tradition?

5. **Adult authority and institutional trauma**: Many teens referred through juvenile justice have experienced adult authority as punitive and unreliable — what does the mentor training need to address about how these teens may read even well-intentioned adults, and how does the program create space for teens to define what a good relationship looks like on their own terms?

6. **What teens say they want vs. what they actually need**: Research on youth mentorship consistently shows teens report wanting "someone who listens" but disengage when mentors shift toward goal-setting and accountability — how does this program design for the relationship teens want while still achieving the outcomes funders expect?

---

## Family and Guardian Realities

7. **Guardians as gatekeepers or partners**: Parental/guardian consent is a legal requirement, but from the community's perspective, how the organization engages families — with respect or with paperwork — determines whether families become genuine allies or quiet resisters who subtly discourage their teen's participation?

8. **Navigating complex and non-traditional households**: A significant portion of at-risk teens live in households with grandparents, older siblings, foster parents, or rotating guardians — how does the consent and communication framework account for families that don't fit the assumed "two parents" model?

---

## Accessibility and Structural Barriers

9. **Transportation as a silent dropout factor**: Weekly meetings require consistent transportation in a high-poverty urban neighborhood — what assumption is the program making about how teens will get there, and what happens when a teen misses three sessions in a row not because they've disengaged but because they couldn't get a ride?

10. **Digital equity in program communication**: If the program relies on apps, email, or online portals for scheduling, check-ins, or reporting, who gets left out — teens without reliable smartphones, families without consistent internet access — and is the organization designing for its median participant or its most resourced one?

---

## Dignity, Data, and the Feeling of Being Studied

11. **Outcome measurement as surveillance**: From the teen's perspective, being asked to fill out surveys about their school attendance, family relationships, and mental health — especially when referred through juvenile justice — may feel less like "we care how you're doing" and more like "we're tracking you for someone else." How does the program design data collection so teens understand who sees it, why it's gathered, and what they gain from sharing honestly?

12. **Who defines success**: The program's outcomes are largely defined by the organization and its funder — school attendance, reduced recidivism, goal completion — but what do the teens themselves consider a successful year of mentorship, and is there a mechanism for their definition to shape how the program evolves?

13. **The dignity of the referral label**: Teens who enter through school counselors or juvenile justice carry the implicit label "at-risk" from day one — how does the program culture, staff language, and mentor training ensure that label never becomes how a teen is treated, and what's the alternative framing the program offers to teens about who they are and why they're here?
