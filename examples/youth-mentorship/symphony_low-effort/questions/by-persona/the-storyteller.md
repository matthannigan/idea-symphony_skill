# Brainstorming Questions from The Storyteller: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## The Day a Match Changes Everything

1. **The first meeting moment**: Imagine Marcus, 15, referred through juvenile justice, sitting across from a volunteer mentor for the very first time in the church meeting room — what does that scene look, sound, and feel like, and what structures or rituals could the program design into that first encounter so the relationship begins with safety and trust rather than awkwardness and surveillance?

2. **The turning-point conversation**: Picture a mentor six months in who notices their mentee's chronic absenteeism shifting — slowly, then suddenly — toward engagement. What series of small interactions, boundary-respecting check-ins, and consistent show-ups led to that invisible threshold being crossed, and how does the program's design make space for that slow-burn transformation rather than optimizing only for measurable milestones?

3. **When the story gets hard**: Walk through what happens the week a mentor suspects their teen is experiencing something dangerous at home — the moment they recognize the sign, the internal conflict between confidentiality and duty, and the first phone call they make. How does a mentor training curriculum prepare someone emotionally, not just procedurally, for that chapter of the story?

---

## The Volunteer's Own Journey

4. **The moment a volunteer almost quit**: Imagine Deb, a 42-year-old congregant who signed up full of hope but hit a wall at month four — her mentee stopped returning texts, showed up visibly withdrawn, and Deb felt helpless and unqualified. What does the program's support structure look like in that exact moment, and how does someone go from "I don't know if I'm helping" to "I understand this is part of the work"?

5. **The weight of the background check gate**: Picture two volunteers: one who breezes through screening feeling confirmed and welcomed, and one who is disqualified for a past offense they've long moved past. How does the program write and communicate its disqualifying criteria in a way that feels principled rather than punitive, and what does the organization say to the second person to honor their willingness to serve while protecting the youth they sought to help?

---

## The Teen's Experience of Being Known

6. **The privacy paradox for court-referred youth**: Imagine Jordan, 16, referred through the juvenile justice system, sitting in the program intake meeting while an adult they've just met fills out a form with information from their case file. What does trust feel like for Jordan in that moment — and how does the program design consent conversations, data handling, and information-sharing boundaries so that Jordan experiences being protected rather than being processed?

7. **The end of the year — and what comes next**: Let me walk you through the last meeting of the first year: a mentor and teen who've built something real, now facing the uncertainty of whether the program continues, whether the relationship outlasts the formal commitment, and what "graduation" even means for a kid whose life is still unfolding. How does the program design the transition out of a formal match in a way that honors what was built without creating another abandonment in a life that may already hold too many?
