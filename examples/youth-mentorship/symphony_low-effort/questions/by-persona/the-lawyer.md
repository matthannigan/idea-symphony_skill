# Brainstorming Questions from The Lawyer: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Background Check Architecture and Disqualifying Criteria

1. **Background check scope and standards**: What specific background check tiers should the program require — and under what legal authority? Most states mandate FBI fingerprint checks and state sex offender registry screening for volunteers working with minors in youth-serving organizations, but the disqualifying offense list varies by state; does the program's jurisdiction impose a categorical bar for any felony involving minors, or only certain offense classes, and who bears legal responsibility if a volunteer with an undisclosed offense causes harm after a compliant check?

2. **Juvenile justice referral pipeline and record access**: When teens are referred through juvenile justice channels, what legal framework governs whether program staff can be informed of a youth's offense history? Juvenile records are typically sealed under state juvenile code and accessible only to enumerated parties — does the organization have a legal basis to receive that information, and if not, how does the program design appropriate supervision and mentor-matching criteria without it?

---

## Mandatory Reporting Obligations and Training Standards

3. **Mandatory reporter classification for volunteer mentors**: Under most state child abuse and neglect reporting statutes, whether an adult volunteer in a faith-based youth program qualifies as a mandatory reporter depends on whether the statute extends to "persons responsible for the care of a child" or to specific licensed categories — which classification applies here, and if mentors are not statutory mandatory reporters, what organizational policy should fill the gap to ensure consistent reporting practice and limit organizational liability?

4. **Reporting chain design and documentation**: If a mentor receives a disclosure of abuse or neglect during a one-on-one meeting, what is the legally defensible reporting chain the organization must establish — specifically, does a report to an internal supervisor satisfy the reporting obligation under state law, or must the mentor make the report directly to the state child protective services hotline? What documentation of training completion should the organization retain to demonstrate due diligence if a failure-to-report allegation arises?

---

## Liability Exposure, Insurance, and Organizational Protections

5. **Liability insurance minimums and coverage gaps**: A one-on-one youth mentorship program involving minors typically requires general liability coverage in the $1–2M per-occurrence range, plus an umbrella policy; but does the program's faith-based organizational structure create coverage gaps — specifically, are volunteer mentors covered as agents of the organization under the existing policy, or does each mentor need individual acknowledgment of coverage, and is there a sexual abuse and molestation (SAM) rider, which many standard CGL policies exclude by endorsement?

6. **Volunteer protection statutes and their limits**: Most states have enacted volunteer protection acts (modeled on the federal Volunteer Protection Act of 1997, 42 U.S.C. § 14503) that limit personal liability for volunteers acting in good faith within the scope of their role — but these protections do not extend to gross negligence, willful misconduct, or crimes; how should the program's intake and training documentation be structured to establish the scope of volunteer roles clearly enough that the organizational shield is meaningful when conduct falls outside it?

---

## Consent Frameworks and Guardian Authority

7. **Parental consent complexity for juvenile-justice-referred youth**: For teens referred through juvenile justice channels, the legal authority to provide parental consent for program participation may rest with a biological parent, a foster parent, a legal guardian, or a probation officer depending on the youth's custody and supervision status — what consent framework does the program need to map out, and what happens when a youth's custodial arrangement changes mid-program (e.g., placement change, custody modification) that might affect the consenting party's authority?

8. **Ongoing consent and withdrawal rights**: A one-time enrollment consent form does not address ongoing consent obligations — particularly for activities beyond the standard weekly meeting (off-site outings, photographing for program materials, sharing outcome data with the funding foundation); what layered consent structure should the organization adopt, and for teens who turn 18 mid-program, at what point does the youth's own consent supersede or replace the guardian's?

---

## Data Privacy for Minors and Juvenile Records

9. **Minors' data collection and privacy architecture**: The program will collect sensitive data about participants — including referral source, school attendance records, and potentially behavioral or mental health context — from multiple institutional partners; what data minimization and access-control policies does the organization need to implement as best practice inspired by FERPA (which governs school records shared by partner schools) and as direct obligation under applicable state minor-privacy statutes, and who within the organization may lawfully access juvenile justice referral information without becoming a party to a confidentiality violation?
