# Brainstorming Questions from The Visionary: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Reimagining the Relationship Itself

1. **Beyond guidance to co-creation**: What if we completely reimagined the mentor-mentee relationship not as adult-to-youth knowledge transfer, but as a genuine mutual discovery partnership — where teens are positioned as co-investigators of their own futures, and mentors are explicitly there to learn something too? How would the program structure, matching criteria, and success metrics change if both parties were required to bring an unresolved question they couldn't answer alone?

2. **Community as the real mentor**: Imagine a world where the "program" is not a series of weekly one-on-one meetings but a living ecosystem — where the congregation, the neighborhood, the schools, and the teens themselves form a web of ongoing relationship that has no single point of failure and no moment when a match "ends." What would it look like to design for this kind of distributed mentorship from the beginning, so that the formal pairing is a catalyst rather than the container?

---

## Inverting Power and Expertise

3. **Teens as the safety architects**: What if the teens themselves — not compliance officers, not legal counsel — were the primary designers of the safety and consent protocols? Imagine convening a youth advisory panel to draft the boundary framework, the meeting-location norms, and the escalation procedures. What new forms of trust and genuine protection might emerge that no adult-designed policy has ever produced?

4. **Rethinking "at-risk" as a category**: What if we completely eliminated the label "at-risk" from the program's internal language and infrastructure — not as a cosmetic change, but as a structural one? If the program were designed around the premise that every teen in a high-poverty urban neighborhood is navigating extraordinary complexity with underrecognized capability, how would matching criteria, training curriculum, and outcome measurement need to be reinvented from scratch?

5. **Mentors as students of the neighborhood**: What if mentor training started not with background checks and mandatory reporting procedures, but with a period of genuine apprenticeship to the community — where volunteers spent time learning from teens, families, and neighborhood institutions before any pairing occurred? How might this inversion change who shows up as a volunteer, who stays, and what kinds of relationships actually form?

---

## Designing for Emergence Over Time

6. **A program that grows a generation, not a cohort**: Imagine if this program were designed with an explicit 20-year horizon — not as an annual intake of matched pairs, but as a living institution that a teen who ages out of mentorship eventually re-enters as a mentor, then as a program elder, then as a community trustee. What would need to be true about the program's structure, governance, and culture in year one to make that kind of intergenerational emergence possible?

7. **What if outcomes weren't measured, but witnessed?**: What if we completely reimagined how success is understood in this program — not through attendance rates, GPA change, or recidivism reduction, but through a practice of collective witnessing where participants, families, and community members narrate what they have seen change over time? What new forms of accountability and program learning might emerge if the measurement apparatus was replaced with a structured storytelling practice rooted in the faith community's own traditions of testimony?
