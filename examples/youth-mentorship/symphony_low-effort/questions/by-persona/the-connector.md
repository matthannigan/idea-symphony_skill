# Brainstorming Questions from The Connector: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Bridging Institutional Gatekeeping Across Systems

1. **Parallel screening architectures**: Background check and disqualifying criteria systems exist in at least three adjacent domains — licensed foster care, Big Brothers Big Sisters, and gig-economy trust-and-safety platforms like Uber — each of which solved the same core tension: how do you screen at scale without creating so much friction that you lose your volunteer pipeline? What can the faith org borrow from how gig platforms use tiered, risk-weighted screening (rather than binary pass/fail) to calibrate who can work unsupervised vs. who needs supervision, and how might adopting that frame change which volunteers you can accept?

2. **Consent architecture as an interface design problem**: Juvenile justice referrals and school counselor referrals operate under different consent regimes — FERPA, state juvenile records law, HIPAA-adjacent mental health confidentiality — yet the program must weave a single teen's information through all three systems while keeping parents/guardians informed. There's a structural parallel here with healthcare interoperability: the way a hospital patient portal federates data from multiple siloed EHR systems without exposing raw records. What would it look like to design the program's information flow like an interoperability layer — each institution shares only a defined, minimal data type outward, and the program holds the integration layer — and what would that mean for how consent forms are structured?

---

## Cross-Domain Models for Sustaining Volunteer Relationships

3. **The sponsor/sponsee model from recovery communities**: AA and NA have sustained one-on-one peer relationships at massive scale, with minimal institutional overhead, for decades — a structural feat this program is attempting to replicate. The key design elements are: mutual identification (not charity), a shared framework that gives both parties a script for hard conversations, and a community of practice that holds both the helper and the helped. What would it mean to deliberately borrow these design elements — not the spiritual content — and how would the program's training curriculum change if it treated mentor sustainability the same way recovery communities treat sponsor sustainability?

4. **Firefighter crew cohesion as a model for mentor support**: Research on high-reliability organizations shows that peer cohesion within a small team (not just top-down supervision) is what sustains people through difficult, emotionally taxing work. Firefighter crews eat together, debrief together, and carry shared accountability. Mentors in one-on-one programs typically work in isolation and burn out silently. What if the program deliberately built horizontal cohesion among mentors — small cohorts of 4–6 who share a monthly debrief — and how might that peer infrastructure also serve as an early-warning system for match difficulties that the program coordinator currently can't see?

---

## Structural Interfaces Between the Faith Org and External Systems

5. **The faith org as a "trusted intermediary" — a pattern from immigrant services**: Community Development Financial Institutions and immigrant legal aid organizations occupy a distinctive structural role: they are trusted by a community that distrusts institutions, yet they are also credentialed enough to interface with those institutions. The faith org here holds the same structural position — trusted by families who are wary of schools and juvenile justice — but has likely not made that intermediary role explicit. What governance and communication protocols would the program need to formalize to act as a deliberate trusted intermediary, and what obligations and risks does that role create that the org currently hasn't named?

6. **Insurance and liability as a system-design constraint, not just a compliance task**: Organizations that run adventure therapy programs — ropes courses, wilderness programs — face a structurally identical liability problem: unsupervised adults with minors in uncontrolled environments. They've developed a specific set of institutional designs in response — activity logs, check-in protocols, pre-approved venue lists, incident response trees — that satisfy insurers while not strangling the relationship. There's a parallel here with this program's meeting-location and one-on-one contact policy. What could the program borrow from adventure therapy's operational playbook that would give the insurer sufficient oversight signals without creating so much documentation burden that mentors disengage?

---

## Cross-System Outcome Measurement

7. **The hidden connection between measurement and trust**: Schools report 35% chronic absenteeism, but the teens most likely to benefit from this program are also the most likely to resist being measured — juvenile justice involvement often means a history of being tracked, filed, and labeled. There's a structural parallel here with participatory action research: communities that have been over-studied often respond to outcome measurement as surveillance. The engagement problem and the measurement problem are actually the same problem viewed from different angles — a teen who distrusts being observed will also resist the relationship with a mentor who seems to be evaluating them. What if the program borrowed the "member-defined outcomes" model from youth participatory action research, where teens co-design what counts as progress, and how would that reframe both the measurement instrument and the mentor's role?
