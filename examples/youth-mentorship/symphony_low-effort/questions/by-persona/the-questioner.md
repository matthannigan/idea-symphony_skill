# Brainstorming Questions from The Questioner: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Hidden Assumptions About Who the Program Is For

1. **The "at-risk" frame**: Why do we assume labeling teens as "at-risk" before a relationship even begins is neutral — and what if that framing shapes how mentors perceive and relate to their mentees from day one?
2. **Referral source bias**: What if the teens most in need of mentorship are precisely those who would never be flagged by school counselors or juvenile justice — who else in the community might identify candidates that the formal systems overlook?
3. **Volunteer motivations**: Have we examined what the 40 interested adults actually want from this experience, and how their motivations might diverge from what teens need from the relationship?

---

## The Relationship Architecture

4. **One-on-one as the only model**: What if the assumption that one-on-one mentoring is universally preferable ignores teens who would thrive more in a small peer group with a mentor, especially those who find one-on-one adult attention uncomfortable or culturally unfamiliar?
5. **Year-long commitment framing**: Why do we assume a one-year minimum commitment creates safety and stability rather than pressure — and how might teens experience a formal "commitment" differently from an ongoing relationship that simply continues?
6. **Power and trust dynamics**: What structural features of the program either reinforce or help dissolve the inherent power imbalance between an adult volunteer and a teen referred through juvenile justice, and have we thought through what "voluntary" really means for court-involved youth?

---

## Safety Protocols and Their Unintended Consequences

7. **Surveillance vs. safety tradeoff**: What if the safety protocols we design — mandatory reporting, location logging, regular check-ins — create exactly the environment of surveillance that erodes trust with teens who already distrust institutions?
8. **Who defines a safety concern**: How do we design escalation processes so that mentor discomfort with a teen's choices doesn't get coded as a safety issue, and whose definition of "concern" controls the trigger for intervention?
9. **Faith-based context and disclosure**: What if a teen's personal situation — family composition, identity, beliefs — creates tension with the organization's faith-based values, and how does the program protect the teen's right to be fully themselves in a mentorship relationship hosted by a religious institution?

---

## Measuring What Actually Matters

10. **Outcomes vs. proxies**: Have we interrogated whether the outcomes we plan to measure — attendance, grades, recidivism rates — are actually the outcomes that matter to the teens themselves, or whether they're the metrics that matter to funders and referral partners?
11. **What success looks like at 18 months**: What if a match ends before the one-year mark not because it failed but because the teen no longer needs it — and how does our measurement framework distinguish premature exit from healthy graduation?
12. **The unmeasurable core**: What would we need to believe about relationship quality and human development to accept that a program can be working even when the quantitative indicators don't yet show it — and how do we build that case for the foundation without abandoning rigor?

---

## Organizational Fit and Long-Term Sustainability

13. **What the organization is not**: Given that this faith-based organization has a 20-year community presence but is launching a new program type with $15K in seed funding and no dedicated program staff, what do we assume we know how to do that we have not actually done before — and where are the competency gaps that could quietly undermine the program?
