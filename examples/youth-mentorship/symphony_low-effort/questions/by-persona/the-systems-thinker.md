# Brainstorming Questions from The Systems Thinker: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Feedback Loops in Mentor-Mentee Relationships

1. **Relationship momentum cuts both ways**: Once a mentoring relationship gains traction — regular meetings, growing trust, visible progress — that positive momentum tends to build on itself. But the same dynamic operates in reverse: an awkward early match, a cancelled meeting, a miscommunication about expectations can start a downward spiral that's hard to interrupt once it gains speed. What early signals would indicate whether a pair is in a compounding-positive or compounding-negative dynamic, and how quickly can the program intervene before a struggling match locks in?

2. **Mentor confidence and mentee engagement**: A mentor who feels effective shows up with more energy and creativity; a mentee who feels that energy is more likely to engage openly; that engagement makes the mentor feel more effective. How does the program design reinforce this virtuous cycle in its first 90 days — before most pairs would naturally find their rhythm on their own?

3. **Referral network as a self-reinforcing signal**: School counselors and juvenile justice officers are gatekeepers to the youth this program wants to reach. If early matches go well and staff hear about it, referrals flow more freely and better-fit youth enter the pipeline. If early matches struggle and staff lose confidence, referrals dry up. What does the program do in its first year to ensure the referral network sees and hears the right signals — not just outcomes, but the process quality that leads to outcomes?

---

## Second-Order Consequences of Safety and Compliance Design

4. **Over-engineering safety can hollow out the relationship**: Rigid protocols — mandatory public meeting locations, required chaperones, logged communications — signal distrust between mentor and mentee, which may protect the organization while undermining the one thing that makes mentorship work: a genuine, trusting relationship. What's the second-order effect if teens perceive the program's safety rules as surveillance rather than care? How does the program design protocols that protect without corroding the relational core?

5. **Mandatory reporting as a hidden dynamic**: Training mentors as mandatory reporters is legally and ethically correct, but it changes what teens will and won't share. A teen who learns (directly or through word-of-mouth from peers) that their mentor is obligated to report disclosures may self-censor exactly the material that most needs surfacing. How does the program communicate mandatory reporting obligations in a way that builds rather than erodes trust — and what peer effects should it anticipate as teens talk to each other about what "can" and "can't" be said?

6. **Liability frameworks that shape program scope**: Legal protections are often designed defensively, restricting activities that carry any risk — which inadvertently limits the kinds of activities that build the most meaningful mentor-mentee bonds (sports, outings, shared projects). Over time, a highly restricted program may technically continue while losing the relational depth that justifies its existence. What activities does the program explicitly protect space for, and how does legal review support rather than narrow the program's relational ambition?

---

## Delays, Burnout, and the Long-Cycle Volunteer Problem

7. **The 12-month commitment as a pressure vessel**: Asking 40 volunteers for a year-long commitment creates a natural endpoint — and at the 6-9 month mark, when the novelty has worn off and outcomes aren't yet visible, many mentors hit a motivational trough. That trough coincides with a period when teens (who've invested emotionally in the relationship) are most vulnerable to abandonment. What structures does the program put in place at the 6-month mark specifically, knowing that this is when the system is most likely to produce the harm it was designed to prevent?

8. **Measuring outcomes on the wrong timescale**: Youth development outcomes — school attendance, reduced justice involvement, sustained behavioral change — often take years to manifest, well past the funding cycle or program evaluation window. If the program measures what's measurable at 12 months, it may optimize for visible short-term signals (mentor satisfaction, meeting frequency) while missing whether the underlying goal is being achieved. How does the program build an outcome framework that's honest about the mismatch between what funders want to see and when real results become legible?

9. **Volunteer pipeline erosion over time**: The initial pool of 40 interested adults is a one-time cohort. If the program relies on that congregation-driven surge without building a self-replenishing recruitment system, it will face a structural shortage by year two regardless of how well year one goes. What does the program design to make volunteer recruitment a continuous, embedded function rather than a launch-phase event?

---

## Unintended Consequences of Matching and Selection

10. **Matching criteria that systematically exclude the hardest cases**: Any matching algorithm that optimizes for relationship quality will tend to route the most engaged mentors to the most responsive teens — because that's what produces visible success. Teens with the deepest needs (most trauma, most distrust of adults, most erratic behavior) may end up matched with less experienced mentors or in lower-priority slots. How does the program deliberately counteract this drift toward serving the "easy" cases more richly than the difficult ones?

11. **The juvenile justice referral as a stigma signal**: Teens referred through juvenile justice channels carry a label that mentors may consciously or unconsciously respond to differently than teens referred by school counselors. That differential response can activate a self-fulfilling dynamic — mentors who expect more difficulty may create more difficulty. How does the program structure its intake and matching process to prevent referral source from functioning as a hidden bias variable in how mentors show up?

---

## Program-Community System Dynamics

12. **Faith-based context as both amplifier and filter**: The organization's 20-year community presence is a genuine asset — it means trust, physical infrastructure, and a motivated volunteer base. But a faith-based organizational identity may also filter which teens and families feel welcome to participate, particularly if they come from different traditions or none at all. The program's community embedding is both its strongest system-level asset and a potential ceiling on the diversity of youth it can effectively reach. How does the program design its public-facing identity to capture the trust asset without triggering the filtering effect?

13. **What happens to the neighborhood system when the program ends**: A $15K seed grant funds a launch, not a decade. If the program runs for two or three years and then loses funding, it will have created mentor-mentee bonds that will be disrupted, expectations in the school and juvenile justice systems that will go unmet, and a volunteer community whose capacity will disperse. The program's success in year one makes the cost of its failure in year four higher, not lower. What does the program do from day one to build toward financial self-sufficiency, and what's the honest assessment of what "sustainable" looks like for a program of this size and community context?
