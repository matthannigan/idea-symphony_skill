# Brainstorming Questions: Youth Mentorship Program for At-Risk Teens

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Program Identity, Framing, and Hidden Assumptions

**Cluster focus**: Whether the program's foundational framings — the "at-risk" label, the referral pipeline, volunteer motivations, and the faith-based organization's 20-year community footprint — are quietly shaping who gets reached and how the relationships begin.

1. **The "at-risk" label as a framing decision**: Why are we treating "at-risk" as a neutral classification rather than a lens that shapes how mentors perceive their mentees from day one — and how does the program's culture, staff language, and mentor training ensure that label never becomes how a teen is treated, including an alternative framing the program offers teens about who they are and why they're here?
2. **Who the formal referral pipeline reaches and misses**: What if the teens most in need of mentorship are precisely those who would never be flagged by school counselors or juvenile justice — who else in the community might identify candidates the formal systems overlook, and what does the referral source signal about which teens get prioritized?
3. **What the 40 volunteers actually want and what teens actually need**: Have we examined what the ~40 interested adults want from this experience, where their motivations might diverge from what teens need, and what self-selection filters or commitment structures would surface a misalignment before a match is formed?
4. **Faith identity as both trust asset and filtering effect**: How does the organization's 20-year faith-based community presence operate simultaneously as a relational asset (trust with skeptical families, motivated volunteers, physical infrastructure) and a filter that may quietly disqualify teens and families from different traditions or none at all — and what's the honest day-to-day answer when a teen's family composition, identity, or beliefs sit in tension with the host institution's values?
5. **Hidden assets in the community's 20-year relational track record**: What has this faith-based organization already learned about building trust with families who are skeptical of institutions, which volunteers among the 40 already have natural gifts for connecting with young people before any training, and how could the program design amplify and spread those instincts as the foundation of how mentors are trained to show up?
6. **Beyond guidance to co-creation**: What if we completely reimagined the mentor-mentee relationship not as adult-to-youth knowledge transfer, but as a genuine mutual discovery partnership — where teens are positioned as co-investigators of their own futures, and mentors are explicitly there to learn something too? How would the program structure, matching criteria, and success metrics change if both parties were required to bring an unresolved question they couldn't answer alone?
7. **The faith org as a deliberate trusted intermediary**: Community Development Financial Institutions and immigrant legal aid organizations occupy a distinctive structural role: they are trusted by a community that distrusts institutions, yet they are also credentialed enough to interface with those institutions. The faith org here holds the same structural position — trusted by families who are wary of schools and juvenile justice — but has likely not made that intermediary role explicit. What governance and communication protocols would the program need to formalize to act as a deliberate trusted intermediary, and what obligations and risks does that role create that the org currently hasn't named?
8. **What if the church's 20-year presence is doing harm by being present**: Imagine the faith-based organization's deep neighborhood roots — its known faces, its reputation, its physical building — are subtly disqualifying certain teens who will never walk through that door because of what the institution represents to their family. What would the program look like if the organization agreed to become functionally invisible?

## Topic Cluster 02: Volunteer Screening, Background Checks, and Disqualifying Criteria

**Cluster focus**: How to design background-check tiers and a screening funnel that calibrate disqualifying criteria across categorical-vs-contextual offenses while preserving enough volunteer pipeline to staff the program.

1. **Disqualifying criteria calibration across the categorical-to-contextual spectrum**: What specific background check tiers should the program require under state law (FBI fingerprint, sex offender registry, state-level disqualifying-offense lists), and what analytical framework should the organization use to set its own thresholds — particularly for borderline cases like old non-violent offenses or expunged records — balancing inclusivity of the volunteer pool against risk tolerance, and who bears legal responsibility if a volunteer with an undisclosed offense causes harm after a compliant check? [User Q]
2. **Screening as a sequential funnel, not a single gate**: If we model volunteer screening as a sequential funnel — application, background check, interview, training completion, matching — where does the most significant drop-off occur in comparable faith-based programs, and how should the program design its active-match capacity expectations around that funnel rather than the raw 40-volunteer headcount?
3. **Parallel screening architectures from adjacent domains**: Background check and disqualifying criteria systems exist in at least three adjacent domains — licensed foster care, Big Brothers Big Sisters, and gig-economy trust-and-safety platforms like Uber — each of which solved the same core tension: how do you screen at scale without creating so much friction that you lose your volunteer pipeline? What can the faith org borrow from how gig platforms use tiered, risk-weighted screening (rather than binary pass/fail) to calibrate who can work unsupervised vs. who needs supervision, and how might adopting that frame change which volunteers you can accept?

## Topic Cluster 03: Mandatory Reporting, Escalation, and Concern-Raising

**Cluster focus**: How mentors are trained as mandatory reporters, how the reporting chain handles grey-zone disclosures, and how reporting obligations are communicated without producing self-censorship in teens.

1. **Mandatory-reporter classification and the legally defensible reporting chain**: Under most state child abuse and neglect reporting statutes, whether an adult volunteer in a faith-based youth program qualifies as a mandatory reporter depends on whether the statute extends to "persons responsible for the care of a child" or specific licensed categories — which classification applies here, what is the legally defensible reporting chain (does a report to an internal supervisor satisfy the obligation, or must the mentor call the state CPS hotline directly), and what documentation of training completion should be retained to demonstrate due diligence if a failure-to-report allegation arises?
2. **Training mentors actually internalize rather than check off**: How do we structure mandatory reporter training so mentors internalize and act on it rather than checking a box during onboarding, and how does the program avoid the safeguarding-theater problem where a compliance posture looks protective without necessarily being protective — particularly given that real harm to teens in this population may come from emotionally harmful but technically-policy-compliant relationships? [User Q]
3. **The grey zone between mentor concern and mandatory report**: How should a mentor raise a concern about a mentee when they're unsure whether it rises to the level of a mandatory report, how do we design the escalation process so that mentor discomfort with a teen's choices doesn't get coded as a safety issue, and how do we communicate reporting obligations in a way that builds rather than erodes trust — anticipating the peer effects as teens talk to each other about what "can" and "can't" be said to a mentor who is a mandatory reporter? [User Q]

## Topic Cluster 04: Liability, Insurance, and Organizational Protections

**Cluster focus**: Mapping the program's liability exposure surfaces and selecting insurance coverage and statutory protections appropriate for a faith-based one-on-one youth program of this size.

1. **Liability exposure mapping and SAM rider**: Across the four distinct liability surfaces (volunteer conduct, mandatory reporting failures, data breach of juvenile justice records, and match-related incidents), what level of general liability coverage is appropriate for a program of this size — typically $1–2M per-occurrence plus an umbrella policy — and should the organization carry a separate sexual abuse and molestation (SAM) rider given that many standard CGL policies exclude it by endorsement, and are volunteer mentors covered as agents of the organization under the existing policy or do they need individual acknowledgment? [User Q]
2. **Volunteer Protection Act limits and the scope of the organizational shield**: Most states have enacted volunteer protection acts (modeled on the federal Volunteer Protection Act of 1997, 42 U.S.C. § 14503) that limit personal liability for volunteers acting in good faith — but these protections do not extend to gross negligence, willful misconduct, or crimes; how should the program's intake and training documentation be structured to establish the scope of volunteer roles clearly enough that the organizational shield is meaningful when conduct falls outside it?
3. **Liability frameworks shaping program scope as a second-order effect**: Legal protections are often designed defensively, restricting activities that carry any risk — which inadvertently limits the kinds of activities (sports, outings, shared projects) that build the most meaningful mentor-mentee bonds. Over time, a highly restricted program may technically continue while losing the relational depth that justifies its existence. What activities does the program explicitly protect space for, and how does legal review support rather than narrow the program's relational ambition?

## Topic Cluster 05: Consent Frameworks and Data Privacy

**Cluster focus**: Designing consent and data-handling architecture across juvenile justice, school, and family systems while protecting minors' privacy and dignity.

1. **Informed consent for juvenile-justice-referred youth across custodial complexity**: How do we obtain informed consent from teens referred through juvenile justice given the power dynamics and privacy stakes of their records, and what consent framework maps the legal authority across biological parent, foster parent, legal guardian, or probation officer depending on the youth's custody and supervision status — including non-traditional households with grandparents, older siblings, or rotating guardians, and what happens when a youth's custodial arrangement changes mid-program? [User Q]
2. **Layered ongoing consent and the age-18 transition**: A one-time enrollment consent form does not address ongoing consent obligations — particularly for activities beyond the standard weekly meeting (off-site outings, photographs for program materials, sharing outcome data with the funding foundation); what layered consent structure should the organization adopt, and at what point does a youth's own consent supersede or replace the guardian's when they turn 18 mid-program?
3. **Minors' data architecture and the firewall around juvenile records**: What data minimization, access-control, and FERPA-inspired policies does the program need for sensitive data flowing from multiple institutional partners (referral source, attendance, behavioral or mental health context), what firewall exists between juvenile-justice referral source information and what mentors are told so a teen's history isn't inadvertently communicated in ways that damage their reputation in the congregation community, and who within the organization may lawfully access juvenile justice referral information without becoming a party to a confidentiality violation?
4. **The privacy paradox for court-referred youth**: Imagine Jordan, 16, referred through the juvenile justice system, sitting in the program intake meeting while an adult they've just met fills out a form with information from their case file. What does trust feel like for Jordan in that moment — and how does the program design consent conversations, data handling, and information-sharing boundaries so that Jordan experiences being protected rather than being processed?

## Topic Cluster 06: Matching, Power, and Youth Agency

**Cluster focus**: The criteria, voice, and power dynamics that determine who gets matched with whom — and how matching design either reinforces or counteracts hidden bias toward easier cases.

1. **Matching criteria, predictors of retention, and matching as a relational craft**: What matching criteria produce the strongest mentor-mentee outcomes — demographic similarity, shared interests, personality fit, proximity, communication style, or something less obvious — which of these is most predictive of 12-month retention based on mentorship research, and what would a matching process look like that is a thoughtful relationship-centered practice taking seriously both the teen's strengths and aspirations and the mentor's particular gifts, rather than just availability and demographics? [User Q]
2. **Whose criteria drive matching and how teens shape who they're paired with**: Matching criteria developed by adults — school counselors, juvenile justice workers, congregation — may reflect what adults think teens need rather than what teens themselves say they want; how can teens meaningfully shape the pairing rather than experiencing it as something done *to* them, what does a youth-voice component of the matching process look like, and what happens when a match feels wrong to the teen?
3. **Power dynamics and what "voluntary" means for court-involved youth**: What structural features of the program either reinforce or help dissolve the inherent power imbalance between an adult volunteer and a teen referred through juvenile justice, and have we thought through what "voluntary" really means when participation is connected to a court-involved status?
4. **Drift toward serving the easy cases and the JJ-referral as a self-fulfilling dynamic**: Any matching algorithm that optimizes for relationship quality tends to route the most engaged mentors to the most responsive teens — leaving those with the deepest needs (most trauma, most distrust, most erratic behavior) matched with less experienced mentors or in lower-priority slots; meanwhile, mentors who consciously or unconsciously expect more difficulty from juvenile-justice-referred teens may activate a self-fulfilling dynamic that creates the difficulty they expected. How does the program structurally counteract both the drift toward easy cases and referral-source as a hidden bias variable?
5. **Teens as the safety architects**: What if the teens themselves — not compliance officers, not legal counsel — were the primary designers of the safety and consent protocols? Imagine convening a youth advisory panel to draft the boundary framework, the meeting-location norms, and the escalation procedures. What new forms of trust and genuine protection might emerge that no adult-designed policy has ever produced?

## Topic Cluster 07: Safety Protocols and the Relational Core

**Cluster focus**: Designing meeting-location, contact, and protective protocols that strengthen rather than corrode the trusting relationship that makes mentorship work.

1. **Public-vs-private meeting locations and safety systems that strengthen rather than chill the relationship**: Should mentor-mentee meetings always be held in public spaces, or are there specific circumstances where private or home settings are appropriate and defensible — and more broadly, how does the program design protective protocols (mandatory public locations, required chaperones, logged communications, background checks, boundary guidelines) so they support deeper connection rather than create the bureaucratic distance and surveillance feel that erodes trust with teens who already distrust institutions, recognizing the second-order effect when over-engineered safety hollows out the relational core? [User Q]
2. **Faith-based context, teen authenticity, and institutional-trauma-aware adult authority**: Many teens referred through juvenile justice have experienced adult authority as punitive and unreliable, and a teen's personal situation — family composition, identity, beliefs — may create tension with the organization's faith-based values; how does mentor training address how these teens may read even well-intentioned adults, and how does the program create space for teens to define what a good relationship looks like on their own terms, including the right to be fully themselves in a mentorship hosted by a religious institution?
3. **What if safety protocols are the danger**: What if every safeguard you install — the check-in logs, the two-adult rules, the scheduled meeting times — quietly signals to a teenager that they are a threat to be managed rather than a person to be trusted, and what would you redesign if you accepted that the bureaucratic apparatus of protection is actively eroding the thing you're trying to build?
4. **The first meeting moment**: Imagine Marcus, 15, referred through juvenile justice, sitting across from a volunteer mentor for the very first time in the church meeting room — what does that scene look, sound, and feel like, and what structures or rituals could the program design into that first encounter so the relationship begins with safety and trust rather than awkwardness and surveillance?

## Topic Cluster 08: Mentor Support, Retention, and Burnout

**Cluster focus**: The cadence, peer infrastructure, and organizational scaffolding that sustain mentors through the predictable motivational troughs of a year-long match.

1. **The 6-month motivational trough and ongoing support cadence**: What ongoing support and check-in cadence do mentors need to prevent burnout, especially during the first six months of a match, knowing that at the 6–9 month mark the novelty has worn off and outcomes aren't yet visible — a trough that coincides with when teens (who've invested emotionally) are most vulnerable to abandonment, and how does the program remain viable if a significant portion of the volunteer base is lost to life changes, burnout, or a single damaging incident that chills participation? [User Q]
2. **The mentor-confidence-mentee-engagement virtuous cycle in the first 90 days**: A mentor who feels effective shows up with more energy and creativity; a mentee who feels that energy is more likely to engage openly; that engagement makes the mentor feel more effective. How does the program design reinforce this virtuous cycle in its first 90 days — before most pairs would naturally find their rhythm on their own?
3. **Coordinator single-point-of-failure and a self-replenishing volunteer pipeline**: One-on-one matching programs concentrate enormous relationship risk in a single staff coordinator — if that person leaves mid-year, what is the protocol for maintaining 40 active matches without disrupting fragile trust relationships; and because the initial pool of 40 is a one-time congregation-driven cohort, what does the program design to make volunteer recruitment a continuous, embedded function rather than a launch-phase event?
4. **Training rooted in 20-year wisdom, peak-mentor instincts, and ready-to-lead volunteers**: This faith-based organization already knows how to accompany people through hard seasons, some of the 40 interested adults already have natural gifts for connecting with young people before any training, and some have the relational credibility and passion to help shape the program from the inside — what existing pastoral, community, and volunteer practices could form the backbone of mentor training (rather than importing a generic curriculum), what do the natural-gifted adults already do instinctively that creates safety and rapport, and how could involving the ready-to-lead volunteers early change the quality of what gets built?
5. **The sponsor/sponsee model from recovery communities**: AA and NA have sustained one-on-one peer relationships at massive scale, with minimal institutional overhead, for decades — a structural feat this program is attempting to replicate. The key design elements are: mutual identification (not charity), a shared framework that gives both parties a script for hard conversations, and a community of practice that holds both the helper and the helped. What would it mean to deliberately borrow these design elements — not the spiritual content — and how would the program's training curriculum change if it treated mentor sustainability the same way recovery communities treat sponsor sustainability?
6. **The moment a volunteer almost quit**: Imagine Deb, a 42-year-old congregant who signed up full of hope but hit a wall at month four — her mentee stopped returning texts, showed up visibly withdrawn, and Deb felt helpless and unqualified. What does the program's support structure look like in that exact moment, and how does someone go from "I don't know if I'm helping" to "I understand this is part of the work"?

## Topic Cluster 09: Outcome Measurement and Success Definition

**Cluster focus**: Designing measurement that is honest about timescale mismatches, that doesn't overburden teens, and that distinguishes funder-legible metrics from what teens themselves consider success.

1. **Measurement that doesn't overburden teens or surveil mentors**: How do we measure program outcomes without overburdening teens with surveys or making mentors feel surveilled, what is the minimum viable measurement set that satisfies a regional foundation's reporting expectations while avoiding survey fatigue in a population already stressed by school and justice-system demands, and how do we design data collection so teens understand who sees it, why it's gathered, and what they gain from sharing honestly rather than feeling they are being tracked for someone else? [User Q]
2. **Funder metrics vs teen-defined success and the timescale mismatch**: The metrics most legible to funders — attendance, recidivism, grades — may not capture what actually matters to the teens themselves, and youth-development outcomes often take years to manifest, well past a 12-month grant cycle; how does the program build an outcome framework honest about the mismatch between what funders want and when real results become legible, while preventing optimization for funder-visible metrics from gradually reshaping the program in ways that make it less genuinely useful to the young people, and creating a mechanism for teens' own definition of success to shape how the program evolves?
3. **Leading vs. lagging indicators and a baseline data window**: What leading indicators (school attendance trajectories, mentor check-in consistency, self-reported goal progress) are both measurable within 12 months and predictive of longer-term outcomes based on established mentorship evaluation frameworks, what data points should be captured at match initiation as a baseline (attendance records, grades, self-efficacy scores), and which can be collected through existing school counselor relationships rather than requiring the program to administer its own assessments?
4. **Healthy graduation vs premature exit and the unmeasurable core**: What if a match ends before the one-year mark not because it failed but because the teen no longer needs it — and how does the measurement framework distinguish premature exit from healthy graduation, and what would we need to believe about relationship quality and human development to accept that a program can be working even when the quantitative indicators don't yet show it, and how do we build that case for the foundation without abandoning rigor?
5. **What if chronic absenteeism is the correct response to the environment**: Imagine the 35% absenteeism rate is not a symptom of at-risk behavior but a rational, accurate assessment by teenagers that school is not serving them — and this mentorship program is being designed to make teens more compliant with a system that deserves their skepticism. What would a program look like that took that premise seriously instead of treating presence as inherently good?

## Topic Cluster 10: Sustainability, Endings, and Long-Term Horizon

**Cluster focus**: How matches end well, how the program survives past seed funding, and what the organization owes to the neighborhood system if the program eventually winds down.

1. **Match-termination protocol and the year-one ending without abandonment**: What's the right protocol when a mentor-mentee pairing isn't working, and how do we transition without causing harm to the teen — and given that the program commits mentors to one year while at-risk teens 13–17 often need sustained support across multiple years, how do we distinguish a formal "commitment" from an ongoing relationship that continues, and ensure that ending a match doesn't produce measurable harm by abruptly stranding teens who've invested emotionally? [User Q]
2. **The first 90 days as an investment in five strong matches**: If the program launched with even five strong mentor-mentee matches in the first 90 days and invested deeply in those relationships, what would you hope those five pairs would experience, learn, and demonstrate — and how would that inform how the program grows from there?
3. **Seed-to-sustaining funding bridge and the neighborhood-ripple cost of failure**: The $15K seed grant funds a launch, not a decade — what does the cost structure look like at 15, 25, and 40 active matches, what funding sources do comparable community mentorship programs use to bridge seed to Year 2, what is the honest probability the program is still operating at the 18-month mark, and have we designed it so a sudden funding loss causes an orderly wind-down rather than abruptly stranding 40 active pairs — recognizing that the program's success in year one makes the cost of failure in year four higher, not lower, for the school and juvenile justice systems, the volunteer community, and the neighborhood it has begun to serve?
4. **A program that grows a generation, not a cohort**: Imagine if this program were designed with an explicit 20-year horizon — not as an annual intake of matched pairs, but as a living institution that a teen who ages out of mentorship eventually re-enters as a mentor, then as a program elder, then as a community trustee. What would need to be true about the program's structure, governance, and culture in year one to make that kind of intergenerational emergence possible?
