# Privacy Architecture and Data Pipeline - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Q1: Raw-data boundary enforcement under all failure modes, including the model-update path
* **Policy ≠ enforcement.** The claim that "raw biometric data never leaves the device" is a policy statement, not a technical guarantee. Have we audited every code path that writes to flash or to a transmit buffer? A crash dump handler that naively serializes the MCU's RAM will cheerfully exfiltrate a 30-second PPG ring buffer to the companion app's debug log. Mitigation: define a formal taint-tracking policy at the firmware level — any buffer tagged as raw sensor data must be zeroed before it can be passed to any output subsystem.

* **OTA model updates are a two-way channel you're pretending is one-way.** If the NPU weights can be pushed to the device, what prevents a malicious or negligent update from including a small encoder that compresses recent sensor history into the model's bias parameters before calling "home"? This isn't science fiction — it's a well-documented side-channel in federated learning literature. Mitigation: model updates should be cryptographically signed and size-bounded, and an independent on-device verifier should reject any update that touches runtime sensor buffers rather than only the weight store.

* **Have alternative isolation primitives been seriously evaluated, or did the team default to "on-device"?** Trusted Execution Environments (ARM TrustZone is available on many Cortex-M33+ MCUs) can cryptographically isolate the inference engine from all firmware except a narrow attestation interface, achieving stronger guarantees than process-level separation. The power overhead is modest. If the team hasn't prototyped this, they may be building a privacy story on an assumption of perfect firmware correctness — which is never warranted.

* **Diagnostic telemetry is the most likely leak vector, yet it's rarely in scope for threat modeling.** Manufacturing test modes, field-diagnostics APIs, and Bluetooth HCI traces are almost always designed before the privacy architecture is finalized, then never revisited. One engineer adds a "dump last 10 seconds of raw PPG for debug" command that's gated behind a flag — and that flag ships enabled in early retail units. Mitigation: treat diagnostic interfaces as a first-class attack surface and require explicit security review before any diagnostic command that touches sensor history is merged.

* **The model-update path and privacy boundary interact in a subtle way the team should model explicitly.** If the on-device model is retrained or fine-tuned on personal history — even locally — the updated weights are a compressed encoding of that history. Sending those weights anywhere (telemetry, model improvement programs) re-creates the privacy exposure the on-device architecture was designed to eliminate. Mitigation: if any form of on-device personalization is in scope, define now whether updated weights are classified as PHI-equivalent under the HIPAA posture you're building toward.

## Q2: Aggregated summaries, on-device evidence retention, and the adversary model
* **"Aggregated summaries" is doing a lot of undefined work.** Have we specified what aggregation level is sufficient to prevent re-identification, or are we assuming that anything short of raw waveforms is safe? A summary that includes "detected 3 arrhythmia events between 02:14 and 02:31, mean HR 112 bpm, SpO2 nadir 91%" is clinically meaningful — and it's also a near-unique fingerprint for a motivated adversary with access to hospital admission records. Mitigation: define the aggregation specification in terms of a concrete adversary model before finalizing the sync protocol, not after.

* **Users almost certainly don't understand that aggregated summaries are still a persistent model of their cardiac behavior.** We're asking users to trust a privacy claim ("raw data stays on device") while syncing a rich longitudinal health record to a companion app they've agreed to in a EULA they didn't read. That's not a privacy architecture — it's a liability posture. Mitigation: design an informed-consent flow that uses plain language to explain what the companion app actually stores, and give users a one-tap "delete my health history from the app" control before launch.

* **On-device evidence representation for clinical actionability has not been specified, and the gap between "enough for an alert" and "enough for a 510(k) submission" is large.** Storing only a confidence score and duration for a detected arrhythmia event may be sufficient for a consumer alert, but FDA reviewers for a future 510(k) will want to see the evidence record that drove the classification — which probably means derived features, not just the final score. If the team designs the evidence store for the consumer case and later needs to retrofit it for clinical use, that's a re-architecture of both on-device storage and the sync protocol. Mitigation: design the evidence representation now with the 510(k) evidentiary standard as a constraint, even if that data stays on-device and is only exportable under explicit user action.

* **The adversary model has likely been scoped too narrowly.** The obvious adversary is a cloud hacker. The less obvious but more probable adversaries are: (1) a domestic partner who has physical access to the device and companion app, (2) the manufacturer's own data science team via an "anonymized" telemetry feed, and (3) insurance underwriters who gain access through a future B2B partnership. Have any of these been threat-modeled explicitly? Mitigation: write down the adversary model as a named document, circulate it to legal and product, and revisit it before each new data partnership is signed.

## Q3: Subpoena exposure of "aggregated summaries"
* **The consumer wellness carve-out from HIPAA does not protect against civil subpoenas.** HIPAA's Safe Harbor provisions apply to covered entities and their business associates — a consumer wellness app that has never signed a BAA with anyone is not a covered entity, and its data is fully subpoenable in civil litigation, employment disputes, and insurance underwriting. The team may be conflating "not subject to HIPAA" with "legally protected," which are different things. Mitigation: have outside counsel issue a written opinion on the civil subpoena exposure of companion app data before Series B, not after your first enterprise partnership conversation.

* **"Aggregated summaries" may already meet the functional definition of PHI under scenarios the team hasn't modeled.** If the device is worn by someone who later enters a covered entity's care and the companion app data is shared with that provider — even informally — the summaries retroactively become PHI under HIPAA's definition, regardless of how they were classified at the time of collection. Mitigation: architect the companion app as if summaries are PHI from day one. The marginal cost of doing so now is low; the cost of retrofitting after a partnership is signed is very high.

* **Employers and insurers are more likely adversaries than attorneys, and they operate through softer pressure than subpoenas.** A corporate wellness program that integrates with the companion app, or a life insurer that offers premium discounts for "sharing your health data," creates a coercive pathway to aggregate summaries that bypasses legal process entirely. Have we considered that the privacy architecture protects against technical exfiltration but does nothing to prevent the user from being economically pressured into voluntary disclosure? Mitigation: establish a clear policy — enforceable in the terms of service — prohibiting employer or insurer integration, and design the companion app's data export function to make bulk disclosure deliberately cumbersome.

* **The legal distinction between "processed alerts" and "aggregated summaries" is not stable.** A single alert — "possible atrial fibrillation detected, 23:47, duration 8 minutes" — is clinically specific enough that a court, insurer, or employer would treat it as medical information regardless of what the product's privacy policy says. Mitigation: consult with a health privacy attorney to define a legally defensible categorization of what the companion app stores, and design the data model around that categorization, not around a technical definition the legal team wasn't consulted on.

## Q4: When on-device privacy architecture becomes a genuine asset
* **The differentiator claim is only compelling if you can demonstrate enforcement, not just assert policy.** Imagine pitching a clinical partner: "raw data never leaves the device." Their first question will be "how do you know?" If the answer is "our firmware doesn't do that," you've lost the room. If the answer is "here's the formal attestation report from our TrustZone enclave and here's the independent audit we commissioned," you've won a procurement conversation. The on-device architecture is only a genuine asset when it's verifiable — and right now, it isn't. Mitigation: build the attestation infrastructure now, while the firmware is still small enough to audit, rather than treating it as a marketing claim.

* **Privacy-conscious users in the 40+ demographic are more likely to be persuaded by what you don't collect than by what you do.** One potential challenge is that the marketing default is to lead with features — "detects arrhythmias, monitors SpO2" — rather than with the privacy architecture. But for users who've watched a family member's medical history weaponized by an insurer, the proposition "your cardiologist can never be subpoenaed for data that doesn't exist in the cloud" is more compelling than any sensor spec. Have we tested this message with actual target users, or are we assuming the technical team's instincts about what users value are correct?

* **The investor conversation around on-device privacy is a double-edged asset.** Privacy-focused investors will find it compelling; growth investors will see it as a constraint on future monetization via health data licensing. Have we thought through how the privacy architecture commits us — or doesn't — to a particular business model? Being explicit about this now prevents a fractured board conversation later when a data partnership opportunity surfaces. Mitigation: define a written policy on what data the company will never monetize, and include it in Series B investor materials so alignment is established before the term sheet.

## Q5: Imagine the opposite — the device is a black box even to the user
* **The current architecture already has significant black-box properties that the team may be understating.** The user receives a binary alert but cannot inspect the feature vector that drove it, cannot see the model's confidence interval, and cannot challenge a false positive with any supporting evidence. One potential challenge here is that "privacy from the cloud" and "auditability for the user" are being conflated — the architecture protects the user's data from Anthropic and insurers, but it does not give the user any meaningful ability to audit what conclusions the device is drawing about their body. Mitigation: design a "show your work" mode that surfaces derived features (not raw waveforms) alongside each alert, giving users enough information to make an informed decision about whether to seek care.

* **A fully sealed black box would eliminate false-positive liability — and that's worth thinking through seriously.** If the user can never see the model's confidence score, they can't argue that a 51% confidence threshold is too low to justify an emergency room visit. The manufacturer controls the narrative entirely. This is not a recommendation — it's a risk the current design partially creates by surfacing alerts without uncertainty quantification. Mitigation: define the alert presentation standard now: will confidence intervals be shown? Will the user be told "this may be a false alarm"? These are liability decisions disguised as UX decisions.

* **The real tension this question exposes is between two different notions of privacy that the team is conflating.** Privacy-from-cloud means third parties can't see your data. Privacy-from-manufacturer means the company itself can't build a persistent model of your health behavior. The current architecture achieves the first but not the second — every firmware update, every alert pattern, every sync event is visible to the manufacturer's infrastructure. Have we acknowledged this gap to users anywhere in the consent flow? Mitigation: add a clear statement to the privacy policy distinguishing what the company can and cannot infer from device behavior, distinct from raw biometric data.

* **A sealed black box would actually make the FDA pathway harder, not easier.** 510(k) clearance requires demonstrating substantial equivalence to a predicate device, which requires showing the algorithm's performance characteristics on a labeled dataset. A model the user cannot audit is also a model that's harder to explain to FDA reviewers. The architecture's opacity is already a regulatory liability — not just a user-experience limitation. Mitigation: design the on-device evidence store and the model's explainability layer in parallel with the regulatory strategy, not as a downstream engineering task.

## Q6: Radical Data Sovereignty as a New Paradigm
* **Treating biometrics as personhood rather than data has serious practical implications that the team should pressure-test before adopting as a design philosophy.** If the wearer's patterns are legally and philosophically indistinguishable from the person, then a firmware bug that corrupts the on-device model isn't just a reliability failure — it's a harm to the person. That's not a metaphor; it's a liability exposure. Have we considered whether this framing creates obligations the company is not prepared to honor? Mitigation: be precise about what "biometric patterns as personhood" means in operational terms before embedding it in the product's founding philosophy.

* **One potential challenge with the radical sovereignty framing is that it forecloses the model improvement path entirely.** If no external entity can ever hold or interpret biometric patterns, then federated learning — even with differential privacy — is off the table, because the aggregated gradient updates are derived from those patterns. That means the model shipped at launch is the model you're stuck with, permanently, for every user. For a cardiac anomaly detector targeting a population where edge cases kill people, a static model is a clinical safety risk. Mitigation: define explicitly what "no external entity can interpret patterns" means for model improvement — because the current framing implies a product that can never be made safer.

* **The philosophical premise is compelling marketing but it conflicts with the regulatory reality the team is already committed to.** FDA 510(k) clearance for a cardiac device requires clinical validation on a representative population dataset — which necessarily means someone, somewhere, is holding and interpreting biometric patterns from real users. If data sovereignty means the manufacturer can never access that data, the 510(k) pathway closes. Have we resolved this contradiction, or are we assuming data sovereignty applies to the consumer product and HIPAA-compliant access applies to the clinical pathway — without a clear protocol for the transition?

* **"No external entity, including the manufacturer" is an extraordinary claim that requires extraordinary infrastructure.** Zero-knowledge proof systems and fully homomorphic encryption can theoretically allow model inference without the manufacturer seeing inputs — but they currently run at 100-10,000x the computational cost of standard inference on the same hardware. On an NPU sized for a 7-day battery budget, this isn't feasible today. Mitigation: if radical sovereignty is the north star, be honest about the gap between the aspiration and current hardware constraints, and define a roadmap that names specific cryptographic milestones rather than asserting the goal is already achieved.
