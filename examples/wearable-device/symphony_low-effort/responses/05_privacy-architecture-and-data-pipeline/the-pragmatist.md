# Privacy Architecture and Data Pipeline - The Pragmatist

---

## Q1: Raw-data boundary enforcement under all failure modes, including the model-update path
* **Start with hardware-enforced read isolation.** The most durable raw-data boundary is one that firmware policy can't accidentally undo. A practical first step: configure the NPU's DMA controller so that sensor ring buffers are write-only from the application processor's perspective — the AP can trigger inference but cannot read back raw samples. This is implementable on most MCU-class devices with MPU regions in a week or two; it doesn't require a full TEE and survives most firmware bugs by default.
* **Treat crash dumps as a separate attack surface with a specific mitigation.** The failure mode teams most often overlook is the debug path. A practical approach: build a crash-dump scrubber that runs before any data leaves flash — it zeroes the sensor ring-buffer addresses and replaces them with a checksum. This can be a 200-line post-processing hook in your firmware build pipeline and should be on the sprint board now, not after launch.
* **OTA model updates should be one-way envelopes.** The OTA mechanism is a legitimate re-identification risk if the update payload can encode sensor history (e.g., model weights updated per-device). To make this feasible without a full federated-learning stack, ship a universal model binary signed by your key, delivered through a standard FOTA partition with no device-specific payload. You lose personalization but close the exfiltration channel cleanly — a reasonable tradeoff at consumer wellness stage.
* **Audit the companion-app debug log pipeline before beta.** In practice, most raw-data leaks happen through SDK telemetry and crash reporters (Firebase, Sentry) in the mobile app, not the firmware. Do a one-day audit of every third-party SDK in the companion app and enumerate what it captures on exception. That's a concrete, bounded task that can be completed this sprint and will surface real exposure.

## Q2: Aggregated summaries, on-device evidence retention, and the adversary model
* **Define "aggregated summary" with a concrete schema, not a principle.** The term "aggregated summary" is doing a lot of work right now. A practical approach: draft the actual sync payload schema — field names, types, resolutions, retention windows — and run it through a simple re-identification checklist (quasi-identifier overlap, timestamp granularity, rare-event frequency). This is a 2–3 hour exercise that forces the team to move from policy language to implementation decisions.
* **Store derived features, not waveforms, as anomaly evidence.** For clinical actionability without raw PPG exposure, the on-device evidence record should contain: event timestamp (minute resolution), classification confidence, duration in seconds, heart rate variability summary statistics (mean, SDNN), and SpO2 nadir. This is enough for a clinician to act on and for a 510(k) submission to reference, and it can't be reverse-engineered into a raw waveform. Define this schema now; it shapes NPU output layer design.
* **Model a realistic adversary, not a theoretical one.** A practical adversary model for a 12-person startup is: (1) a determined journalist with your companion-app network traffic, (2) a backend breach exposing your sync database. Design for those two. Full cryptographic re-identification proofs are out of scope for Series A; a STRIDE threat model on the sync endpoint is not. Schedule a half-day session with your firmware and mobile engineers to run through it.
* **Timestamp granularity is your biggest re-identification lever.** Aggregated summaries with per-second event timestamps are meaningfully different from per-minute or per-hour. If you round alert timestamps to the nearest 15 minutes in the sync payload, you retain almost all clinical utility while substantially degrading correlation attacks. This is a one-line change to your serialization layer — do it by default.

## Q3: Subpoena exposure of aggregated summaries
* **Get a one-page legal memo on this before launch, not after.** The consumer wellness vs. HIPAA boundary is genuinely unsettled for aggregated biometric summaries. A practical next step: retain a health-data attorney for a two-hour engagement to give you a written opinion on whether your specific sync payload constitutes PHI under the HIPAA definitions you actually plan to use. That memo becomes a board artifact and informs your TOS drafting. This is a $500–1,500 spend that closes a material liability question.
* **Your TOS and privacy policy need to acknowledge the subpoena scenario explicitly.** Most consumer wellness TOS documents say nothing about third-party legal process. A practical approach: add a "law enforcement and legal process" section that tells users what you will and won't resist, and what you actually hold. If you hold only aggregated summaries, say so specifically — it's a genuine differentiator in your favor and sets user expectations correctly.
* **Design the backend to hold as little as possible.** The safest legal posture is one where there is genuinely nothing useful to produce. A practical approach: default retention for aggregated summaries on your backend to 90 days with user-controlled deletion, and store no PII alongside the biometric payload beyond a device pseudonym. This isn't just good privacy practice — it's a concrete litigation cost reduction. Implement it in your initial backend design, not as a retrofit.

## Q4: When on-device privacy architecture becomes a genuine differentiator
* **The clinical partner conversation hinges on data governance, not technology.** When pitching a hospital system or research institution, the practical differentiator is that you can sign a BAA that is trivially auditable: "we never hold raw biometrics; here is the sync schema; here is the firmware source for the boundary enforcement." That's a three-page technical appendix, not a 40-page compliance review. To make this feasible, document your boundary enforcement architecture now in a form that a compliance officer can read — not engineering notes, but a one-page data flow diagram with explicit "never crosses this boundary" callouts.
* **Privacy-conscious users respond to specificity, not slogans.** "Your data never leaves your wrist" is a marketing claim. "Your raw PPG waveform is processed on a dedicated neural processor and the result — a confidence score and event timestamp — is what syncs" is a verifiable statement. A practical approach: build a "what we hold" screen into the companion app that shows the user the exact last sync payload. This is a trust-building feature that costs one sprint and turns your architecture into a visible product differentiator.
* **Investor due diligence increasingly includes data liability.** Series B and strategic investors in health tech are running data governance diligence as a standard item. Your on-device architecture substantially reduces that risk surface. To make this a credible asset, prepare a one-page "data liability summary" for the data room: what you hold, retention policy, legal exposure scenarios, and how your architecture constrains each. That document converts a technical decision into a fundraising asset.

## Q5: The sealed black-box device — privacy from the device maker vs. privacy from third parties
* **The sealed-firmware model is actually closer to current medical device practice.** FDA-cleared devices routinely run algorithms users cannot audit. The practical tension the question reveals is real: your current architecture protects users from cloud exposure but not from opaque on-device inference. A minimum viable step toward user-auditable inference: publish the anomaly detection thresholds and feature definitions in your support documentation. Users can't audit the model weights, but they can understand what triggers an alert. This is a documentation sprint, not an engineering one.
* **An export function for on-device inference logs is the practical middle ground.** You don't need to expose raw waveforms to give users meaningful transparency. A practical approach: implement an optional "export my data" function that produces a JSON file of the on-device event log — timestamps, confidence scores, sensor states — that the user can download and share with their cardiologist. This preserves the privacy-from-cloud guarantee while giving users genuine agency. It's also a competitive feature against devices that offer no export at all.
* **The binary-alert-only model has a real product failure mode.** If the device produces only an alert or non-alert with no supporting context, users who receive a false positive have no recourse — and users who receive a false negative and have an event have no record to show a physician. A practical approach: surface at minimum a "why this alert was triggered" summary (e.g., "elevated heart rate variability detected over 4 hours, SpO2 dipped below threshold twice") alongside the binary alert. This preserves the sealed-model architecture while giving users enough to act on and substantially reducing your false-positive liability exposure.

## Q6: Radical data sovereignty as a new design paradigm
* **The philosophical premise is interesting; the practical test is whether it changes any engineering decision.** "Treating biometric patterns as personhood" is a framing, not a specification. A practical approach: take that premise and ask what it would require differently in the next sprint. If the answer is "nothing" — if your current on-device architecture already satisfies it — then the framing is marketing copy. If it requires, say, user-controlled encryption keys for the on-device model state or a hardware-enforced wipe mechanism, then it's a real constraint. Write down the two or three specific engineering choices that would differ, and decide which ones are worth the cost.
* **User-controlled model keys are the most technically tractable version of this idea.** If you want the device maker to be genuinely unable to interpret a user's inference state, a practical approach is to encrypt the on-device model's activation state with a key derived from the user's PIN or biometric. The manufacturer never holds the key. This is implementable on ARM TrustZone-enabled MCUs and adds one enrollment step to onboarding. It doesn't address the model-weight opacity problem, but it does mean that even a firmware extraction gives an attacker encrypted feature state, not plaintext inference history. Estimate this at 3–4 weeks of firmware engineering.
* **The companion app is where the sovereignty premise breaks down first.** Even if the device is architecturally sovereign, the companion app almost certainly runs on iOS or Android, reports crashes to Apple or Google, and syncs through your backend. A practical approach: map every data flow in the companion app against the "no external entity can hold or interpret" premise and identify which flows are genuinely necessary vs. incidental (analytics, crash reporting, push notifications). Eliminating the incidental ones is a finite sprint; it doesn't require rearchitecting the app.
