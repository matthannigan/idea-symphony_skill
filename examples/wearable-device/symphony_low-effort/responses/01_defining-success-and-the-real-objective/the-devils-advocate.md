# Defining Success and the Real Objective - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Load-Bearing Constraint: Accuracy, Latency, Power, Privacy

* **"All four" is a budget fiction.** Have we considered that framing accuracy, latency, power, and privacy as co-equal constraints is a political compromise, not an engineering decision? For early cardiac arrhythmia detection, sensitivity at clinically relevant specificity (e.g., >95% sensitivity at 80% specificity for AFib onset) is the only constraint that makes the device worth wearing. If optimizing for that sensitivity target requires running the NPU at full duty cycle, you'll learn that early and adjust the battery target — not the other way around. Ship the accuracy spec first, then discover the real power budget.

* **"Early detection" timescale mismatch.** One potential challenge: the team may be designing against the wrong detection window. In clinical literature, "early detection" of paroxysmal AFib means catching 30-second episodes before they terminate; for obstructive sleep apnea, it means identifying apnea-hypopnea index above 15 events per hour across a full night. Those are structurally different inference problems with opposite latency requirements — one needs real-time windowed classification, the other needs multi-hour temporal aggregation. Have we built consensus on which problem is primary, or are we quietly trying to solve both without acknowledging the tradeoff?

* **Privacy as constraint may be masking a regulatory hedge.** The "raw data never leaves the device" requirement is positioned as a privacy value, but it may actually be a strategy to avoid FDA Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) classification for the cloud-side component. That's a legitimate tactic — but conflating it with user privacy creates confusion in architecture decisions. If the real driver is regulatory risk reduction, name it explicitly, because it changes which privacy tradeoffs are actually negotiable (e.g., aggregated waveform features vs. fully processed alerts).

* **Alert-actionability is the missing metric.** The team has no stated metric for alert actionability — the gap between "fired an alert" and "user took a meaningful clinical action." If the device fires a correct AFib alert but the user dismisses it as a false positive 70% of the time, the detection accuracy is irrelevant. Mitigation: before locking the ML architecture, define a "confirmed action rate" metric (user contacts physician within 48 hours of alert) and instrument it in the companion app from day one.

## Defining "Anomaly" and Who Owns the Definition

* **Algorithm drift without an owner is a liability.** Have we considered what happens when the on-device model's anomaly threshold drifts out of alignment with updated clinical guidelines? ACC/AHA AFib guidelines were revised in 2023; if the device's anomaly definition is baked into firmware and updated only via OTA, the company has a window where shipped devices are making clinical judgments against stale criteria. Mitigation: version the anomaly definition as a separable, updatable artifact within the firmware — distinct from the model weights — and establish a clinical advisory process for threshold changes.

* **User-controlled sensitivity is a product trap.** Some teams default to letting users adjust alert sensitivity to reduce complaints. One potential challenge: if a user raises the detection threshold (fewer alerts) and then has a cardiac event that goes undetected, the company faces liability even as a consumer wellness device. Conversely, users who want maximum sensitivity will be flooded with false positives and churn. The definition of "anomaly" cannot be safely delegated to users without explicit legal guardrails. Mitigation: frame user controls as "notification preferences," not "detection sensitivity" — the model runs at fixed clinical thresholds; users only choose how and when they're notified.

* **PPG-derived AFib detection has a known specificity ceiling.** The clinical literature on optical PPG-based AFib detection puts sensitivity around 97% but specificity at 82-88%, meaning roughly 1 in 7 alerts is a false positive in a population without underlying arrhythmia. For a consumer device targeting healthy adults 40+, the false positive rate will look worse than in clinical validation cohorts (which often include enriched arrhythmia populations). Have we stress-tested the anomaly definition against a normative population rather than a clinical one? Mitigation: run validation on a de-identified dataset from a fitness wearable cohort, not a hospital Holter cohort.

* **Conflict between clinical literature and regulatory body is not hypothetical.** FDA's De Novo for consumer AFib detection (e.g., the Apple Watch pathway) required performance validation against a specific clinical comparator with defined endpoints. If the team's anomaly definition deviates from what FDA has already cleared, the 510(k) path becomes novel rather than predicate-based, which adds 18-24 months and requires clinical studies. Mitigation: reverse-engineer the anomaly definition from the desired 510(k) predicate device now, not after the consumer launch.

## Bridging the Gap Between Alert and Action

* **The "alone with their wrist" scenario is the real UX failure mode.** Have we considered that the most dangerous moment in this device's user journey is a true-positive AFib alert at 2 AM when the user is alone, anxious, and has no clinical context? The companion app design almost certainly imagines a user who is calm, informed, and has a cardiologist on speed dial. A 68-year-old with a family history of cardiac events will instead enter a stress spiral that elevates heart rate, generates more motion artifact, and potentially triggers a second false alert. Mitigation: the alert UX must include an immediate "is this an emergency?" triage flow that routes to either 911, a nurse hotline, or a "monitor for the next hour" mode — not just a push notification.

* **Alert fatigue will destroy the value proposition within 30 days.** One potential challenge: consumer health devices consistently show that users who receive more than 2-3 non-emergency alerts per week begin ignoring all alerts by day 30 (see Fitbit and Apple Watch user studies). A PPG-based device worn during exercise, sleep, and daily activity will generate motion-artifact false positives that, even at 88% specificity, produce several alerts per week in an active user. If the device cries wolf in week two, it will be dismissed in week four when a real event occurs. Mitigation: implement a tiered confidence system — low-confidence anomalies log silently, medium-confidence prompts a "we noticed something, tap to review," and high-confidence fires a real alert. Never surface a raw model output directly as a user notification.

* **"Processed alerts only" is not a user-comprehensible output.** The architecture decision to sync only alerts and aggregated summaries — not waveforms — means the companion app has no way to show the user the signal that triggered the alert. For a health-conscious adult 40+, "your device detected an irregular heartbeat" with no supporting evidence will drive unnecessary ER visits (estimated at $2,000-5,000 per visit) and eventual distrust. Mitigation: reconsider the privacy architecture to allow a 30-second encrypted waveform snippet to accompany each alert, stored only on the companion device, shareable only at user discretion. This doesn't violate on-device raw data processing while giving the alert enough context to be actionable.

* **Clinician integration is assumed but not designed.** The device implicitly assumes that the user-physician relationship will bridge the alert-to-action gap, but that bridge doesn't exist in the product. A primary care physician who receives a screenshot of a wearable alert has no validated protocol for responding to it, no liability framework for acting on consumer wellness data, and no reimbursement pathway for the resulting telehealth visit. The gap between "alert fired" and "clinical action taken" may be 6-8 weeks for a user who needs to schedule a cardiology appointment. Mitigation: partner with one telehealth cardiology platform for the launch cohort and design a direct "share with cardiologist" export that produces a report in a format clinicians actually use (PDF with waveform context, not a screenshot).

## The Smallest Experiment Toward the Most Ambitious Vision

* **The ambition may outpace the NPU's real-world capability.** Have we considered that the team's model of the NPU's inference budget is based on datasheet benchmarks, not measured performance under real sensor fusion loads? A two-week experiment that matters: instrument the actual current draw of simultaneous PPG sampling, accelerometer, and NPU inference on the target silicon under a 24-hour simulated wear profile. If the measured power budget differs from the datasheet estimate by more than 20%, the entire architecture is built on a false premise. This experiment costs one engineer two weeks and saves six months of rework.

* **The sprint-scale experiment that generates the most signal is on false positive rate, not accuracy.** The team can validate sensitivity against labeled arrhythmia datasets from PhysioNet today. What they cannot validate without a wearable prototype is the false positive rate generated by motion artifact on a real wrist. A focused experiment: recruit 10 subjects from your target demographic (adults 40+), have them wear a reference PPG sensor for 72 hours during normal life (including exercise and sleep), run the current model on that data, and count how many anomaly flags would have been user-visible alerts. If the answer is more than 3 per week per person, the alert architecture needs fundamental rethinking before any other design decision is meaningful.

* **The "18 months of runway" constraint makes the FDA question urgent, not deferred.** One potential challenge: the team positions FDA 510(k) exploration as a future consideration, but the experiment that should happen in the next two weeks is a pre-submission meeting request to FDA's Digital Health Center of Excellence. That meeting (free, available within 60-90 days of request) will tell you whether your intended consumer wellness claims cross into device territory and whether any data you collect during consumer launch will be acceptable as clinical evidence for a 510(k). Collecting consumer data under a wellness framing and then trying to repurpose it for regulatory submission is a known failure mode. The smallest experiment with the highest strategic value is a single conversation with FDA.

* **Model update mechanism is an unvalidated assumption with cascading risk.** The team's architecture likely assumes periodic OTA model updates as the primary mechanism for improving accuracy post-launch. A focused experiment: simulate one complete model update cycle — new weights, firmware packaging, OTA delivery, on-device validation, rollback — before the first consumer ship. The failure modes in this pipeline (weight corruption, inference regression, rollback failure on low-battery devices) are the ones that will generate a product recall, not the initial model accuracy. Two weeks of end-to-end update testing now is worth more than marginal accuracy improvements in the lab.

## Symbiotic Intelligence: A Genuinely Irreplaceable Device

* **"Irreplaceable by relationship" is a moat that requires data continuity you haven't guaranteed.** Have we considered that the entire premise of symbiotic intelligence — a device that becomes irreplaceable because it knows you — is destroyed by a single hardware failure, software reset, or device upgrade cycle? Apple Health has trained users to expect that their health data survives device transitions. A wearable that builds an intimate, individualized model but stores it only on-device with no migration path creates a perverse loyalty trap: the user is most dependent on the device precisely when they most need to replace it. Mitigation: design the personalized model as a portable, encrypted user artifact from day one — transferable to a new device, restorable from backup — even if the raw biometric data that trained it never leaves the hardware.

* **Intimacy without interpretability breeds distrust, not loyalty.** One potential challenge: a device that "knows you" but cannot explain what it knows is a black box wearing a wellness brand. For a user who has invested months of biometric data into a personalized model, the moment that model fires an unexpected alert — or fails to fire one — becomes a crisis of trust with no resolution path. "The device knows something is wrong" is terrifying; "the device has learned that your resting HRV normally drops before an arrhythmia episode, and it detected that pattern tonight" is actionable. Mitigation: the design philosophy for symbiotic intelligence must prioritize interpretable, user-readable model explanations over accuracy improvements that come at the cost of opacity.

* **Personalized on-device models create a regulatory classification problem that scales with ambition.** The more individualized and clinically meaningful the model becomes, the harder it is to claim it is a consumer wellness device. A model that genuinely predicts your specific arrhythmia pattern — not a population-level anomaly detector — is functioning as a diagnostic tool for a specific individual. FDA's Software as a Medical Device guidance is explicit that individualized diagnostic recommendations cross from wellness into medical device territory. The ambition of symbiotic intelligence and the regulatory strategy of staying in consumer wellness are on a collision course. Mitigation: engage FDA on this specific question during the pre-submission meeting; the answer may require a dual-product architecture where the wellness claims and the personalized clinical layer are legally separated.

* **The "no cloud system could replicate" claim ignores the competitive environment.** Have we considered that the device's competitive moat — an intimate, individualized model that lives only on-device — is precisely what a well-resourced cloud competitor would try to replicate, legally, by getting users to consent to cloud training? The personalization that takes your device 18 months to build from on-device data, a cloud system could build in 6 months from federated data across 10 million users. The "symbiotic" relationship is a moat only if the team can achieve personalization depth faster than cloud systems with vastly more data. Mitigation: define the specific personalization capabilities that on-device learning can achieve that are architecturally impossible for cloud systems — not just harder, but impossible — and design the product roadmap around those specific capabilities.
