# Topic Cluster 08: Equity, Access, and the People Being Served

## Questions

1. **Excluded populations beyond the stated "health-conscious 40+ with family cardiac history" framing — economic, geographic, and physical-accessibility dimensions**: When you map the full population of people who would benefit most from early arrhythmia and sleep apnea detection — those with limited access to cardiology care, those in rural areas, those without family health history due to adoption or estrangement, those on fixed incomes for whom a Series A hardware startup's price point is a genuine barrier, and those with dexterity limitations, visual impairments, or conditions like Parkinson's that affect how they interact with a wrist-worn wearable — how does the product's design, price point, companion app experience, and presumably-visual-and-touch-driven alert interface hold up for those people compared to those it was built for, and has the team modeled what health outcomes look like when the product is accessible only to the top two income quintiles of its target demographic?
2. **Smartphone access and digital literacy assumptions baked into the companion-app architecture**: If the wearable requires a smartphone to surface processed alerts and aggregated summaries, what happens to the older adult who owns a basic phone, shares a family device, or prefers no smartphone at all — and how does that design choice effectively re-gate the health intelligence behind a second device purchase that not every target user can make?
3. **Emotional state diversity at first use and the intimacy of sleep monitoring**: From the community's perspective, "health-conscious" is a heavily loaded filter — how might the device experience differ for someone newly health-anxious after a family cardiac event versus someone managing a chronic condition for years, what emotional state does the first-time user actually arrive in, and have users been asked what it feels like to know a microphone is listening to their breathing while they sleep — even if that data never leaves the device?
4. **Post-alert pathway and translating regulatory disclaimers into lived understanding**: The device generates an alert; the user receives it; and then what — what happens when a user lacks the infrastructure to act (no insurance, no primary care physician, no cardiologist in their geography) and the product creates a new health-information moment without creating the pathways to respond, and how is the limitation that the device is not FDA-cleared for clinical diagnosis translated from a legal disclaimer into a lived understanding of reliability without inducing either false security or health anxiety in the people most likely to be harmed by a false negative?
5. **Whose voice has shaped the design**: The design team is twelve engineers, and the stated target users are adults 40+ with cardiac family history — how many people from that demographic have participated in iterative design decisions, not just usability testing at the end, at what points in the architecture decisions (sensor selection, alert thresholds, notification language, data pipeline design) were actual people from the target demographic in the room, and what did they change?
6. **The device that earns genuine trust from a 40+ user**: Imagine it is three years from now and a 55-year-old with a family history of cardiac events tells a cardiologist that this device gave them a confidence in their own body they have never had before. What is the device doing — not just technically, but experientially — that produces that response? What strengths already visible in the current design are the seeds of that outcome?
