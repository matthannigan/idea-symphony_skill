## Synthesized Insights by Question

### Question 1: Model size, quantization, and the accuracy-energy curve on the NPU

**Full question**: What is the empirical relationship between parameter count, NPU inference latency, energy per inference, and arrhythmia/apnea detection F1 score on the target microcontroller, and at what INT8 / INT4 (or lower) quantization level do detection models begin to degrade in clinically meaningful ways — i.e., where on that curve does the design currently sit relative to the 7-day battery constraint, and how should the team determine the quantization threshold before committing to a silicon target?

* **Measure on real silicon before any quantization or silicon commitment; theoretical curves will mislead you.** All four perspectives converge here, with different emphases. The shared instruction is to run a short, bounded bring-up on actual dev boards (2-3 weeks) that measures latency, energy-per-inference, and F1 together rather than reasoning about them separately, and to treat a multi-day battery stress test as a gating artifact before tape-out. This convergence across a feasibility lens, a technical lens, a reductive lens, and an adversarial lens is the strongest signal in the cluster.
  * **Summary.** Establish a real battery footprint baseline on actual hardware before committing to silicon: build a firmware prototype on dev boards and stress-test for 10+ days under realistic usage, because aggressive quantization may force more frequent inference and erode the 7-day claim faster than calculations predict. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Start with off-the-shelf benchmarks.** Harvest published INT8/INT4 cardiac-detection studies (TinyML, ARM Cortex-M), adapt one proven 200K-500K parameter architecture, and run a 2-week validation sprint on the target NPU to surface silicon-specific latency surprises without months of exploration. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Summary.** Stop benchmarking the entire curve. Pick INT8, run three models (tiny/small/medium), and measure what breaks first on actual hardware now, F1 or battery life; the "empirically find the threshold" mindset assumes infinite time and silicon iterations you don't have. *—The Simplifier*
  * **Empirical characterization via silicon bring-up.** Run a design-of-experiments sweep over model sizes (2M-8M params), quantization levels (INT8/INT4/INT2), and inference frequencies; measure latency, energy, and F1 on a validation set at real arrhythmia prevalence (~2-5%), and plot the energy-vs-F1 Pareto frontier for product and regulatory stakeholders. Prioritize before firmware architecture freeze. *—The Technical Expert*

* **Default to INT8 and treat INT4 as suspect; the marginal energy savings rarely justify the clinical-signal loss.** Three perspectives agree that INT8 is the conservative operating point and that lower precision should not be adopted without field evidence. The technical lens supplies the quantitative shape (INT8 typically loses <2% F1, INT4 loses 5-8%); the reductive lens argues INT4 saves only 10-15% energy while harming detection; the feasibility lens recommends testing INT4 first but pivoting to INT8 the moment arrhythmia F1 drops below 0.85.
  * **Quantization-first threshold testing.** Work backward from the energy budget, test INT4 first, and if F1 drops below 0.85 for arrhythmia, pivot to INT8; document the exact clinical-grade accuracy floor before locking silicon. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Summary.** Quantization is a knob you'll turn the same way regardless: conservative, on INT8. INT4 likely saves only 10-15% energy while harming clinical signal detection in ways you'll regret at launch. *—The Simplifier*
  * **Empirical characterization via silicon bring-up.** Most teams find INT8 loses <2% F1 while INT4 loses 5-8%; the bring-up run reveals the empirical accuracy cliff. *—The Technical Expert*

* **Set the quantization floor from regulatory tier, not from energy alone; consumer wellness and 510(k) demand different F1 thresholds.** A distinctive single-persona reframe that reorders the decision: the acceptable precision is defined by which regulatory claim the product is making, so the quantization choice is a cross-functional negotiation rather than an ML optimization. This reframes Q1's "where on the curve" into "what clinical floor must the curve clear," and it ties quantization decisions to downstream retraining cost if clinical claims come later.
  * **Regulatory calibration of accuracy thresholds.** Consumer wellness can tolerate F1>85% for arrhythmia, but FDA 510(k) demands F1>95% with stratified performance across age/sex/BMI subgroups. Run a "quantization budget" conversation where product defines minimum F1, clinical/regulatory defines stratified floors, and firmware/ML jointly optimize to meet both, locking down risk before tape-out. *—The Technical Expert*

* **Validate on a clinically diverse cohort before launch; in-distribution benchmarks hide subgroup failures that become recalls.** Three perspectives raise the gap between lab performance and real-world cohorts, framing it as the dominant clinical risk. The adversarial lens names the specific failure (elderly irregular baselines, athletes with bradycardia, beta-blocker patients); the feasibility lens specifies a 20-30 user cohort with ECG gold-standard labels; the technical lens folds stratified subgroup performance into the regulatory floor.
  * **Summary.** Pre-launch, recruit a clinically diverse validation cohort (age, medications, underlying conditions) and explicitly profile model performance on each subgroup; optimizing for your training population risks high false-negative rates in a subgroup, forcing a costly recall. Quantization-induced drops may be masked by an internal test set and emerge catastrophically in the wild. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Staged model validation with real data.** Assemble a 20-30 user cohort with pre-labeled arrhythmia events from ECG gold standard, load quantized candidates onto dev boards, and measure false-positive rates under live motion artifacts and real-world noise, setting hard acceptance thresholds before manufacturing. *—The Pragmatist*

* **Preserve silicon optionality; late-stage quantization or vendor switches are expensive enough to design against now.** Three perspectives independently warn against premature silicon lock-in and recommend architectural decoupling. The mechanisms differ: a modular conversion pipeline that swaps quantization targets, parallel testing on two MCU+accelerator pairs, or a NAS that finds smaller models which sidestep a specific silicon commitment.
  * **Summary.** Architect the model pipeline to decouple quantization strategy from hardware commitment via a modular conversion pipeline, so you can swap quantization targets with minimal firmware overhead; choosing INT4 now could lock you into a silicon choice and reduce flexibility if better NPUs emerge within 18 months. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Competitive silicon hedge.** Test the quantization strategy on two MCU + neural-accelerator pairs (e.g., Snapdragon Wear + Cortex-M with Ethos-U); switching in Q3 is feasible, but committing now and missing budget means a 6-month redesign. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Power-aware model search with hardware constraints.** Run a NAS constrained by target latency (<500ms per inference on a 5-min window) and NPU memory (<4MB); it may discover lower-parameter models that beat INT8-quantized larger models and lets you sidestep premature silicon commitment. *—The Technical Expert*

* **Ship INT8 at launch, then optimize precision with field telemetry rather than guessing now.** Two perspectives recommend treating quantization as a staged decision: launch conservative, instrument the device, and let real-world false-positive/false-negative data justify any move to lower precision.
  * **Online calibration and periodic retraining strategy.** Plan quarterly/semi-annual firmware updates with retrained models; launch INT8, monitor real-world FP/FN rates, and shift to INT4 or mixed-precision only if field data supports it, using alert timestamps and user corrections as retraining signal. *—The Technical Expert*
  * **Summary.** Build a post-deployment monitoring pipeline that flags cohorts where F1 degrades beyond acceptable thresholds, with a clear escalation path to alert users before silent failures accumulate. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Keep the heavy layers low-precision and the decision boundary precise; mixed-precision recovers clinical nuance at small energy cost.** A distinctive technical option that splits the difference between INT8 and accuracy: quantize the convolutional backbone to INT8 while keeping the classification head in FP32.
  * **Integer-quantized backbone with selective full-precision heads.** Keep feature extraction in INT8 but classification heads in FP32, preserving decision-boundary nuance where clinical sensitivity/specificity matters while saving 60-70% energy in the heavier layers; battery impact is ~3-5% vs full INT8, and it reframes the question from "how low can we quantize" to "where is precision clinically critical." *—The Technical Expert*

### Question 2: Multi-task vs. single-task model design

**Full question**: Should arrhythmia and sleep apnea detection share a single multi-task model backbone with task-specific heads, or run as separate specialized models — and how does that choice ripple through battery budget, firmware update complexity, and false-positive rate management?

* **Launch with separate single-task models; the firmware, validation, and liability advantages outweigh the modest power cost of a shared backbone.** All four perspectives converge on this as the v1 answer, which is the cluster's clearest consensus. The shared logic: independent models can each be validated, updated, and hotpatched in isolation, which matters acutely for a 12-person team with 18 months of runway and for fast response to a post-launch false-positive surge. The technical lens quantifies the cost (separate models run ~8-12% hotter than multi-task) and notes firmware complexity is actually lower without shared gradient buffers or inter-task loss weighting.
  * **Summary.** Maintain separate models so a stable apnea detector isn't forced through retraining and revalidation every time arrhythmia needs an update; regulatory submissions may require separate validation packages per clinical claim, which is hard to isolate if tasks are entangled. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Start with single-task separation; unify later if needed.** Run arrhythmia and apnea as distinct 150K-250K parameter models for the first 6 months; each is independently validatable, firmware updates don't require retraining both, and multi-task probably won't save meaningful power for a 7-day target. Firmware complexity is the hidden cost of a shared backbone. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Summary.** Single model, single update path. Ship arrhythmia and apnea separately and update them separately; the winning scenario may be two frozen, validated models running three years, which is less risky than one clever model you'll update twice and break both times. *—The Simplifier*
  * **Separate specialized models for independent inference pipelines.** The tasks live on different temporal scales and modalities (arrhythmia in PPG intervals/QT variability; apnea in accelerometer/microphone breathing patterns), so a shared backbone compromises both; two ~3M-param INT8 models at <200ms each on disjoint threads cost ~8-12% more battery but give cleaner versioning and lower firmware complexity. *—The Technical Expert*

* **Watch for negative transfer; a shared backbone can let the dominant task quietly degrade the other.** Two perspectives flag that capacity competition in a multi-task model is a real, measurable risk, not just a theoretical one, and both recommend explicit testing before trusting a shared backbone.
  * **Summary.** Multi-task models can suffer negative transfer where apnea patterns degrade arrhythmia precision; run ablation studies disabling each task's loss to measure cross-task interference directly, and if apnea tuning hurts arrhythmia F1 by >3%, single-task becomes more defensible. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Multi-task backbone with decoupled output heads and independent thresholds.** A shared 4M-param backbone with task-specific heads saves ~20% energy and keeps alert routing independent, but risks overfitting to the more clinical arrhythmia task and degrading apnea; mitigate by weighting the multi-task loss and validating on apnea-enriched datasets. *—The Technical Expert*

* **Separating models multiplies false-positive surface, so pair them with explicit per-detector thresholds and a conflict story.** Three perspectives engage how the architecture choice routes false alerts, with a productive tension: the reductive and feasibility lenses argue separate models make false positives cleaner to own (each detector fires only past its own threshold), while the adversarial lens warns that two models can disagree and confuse users into disabling notifications.
  * **Summary.** Separate models multiply the surface area for false alerts and can disagree, creating ambiguous signal; establish a clear conflict-resolution protocol that ranks and communicates competing signals, and test user comprehension with early mockups before settling the architecture. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **False-positive liability leans toward isolation.** If the arrhythmia model over-fires post-launch, single-task models let you isolate, test, and deploy a fix fast without retraining apnea and risking regressions. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Summary.** Two separate models mean an alert fires only if the specific detector's confidence exceeds its threshold; multi-task introduces correlations that inflate false alarms when one modality glitches. *—The Simplifier*

* **If a shared backbone is ever pursued, isolate only the feature-extraction layer and keep heads fully independent.** Two perspectives offer the same middle path: capture duplicate-feature savings via a shared low-level extractor while preserving independent, separately-updatable task heads.
  * **Summary.** Decouple by maintaining separate models with a shared feature-extraction layer, letting you update task-specific heads independently while keeping regulatory scope tight. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Multi-task backbone with decoupled output heads and independent thresholds.** Share the convolutional backbone to reduce duplicate feature extraction but branch into independent heads with independent confidence thresholds; firmware is one model load, two inference calls. *—The Technical Expert*

* **Reserve unified single-model and conditional-dispatch designs for v2, when power is the binding constraint and thresholds are calibrated.** A technical lens maps two more aggressive architectures and explicitly times them as post-launch options, preserving the v1 simplicity consensus while keeping a power-optimization path open.
  * **Single unified model with confidence-weighted multiplexing.** One model outputting both confidences is most power-efficient (~25% saving) and simplest firmware, but couples both pipelines so a single update affects both and doubles testing burden; ideal only if power becomes critical post-launch. *—The Technical Expert*
  * **Hybrid architecture with task-conditional inference.** Dispatch to the arrhythmia or apnea model based on signal-quality heuristics, saving ~15-20% battery when switching fires regularly, but makes false-negative debugging harder; reserve for v2 after both models are field-validated. *—The Technical Expert*

### Question 3: Sensor fusion architecture, timing, and confidence weighting

**Full question**: Given that PPG, accelerometer, skin temperature, and microphone signals have different sampling rates and latency profiles and frequently disagree on classification, what fusion architecture (early fusion, late fusion, attention-based, or hybrid) best preserves the temporal relationships that distinguish true cardiac/respiratory events from motion artifacts, quantifies each modality's confidence contribution, and lets the multi-signal richness become a durable competitive moat as a platform for expanding clinical claims later — and what does that imply for on-device buffer management?

* **Default to late fusion for launch; it keeps latency low, stays interpretable for regulators, and lets you add sensors without retraining the whole stack.** All four perspectives land on late fusion (or a late-fusion-dominant hybrid) as the right v1 architecture, diverging only on how much learning to add. The convergent rationale: per-modality feature extraction runs in parallel on the NPU, each sensor's contribution to an alert is inspectable (valuable for FDA review), and new sensors plug in without a full retrain. Early fusion is reserved for later, only if extensive aligned multi-modal clinical data exists.
  * **Summary.** Late fusion is simpler to update than early fusion; consider a hybrid that fuses PPG+accelerometer early to eliminate motion artifacts and does late fusion for temperature and microphone, balancing interpretability with the temporal context that distinguishes real events from artifacts. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Late fusion with attention-based confidence gating.** Process all four sensors independently to raw feature vectors, then feed them through a lightweight 2-4 head, ~50K-param attention layer that learns task-specific confidence weights; late fusion keeps latency low (parallel pathways) and scales as you add sensors like ECG without retraining the backbone. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Summary.** Late fusion, no attention layer: PPG is the arrhythmia signal; accelerometer and temp are veto signals; microphone adds respiratory corroboration only when confident. A simple rule (PPG anomaly + accel quiet) runs in <50 lines, is debuggable, and needs no inference on every feature cross. *—The Simplifier*
  * **Temporal alignment with late fusion and per-modality confidence.** Run independent feature extractors per modality, compute per-modality anomaly scores (0-1), and fuse via a learned weighted combination (~500-param, 4-input dense net, negligible latency); interpretable and modular. *—The Technical Expert*

* **Align asynchronous sensors with a rolling timestamped buffer rather than chasing clock synchronization across drivers.** Three perspectives converge on a ring/rolling buffer that resamples disparate rates (PPG, accel, temp, microphone) to a common grid over a multi-second window, with the adversarial lens adding that drift must be actively monitored, not assumed away.
  * **Ring buffer for async sensor data alignment.** Implement a 5-second rolling buffer that timestamps each measurement and aligns via nearest-neighbor or linear interpolation, keeping temporal relationships intact without cross-driver clock sync; budget ~5KB, negligible against device memory. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Temporal alignment with late fusion and per-modality confidence.** Resample all modalities to a common 10Hz grid, compute rolling features over a 30-sec window (medical-grade anomaly needs temporal context), then invoke fusion; on-device buffering is ~2.4KB. *—The Technical Expert*
  * **Summary.** Sub-millisecond clock skew across PPG, accelerometer, microphone, and temperature subsystems compounds over hours and misaligns fusion; implement on-device time-alignment validation that flags drift beyond tolerance and triggers a re-sync routine, preventing silent fusion failures invisible in short lab tests. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Drop temperature and microphone and ship PPG+accel if it still clears the clinical bar; the simplest stack eliminates whole categories of state management.** A distinctive single-persona reframe that challenges the cluster's premise that four-sensor fusion is the goal. It reorders priorities: instead of architecting fusion for four streams, ask whether two sensors meet the bar, since fewer streams remove buffer management, timing jitter, and cross-modality drift entirely and make later platform expansion easier from a simpler base.
  * **Summary.** Drop temperature and microphone and ship with PPG + accel: you lose respiratory data and apnea detection weakens, but you cut buffer management, timing jitter, and cross-modality drift; if PPG-based arrhythmia detection still meets the clinical bar, you've eliminated entire categories of on-device state management, and platform expansion later is easier from a simpler base. *—The Simplifier*

* **Don't trust confidence scores until they're calibrated; neural nets are overconfident exactly on the unseen phenotypes that matter most.** Three perspectives treat confidence weighting as the fragile link in the fusion story. The adversarial lens warns calibration must be validated and out-of-distribution detection added; the feasibility lens grounds thresholds empirically in the user cohort; the technical lens supplies the attention mechanism that adapts weights dynamically to signal conditions.
  * **Summary.** Neural networks are overconfident on out-of-distribution data; benchmark confidence calibration against ground truth and implement explicit OOD detection (e.g., Mahalanobis distance in embedding space). If confidence scores can't be trusted, the entire weighting architecture is fragile. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Confidence-weighted alert thresholding for clinical readiness.** Compute a fusion-level confidence (0-1) from attention weights: fire if PPG+accelerometer agree above 0.7, hold at 0.5 and aggregate over 30 seconds if only PPG agrees; document thresholds empirically with the 20-user cohort to keep false positives below 2% while preserving sensitivity. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Attention-based sensor weighting for motion artifact rejection.** A transformer fusion layer learns per-timestep attention so high accelerometer motion downweights noise-prone PPG; ~50-100ms and ~15% power cost buys substantially better specificity against motion artifacts. Recommended for medium/high-effort designs; low-effort can skip it. *—The Technical Expert*

* **Start with a transparent rule engine over per-sensor detectors; logical gates are the most defensible fusion for regulatory review.** Three perspectives propose, for v1, replacing learned fusion with explicit per-sensor anomaly detectors combined by readable rules, buying interpretability and time to collect field data before upgrading to learned fusion.
  * **Modular fusion with per-sensor anomaly cascade.** Each sensor feeds its own detector, then a rule engine combines them ("alert only if PPG_arrhythmia > 0.8 AND accel_motion < 0.3"); maximally interpretable for regulatory review, minimal buffering, and buys time to refine thresholds before upgrading to learned fusion in v2. *—The Technical Expert*
  * **Summary.** Write three if-then rules (PPG change magnitude + accel variance below threshold + skin temp stable = real event), train the decision boundary on historical data, then freeze it; no buffers, no timing complexity. The moat isn't fancy fusion, it's the sensor hardware and sampling precision competitors can't copy. *—The Simplifier*
  * **Summary.** Establish a clear conflict-resolution protocol defining how the device ranks and communicates competing signals, tested with user mockups before settling architecture. *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Design the fusion layer as a modular plugin so future sensors and clinical claims don't force a full retrain or topology rewrite.** Three perspectives treat extensibility as a first-class requirement, framing today's fusion topology as a future liability if it can't absorb new sensors cleanly.
  * **Summary.** Design the fusion layer as a modular plugin system with a clear interface where new sensors (EEG for seizures, EMG for muscle events) can be added with minimal retraining, avoiding forced backward-compatibility breaks; adding sensors to a locked fusion topology otherwise requires retraining the entire model. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Modality importance as a competitive advantage.** Turn fusion confidence scores into a "sensor importance dashboard" for future FDA submissions, demonstrating multi-signal harmony as a moat against single-sensor competitors; log confidence contributions anonymized (no raw biometrics) to enable later federated learning without privacy leaks. *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Hierarchical buffer management with adaptive windowing.** Use adaptive windowing that extends the buffer to 60 seconds when anomaly confidence rises and shrinks to 30 seconds otherwise, saving RAM in low-risk states while giving rich context exactly when borderline cases emerge; buffer ~10-20KB. *—The Technical Expert*
