### Question 1: Consumer wellness framing as a one-way door

Stand up immutable raw-sensor logging, algorithm versioning, audit trails, and clinical-grade threshold justification at launch even if those capabilities stay invisible to consumers. The unifying move is to decouple what the system is built to do from what the marketing says it does, so the consumer interface hides FDA-ready rigor rather than papering over its absence. This front-loaded investment (roughly 2-3 engineers for 3 months) is what prevents the costly retrofit that FDA scrutiny punishes.

  * **Flip the starting point: architect everything for clinical-grade excellence, then deliberately choose not to market those capabilities, so the clinical moment changes only which stakeholders you address, not the product.** Original detail from The Visionary. *—The Visionary*

### Question 5: Imagine the 510(k) succeeds and ruins everything

* **The model-freeze problem is real: clearance locks the algorithm, and the privacy advantage can become a clinical straitjacket.** Strong convergence across all four perspectives. Once FDA clears a specific model version, improvements require new submissions (estimated 6-12 months and $200K-500K each), so on-device ML marketed as a privacy advantage risks clinical obsolescence while cloud rivals iterate freely. All perspectives treat this as a genuine strategic risk that must be designed around before filing, not after.
  * **FDA approval freezes the model; competitors update 20 times while you are stuck, and the on-device privacy advantage becomes a clinical straitjacket.** Original detail from The Devil's Advocate. *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Clearance freezes the algorithm; a better model needs a new 510(k) at 6-12 months and $200K-500K, so on-device ML becomes clinically obsolete because you cannot update without approval.** Original detail from The Lawyer. *—The Lawyer*
  * **The model-freeze problem is real and worth taking seriously.** Original detail from The Pragmatist. *—The Pragmatist*

### Question 7: The handoff to the cardiologist

**Full question**: Picture the moment a user's device-detected arrhythmia pattern becomes clinically significant enough that their doctor wants to see the data — how does the narrative shift from personal wellness tool to clinical instrument, and what friction points in that handoff (consent, data format, liability framing) would most erode trust on both sides?

* **At handoff the wellness narrative collapses and the device becomes a clinical instrument; the liability framing shifts and must be made explicit.** Strong convergence across all four perspectives. The moment a user shares device data with a cardiologist, wellness disclaimers stop applying and the device functions as a clinical information source.
