# False Positive/Negative Economics & Alert Design - The Lawyer

---

## Asymmetric Cost of False Positives and False Negatives Across Stakeholders

* **Liability asymmetry favors false positives at consumer stage.** Today, as a consumer wellness device, a false negative (missed arrhythmia) carries reputational risk and potential negligence exposure if a user later proves the device failed to flag a detectable event — but this liability is constrained because you made no medical claim. A false positive sends someone to the ER unnecessarily; their legal remedy is limited (consumer protection, maybe unjust enrichment). Document that your sensitivity threshold was calibrated for consumer wellness, not clinical diagnosis. Once clinical partnership begins, flip: FDA 510(k) review locks your claimed performance; false negatives become regulatory violations and product liability claims. Threshold policy must explicitly state: who bears cost (user bears ER cost for false positive; company bears reputational cost for false negative), and what escalation protocol triggers company liability (e.g., documented failure to flag known-pattern arrhythmia).

* **Design informed consent to allocate cost by disclosure.** If Elena goes to the ER for a false positive, her legal claim depends on whether you disclosed the false positive rate. Best practice: in-app transparency showing model performance (e.g., "flags ~2% of events that are non-arrhythmic"). This doesn't eliminate ER copay risk, but it shifts burden from strict liability (you promised accuracy) to assumption of risk (she knew the limitation). This also protects against ER system claims (they can't sue you for their resource cost if you disclosed the alert uncertainty). Build this disclosure into the Terms of Service and the alert UI itself — "Alert: possible but not confirmed arrhythmia."

* **Stakeholder cost distribution clarifies governance structure.** Lay out matrix: user (emotional cost, ER copay for false positive; clinical harm risk for false negative); family (false positive = unnecessary worry; false negative = missed intervention window); ER system (false positive = wasted bed, lab draw; false negative liability is low if no clinical claim); company (false positive = reputation erosion, some negligence exposure; false negative = product liability, FDA enforcement if clinical). Once you enter clinical partnerships, add hospital (liability for alert-driven clinical decisions) and FDA (regulatory enforcement). This matrix informs your sensitivity choice: at consumer stage, accept higher false positive rate to minimize clinical harm litigation. At clinical stage, FDA guidance (e.g., American Heart Association thresholds for arrhythmia detection) governs tradeoff.

## The Sensitivity Ratchet and Alert Fatigue Feedback Dynamic

* **Document initial sensitivity decision as design boundary.** The sensitivity ratchet embeds when users anchor to early performance. Legally, this matters because changing sensitivity downward later (to reclaim battery or improve specificity) looks like "we broke the device," triggering product liability claims. Mitigation: at launch, explicitly document the sensitivity threshold in Terms of Service and firmware version history. When you later adjust sensitivity, frame it as a feature update with user control — e.g., "Alert Sensitivity: High / Standard / Minimal" toggle. This converts a "downgrade" into a choice, reducing liability exposure. Also: A/B test sensitivity with cohorts early, so you can claim the final threshold was user-validated, not post-hoc ratchet-breaking.

* **Alert fatigue creates negligence exposure if documented but ignored.** If your UX research shows alert fatigue causes users to dismiss real alerts, and you ignore that data, you've documented your own negligence. Protect yourself: if field data shows false positive rate causing alert dismissal, change the threshold immediately and log the change. This shows due care. If you see the pattern and don't act, a user whose real alert got ignored has a strong claim. Solution: build a feedback loop into the product — track which alerts users confirm vs. dismiss, flag when dismissal rate exceeds X%, and trigger automatic threshold review (documented in internal governance). This transforms negligence into managed risk.

* **Latency expectations lock in compliance burden.** If users expect sub-5-second detection because early firmware achieved it, walking that back to 15 seconds for battery reasons creates liability (breach of implied warranty). Protect yourself: clearly separate performance guarantees from observed performance. In Terms of Service, state: "Detection latency may vary 5–30 seconds depending on sensor sampling rate and power mode." Don't let the device advertise "rapid detection" — say "timely detection" with ranges. Update firmware release notes to show latency tradeoff clearly ("increased latency by ~3 seconds to extend battery by 15%"). This prevents users from claiming surprise or breach.

## The Lived Experience of Alerts at 3am, in the ER, and at the Threshold of Probabilistic Uncertainty

* **Medical communication standard under FDA guidance.** When FDA reviews your consumer device for clinical transition, they will scrutinize alert phrasing under the standard for "minimally trained operators" — i.e., can a lay user distinguish "medical attention advised" from "call 911 now" without over-reacting? Best practice inspired by FDA guidance: use tiered language: "Low confidence: possible irregularity (no action needed)" / "Moderate confidence: irregular pattern detected (consider consulting your doctor)" / "High confidence: arrhythmia detected (seek medical attention)." Couple this with UX research showing that these phrases don't trigger panic-driven ER visits. Document this research; it's your defense against claims that you caused emotional or financial harm through unclear messaging.

* **Liability turns on "should a reasonable person have called 911."** If Marcus wakes to an alert and rushes to the ER, your liability depends on whether a reasonable 54-year-old with family cardiac history would have made the same choice given your alert phrasing. Protect yourself: surface confidence thresholds visually — high-confidence alerts show a red icon and "seek medical attention"; moderate-confidence show yellow and "consult your doctor"; low-confidence show blue and "log for your doctor's review." This creates a defensible standard of care. Also: provide a "Recommended next step" field in the alert UI that reflects the confidence level. If a user ignores a low-confidence alert that says "no action needed" and goes to the ER, that choice is theirs, not yours.

* **Probabilistic uncertainty requires epistemic humility in UI design.** Your ML model produces a probability (arrhythmia present: 73%). But communicating "73%" to a scared user at 3am is medically irresponsible — they'll either panic or dismiss. Instead: convert probability into decision-relevant categories (high/moderate/low confidence) backed by UX research showing users understand the difference. Document that your confidence thresholds were informed by conversation cardiologists and tested with lay users. This converts raw model output into clinically responsible communication. If liability later arises, you can show that your communication standard matched medical practice norms.

## Alert Calibration as Nuclear Deterrence Equilibrium

* **Graduated response architecture mirrors graduated escalation doctrine.** Your tiered alerts (low-confidence sleep apnea flag vs. high-confidence cardiac alert) embody the same logic as Cold War deterrence: credibility requires proportionality. Legally: if you alert for every micro-arrhythmia (including physiologic noise), users ignore alerts, and real arrhythmias go undetected, you've failed the standard of care. If you alert only for severe arrhythmias, you miss early intervention windows. Defense: document that your alert thresholds were set using graduated-response logic: low-confidence flags drive self-monitoring (user logs in companion app), moderate-confidence drive physician consultation, high-confidence drive immediate medical attention. This structure defends against negligence claims from both directions (too many alerts / too few alerts) because you can show each tier serves a distinct clinical purpose.

* **Liability exposure shifts with escalation pathway.** A low-confidence sleep apnea flag that prompts self-logging carries minimal liability because you're not driving medical action. A high-confidence cardiac alert that prompts ER visit carries significant liability because you're influencing a costly, emotionally fraught medical decision. Tiered escalation reduces this by setting user expectations. Your Terms of Service should state: "High-confidence cardiac alerts warrant medical evaluation; low-confidence flags are for personal wellness tracking." This distinction protects you because the user who received a low-confidence alert cannot claim you promised a diagnosis.

* **Deterrence credibility requires consistent thresholds.** If your model frequently alerts at 65% confidence but occasionally at 40% confidence (depending on sensor noise), users lose trust — the deterrent fails. Legally, inconsistency creates negligence exposure. Solution: hard-code alert thresholds in firmware (e.g., cardiac alert triggers only if confidence > 75%, sleep apnea alert only if > 60%). Document these thresholds in release notes and user documentation. If you change them, that's a versioned release, not a silent recalibration. This demonstrates that you maintained a consistent standard, not that you secretly tweaked sensitivity after user complaints.

## The 3 a.m. Alert

* **Crisis communication standard: clarity over reassurance.** When Marcus wakes to a vibration, your alert UI has ~3 seconds to establish credibility before he either panics or dismisses the alert. The legal standard is informed consent under time pressure — he must understand enough to make a decision he'd later defend. Best practice: the initial alert shows only what matters (cardiac irregularity detected; consider medical evaluation) and avoids both false reassurance ("probably nothing") and catastrophizing ("possible heart attack"). Include a confidence indicator (e.g., color-coded: yellow = moderate). Link to a "What should I do?" guide that explains options (call nurse hotline, go to ER, wait and monitor, contact your doctor) based on confidence level. Document that this UX was tested with 40–50-year-olds with cardiac risk factors.

* **Liability hinges on decision support, not panic control.** You cannot be responsible for Marcus's emotional state, but you are responsible for giving him decision-relevant information. Your alert should communicate: (1) what was detected (cardiac irregularity), (2) confidence level (based on model probability), (3) recommended next step (call your doctor, consider ER visit). If your alert says "seek medical attention" and he ignores it, that's his choice. If your alert is vague ("something unusual detected") and he panics unnecessarily, that's your failure. Protect yourself by testing alert phrasing with actual users and cardiologists — document that Marcus (or test participants like him) understood the alert intent and felt equipped to decide.

* **Trust calibration requires both accuracy and consistency.** After the first alert, Marcus judges whether to trust the next one based on this one. If this alert was correct (he had an arrhythmia), trust increases. If it was a false positive and the ER found nothing, trust erodes. You can't control the first outcome, but you can control the second by not inflating confidence thresholds. Also: provide post-alert follow-up in the app ("Device detected an arrhythmia; we'd like to know the outcome if you're comfortable sharing"). This lets you track false positive rates and adjust transparently. If Marcus can see that the device's accuracy is being tracked, he's more likely to trust future alerts.

## The False Alarm Aftermath

* **Product liability for emotional harm requires foreseeability standard.** When Elena receives a cardiac alert and rushes to the ER only to learn her heart is fine, she has suffered financial harm (copay, labs) and emotional harm (fear, family worry). Your liability depends on whether this outcome was foreseeable given your model's false positive rate. If your model is known to have a 5% false positive rate and you provide that information in the app, Elena assumed some risk — her claim is weaker. If you claimed "medically accurate detection" without disclosing false positive rate, liability is stronger. Defense: transparent disclosure of model performance (e.g., "alerts correctly identify ~95% of true arrhythmias; ~3% of alerts are false positives"). Document this in Terms of Service, in-app, and at setup.

* **Relationship damage is foreseeable; design for recovery.** Elena's erosion of trust in the device is not a legal claim, but it's a product failure you can mitigate. After a false positive, the app should: (1) acknowledge the alert may have been inaccurate; (2) offer a post-ER survey ("How did things turn out?"); (3) provide reassurance without minimizing ("False positives are rare but do happen; we're working to reduce them"); (4) give control back to her ("You can now adjust your alert sensitivity: high/standard/low"). This UX recovery reduces her likelihood of abandoning the device and improves the data you collect on false positive rates. Legally, this also shows you took the emotional impact seriously, which reduces punitive damages if litigation arises.

* **ER cost recovery: design to prevent over-escalation.** Elena's ER visit was triggered by your alert, but your legal responsibility is limited to whether your alert was medically irresponsible. If you labeled it "high confidence cardiac alert," you bear some responsibility for her ER decision. If you labeled it "moderate confidence; medical evaluation recommended," she shares decision-making responsibility. To minimize false-positive ER visits: (1) use high-confidence thresholds (e.g., 75%+) for alerts that drive ER escalation; (2) in the alert UI, provide a "Call my doctor before going to ER" option that logs her call and decision; (3) offer a 24/7 nurse hotline (outsourced to a medical service, not your company) so she can talk to a clinician before deciding. The nurse hotline also reduces your liability because Elena's decision becomes informed by a licensed clinician, not just your device.

## Designed to Misread

* **Incentive alignment reversal creates fraud exposure.** If your company intentionally tuned the model for false positives because you wanted users to visit doctors more often (assuming healthier outcomes), you have crossed into fraud and potentially healthcare fraud. The FDA, FTC, and state AGs would view this as deceptive — you're claiming your device helps users make health decisions, but you're actually manipulating their behavior to increase medical spending. This is distinct from simply choosing a false-positive-friendly threshold for legitimate reasons (conservative caution, early detection priority). If your intent was to manipulate, documentation will expose it. Protect yourself: ensure any sensitivity decision is documented with legitimate rationale (clinical conservatism, user preference, battery/accuracy tradeoff) and approved by your medical/clinical advisor, not marketing or revenue teams.

* **Accuracy is not a "good" — health outcomes are.** The deeper insight: if false positives make users healthier on average (earlier detection, lifestyle changes), then "high false positive rate = good outcome" is a real possibility. Legally, this is a defense against product liability claims if you can show that users who received false positive alerts actually had better health outcomes. But this requires data and expert testimony. If Elena claims emotional harm from a false positive, you can argue: "Yes, but data shows users receiving such alerts visit physicians more, catch issues earlier, and have better 3-year cardiovascular outcomes." This is a strong defense, but it requires prospective study design (not post-hoc analysis). Design your data collection to track outcomes, not just alert accuracy.

* **Standard of care is context-dependent.** In consumer wellness, the standard of care is lower — you're helping users monitor, not diagnose. A higher false positive rate is acceptable. In clinical settings (FDA 510(k), hospital partnerships), the standard of care requires sensitivity/specificity balances aligned with medical practice. If your device is used in both contexts, you need context-aware thresholds. Legally: document which standard of care applies in each setting, and have your clinical advisor approve thresholds for each. If a claim later arises, you can show you met the appropriate standard, not an inappropriately low one.

## From Alert to Dialogue

* **Paradigm shift liability: informed consent to ongoing relationship.** Moving from discrete alerts to continuous, ambient communication between body and device changes the legal relationship from transaction (device told you something; you acted) to advisory (device is always teaching you to interpret your body). This is a liability shift because now the user's ability to "read" their body depends entirely on your device's teaching. If the user misinterprets the ambient signal and makes a harmful decision, your liability is higher because you created the interpretive framework. Protect yourself: make clear that this continuous communication is educational, not diagnostic. Users should still have periodic clinical check-ins to verify their device-based self-understanding. Also: build in explicit checks — periodic prompts ("Have you seen a clinician in the past 6 months?") — to prevent users from treating the device as a replacement for medical advice.

* **Governance of novel communication modalities requires clinical oversight.** A "continuous, ambient language" between body and device is not an alert — it's a new medical communication paradigm. Before launching, you need: (1) clinical advisors who endorse the paradigm; (2) user research showing users understand the language without misinterpreting it; (3) safety protocols if the user misinterprets (e.g., emergency fallback to discrete alerts if ambient signal is not recognized for 48 hours). Document all of this as a clinical governance decision, not just a UX design choice. If FDA later reviews your device, this documentation shows you considered the clinical implications of a novel modality.

* **Liability reduction through transparency about uncertainty.** The current alert paradigm (discrete: "arrhythmia detected") implies binary certainty. An ambient language paradigm could reduce anxiety by acknowledging uncertainty continuously ("your pattern today looks similar to yesterday; still learning your baseline"). This transparency about uncertainty is a liability reduction because users won't be shocked when the device later seems "wrong" — they'll understand that the device is always in learning mode. Legally, this mirrors the shift from "diagnosis" (binary) to "biomarker monitoring" (continuous, uncertain). Frame your communication in the latter terms. In Terms of Service, state: "This device learns your unique baseline over time; patterns that look abnormal to the device may be normal for you."
