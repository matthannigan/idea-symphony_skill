# Topic Cluster 05: Model Update Strategy & Long-Term Maintainability — The Systems Thinker

---

## Update Mechanism Unit Economics: On-Device Fine-Tuning vs. Federated Learning vs. Firmware OTA

* **Summary.** The three approaches create fundamentally different stakeholder feedback loops. Firmware OTA appears economical upfront but creates a "Shifting the Burden" trap: the easy route is pushing new models to all users indiscriminately, which erodes user confidence in alert patterns (users drift toward distrust), requiring more supporting communication and customer service to manage confusion. The real cost compounds silently. Federated learning inverts this: high upfront engineering overhead to build the infrastructure, but once operational it distributes learning cost across the fleet—meaning the more devices you have, the cheaper learning becomes per device. On-device fine-tuning sits between but requires users to opt in; non-participants become a growing subpopulation running older models, widening divergence in alert behavior across your user base—a quality variance problem that shows up in liability discussions if different users get different detection fidelity from identical symptoms.

* **Summary.** Watch for oscillation traps: if federated learning infrastructure fails or lags, the team reflexively falls back to OTA updates as a "quick fix," which reduces incentive to fix the federated system, which makes future outages more likely. A stabilizing counter: build federated learning redundancy early, when failures are cheap, so the organization never faces a crisis moment where going backward feels necessary. Design for graceful degradation—if federated infrastructure is down, users get high-confidence alerts only (fewer false positives, lower sensitivity) rather than device-wide alerts. That's a clear failure signal, not silent continued operation.

* **Summary.** The hardware specification question reveals a systems tension: pushing federated fine-tuning too far downmarket (into devices with minimal RAM/NPU) forces you to use simpler gradient compression or reduced local training, which increases the error rate in personalized models, which increases false positives and user frustration—at exactly the price point where user support costs matter most. The leverage point: start federated learning at a hardware tier where local training is robust, even if it shrinks your addressable market initially. As you scale and manufacturing costs drop, you can expand downmarket to simpler hardware, but only after proving the personalization mechanism works at scale with better hardware.

* **Summary.** The versioning contract you propose—split-image firmware/model versioning—needs a self-policing mechanism. Require every model update to include a "regression test suite" against synthetic patient trajectories that previously triggered alerts—run it on device before accepting the update, fail gracefully if alert patterns change beyond a threshold. This makes unexpected behavioral drift visible at update time, not six months later in post-market surveillance data. It's a built-in governor that prevents silent creep.

## Model Drift and Personalization Drift Without Raw-Data Feedback Loops

* **Summary.** This is a classic "Tragedy of the Commons" playing out in slow motion: the device becomes less accurate over time, but accuracy degradation is invisible to individual users (they don't know their baseline has shifted) and invisible to your team (no raw data feedback). Each user silently tolerate lower sensitivity because they've adapted their expectations, until suddenly someone misses a real event. The feedback loop is broken at exactly the point where you need it most. The only counter: build recalibration into the use pattern itself. Prompt users to re-baseline every 6 months (sync context: "Your health patterns may have changed — we'll recalibrate your personalized sensitivity in 2 minutes"). Frame it as maintenance, not a confession of model failure. Make recalibration fast and frictionless enough that users see it as helpful, not punishment.

* **Summary.** A proxy feedback mechanism: aggregate device-level alerts (which you do have) across your fleet and compare alert rates per user cohort over time. If users aged 45-50 suddenly show 40% fewer daily alerts than they did 12 months ago, something in the population's physiology changed or the model drifted. Use cohort-level drift detection to trigger recalibration guidance for entire user segments, even though you can't pinpoint which individual needs it. This turns missing raw data into an inferential advantage—you're less vulnerable to overfitting to sensor artifacts because you're optimizing against fleet-wide behavioral patterns, not individual noise.

* **Summary.** Federated fine-tuning addresses this directly: users who opt in can contribute gradient updates that encode their long-term trajectory without ever sharing raw data. A user whose heart rate baseline rose 5 bpm over 18 months sends a gradient update reflecting that drift; the model adapts. But this creates a new risk: users who don't opt in become a control group running on outdated baselines. Over time, you'll have two populations with different alert sensitivities, and you won't know which is "correct" when a cohort diverges. The stabilizing mechanism: make federated learning opt-out instead of opt-in, with strong privacy transparency (the gradient itself is noise-injected and can't reveal individual data), so most users are implicitly participating. Then you have a single, evolving standard of "normal" that the fleet self-corrects toward.

* **Summary.** The clinical partnership scenario (cardiologists confirming arrhythmia events) reveals a high-impact leverage point: don't try to close the feedback loop for all users—close it for the small percentage in your clinical trial or partnership cohorts. Share those confirmed outcomes only with the research team, use them to identify model drift patterns, and push corrective updates to the broader consumer fleet. This is a "just-in-time correction" loop: clinical data informs one global model, which then distributes to all devices via firmware or federated learning. You're not trying to personalize every user; you're using clinical truth to keep the shared model honest.

## Firmware Update as a Trust Event and Governance Question

* **Summary.** Every update that changes detection behavior is a trust transaction with your user—you're asking them to re-calibrate their expectations of what "normal" alert frequency means. If updates are frequent and silent, users slowly lose confidence in alert signals because the baseline keeps shifting. The reinforcing cycle: users mistrust alerts → users disable notifications or ignore them → you're less able to assess real alert accuracy in the field → your team makes bigger model changes → users distrust more. Breaking this requires transparency tied to update timing. Announce model updates quarterly (a predictable rhythm) and explain what changed in human terms: "The September update will reduce false alarms in early-morning heart rate spikes by 30%." Let users see the benefit before they experience the change. That moves updates from invisible maintenance to "good news" events.

* **Summary.** The non-updater problem compounds: 6 months in, 30% of your user base is running outdated models because of storage constraints or poor connectivity. These users now have systematically different alert patterns than the 70% on the latest version. If you publish clinical evidence or regulatory claims based on the current model, the 30% aren't covered by that evidence—and you may not even know which users are non-updated. The governance answer: tie firmware updates to capability tiers, not mandatory adoption. "Standard" version gets quarterly updates; "Wellness Lite" version has annual updates. Users choose their tier, and you manage evidence and liability per tier. No one is silently misaligned.

* **Summary.** Model update governance needs a "circuit breaker" rule: if post-update alert rates diverge by >20% from the previous week, the device automatically rolls back and signals the team for investigation. This prevents the scenario where a bad update silently goes out and degrades performance across 50,000 devices before anyone notices. The rollback is self-correcting; the signal is actionable. It trades responsiveness (you can't push small, frequent updates) for safety (you catch failures immediately).

* **Summary.** Recourse for users experiencing unexpected alert pattern changes: offer a "revert to previous model" option in settings for 30 days after any major update, with clear labeling ("this version is no longer supported; use at your own risk"). This is not a long-term solution, but it gives users agency—they can test the new model and opt back if alert patterns feel wrong. After 30 days, force forward. The transparency mechanism is the key: let users experience the difference and report it, rather than forcing blind adaptation. The data from "users who reverted" becomes a signal for your model evaluation team.

## Federated Learning as Just-in-Time Manufacturing

* **Summary.** The lean manufacturing analogy reveals a critical failure mode: supply disruptions. In Toyota's system, a single supplier delay cascades through the entire production line because there's no buffer. In federated learning, if a cohort of users (say, night-shift workers) goes offline during their typical gradient-sync window, their physiological patterns stop being represented in the next model update. The new model optimizes toward the 90% who do sync regularly, and the 10% who are offline become less well-served. The manufacturing analogy suggests the counter: asynchronous aggregation with decay weights. Gradient updates from last week are less influential than updates from today, but they're never zero—you're always working with the fleet's recent history, not a snapshot. This is the equivalent of maintaining "safety stock" of historical data.

* **Summary.** Quality variance across "parts" (user physiologies) is the real lever. If you're training a federated model where users range from 20-year-old athletes to 70-year-old patients with pacemakers, heart-rate baselines diverge by 200%+. A gradient update from the athlete pulls the model in one direction; the pacemaker patient pulls another. A naive federated system trains toward the "average," which serves no one well. The lean manufacturing parallel suggests "standardized work"—in manufacturing, different products follow different process specs. In federated learning, run separate model streams per demographic cohort (athlete, cardiac patient, sedentary). Each stream aggregates gradients from similar physiology. You're running multiple "production lines," not one, so quality variance doesn't drive oscillation.

* **Summary.** Toyota's system works because suppliers are tightly coupled to demand via daily kanban signals—feedback is immediate, tight, and unambiguous. Federated learning has a fundamental lag: a user's gradient update arrives in batch at 3 AM, gets aggregated with millions of others, and the new model deploys 24 hours later. Users don't see the effect for another 24 hours (if they update). A true lean system would be weekly model updates with asynchronous, continuous gradient ingestion—but that requires 10x more edge infrastructure and real-time aggregation pipelines. Most teams can't sustain that. The honest leverage point: accept that federated learning is "batch-lean," not real-time lean. Optimize for weekly cadence, not daily. That's still faster than quarterly OTA updates and cheaper than running raw data pipelines.

* **Summary.** The analogy breaks when you consider consent and agency. In Toyota's system, suppliers follow the kanban without choice—it's contractual. In federated learning, users opt in, and their "supply" (gradient contributions) is voluntary and revocable. Users who opt out create a missing-data pattern that's harder to predict than a supplier delay—you don't know when they'll go offline or if they'll stay in the system. A manufacturing analogy: you're running a supply chain where 40% of suppliers can unplug without notice. The only stabilizer is accepting high variance and designing models that are robust to missing cohorts. Use gradient robustness testing: train with synthetic data gaps (simulate users dropping out) and measure model degradation. If the model is sensitive to losing one cohort, it's not ready for federated learning at that scale.
