# Topic Cluster 05: Model Update Strategy & Long-Term Maintainability - The Technical Expert

---

## Update Mechanism Unit Economics: On-Device Fine-Tuning vs. Federated Learning vs. Firmware OTA

* **Summary: Firmware OTA dominates initially; federated learning requires multi-year capability roadmap.** From a technical perspective, periodic firmware-delivered updates are the path of least resistance for 18-month runway. Federated learning requires persistent on-device gradient computation—potentially 15-30% battery overhead in your power budget—plus orchestration infrastructure, secure aggregation, and differential privacy engineering. For immediate launch: baseline OTA cadence quarterly, storage-versioned (firmware v2.1_modelA vs. v2.1_modelB), CI/CD validation of detection behavior regression. Realistic federated minimum: Cortex-M7 + 8MB SRAM + external flash for gradient buffers. Total engineering lift: ~6 months ML + 3 months infrastructure for your team size.

* **Summary: Stratify by update category—safety-critical hotfixes vs. performance optimization.** On-device fine-tuning exists in a middle ground: pre-load a lightweight adapter module that recalibrates on-device without retraining full weights. Adapter storage: ~500KB. Compute: 2-4 hours of periodic background sync. Cost model: safety patches bypass OTA queue (direct push to users in sync metadata), performance updates batch quarterly. This de-risks firmware blast-radius while maintaining clinical defensibility—you're updating hyperparameters, not detection thresholds. Requires version contract: each firmware release pins a supported {model version, adapter version} pair.

* **Summary: Bandwidth costs dominate at fleet scale; OTA remains practical to 500K users.** A typical firmware OTA payload (model weights + code deltas) runs 8-12 MB. At 100K active users, 30% daily sync rate = ~360GB outbound bandwidth monthly (~$3K AWS bill). Federated learning flips the cost structure: instead of model upload, you distribute gradient aggregation—but coordinate 30K concurrent users every 7 days, each uploading 2MB gradient snapshots = massive orchestration complexity. OTA practical threshold: maintain until 500K users. Beyond that, federated learning becomes economically competitive, but infrastructure cost doesn't justify until user base justifies clinical-grade model iteration velocity.

* **Summary: Version contract must decouple firmware releases from model behavior changes.** A split-image design prevents silent behavior drift: firmware v2.1 explicitly declares "ships with model-2026-02-15-v3, adapter index 12." Post-market surveillance tracks {user_id, firmware_version, model_version, alert_count_weekly}. When investigating a cluster of false positives among v2.0 users, root cause attribution is clean. For 510(k) submissions, document each model version's validation cohort, alert characteristics, and false positive rate—demonstrating that updates improved or maintained known safety margins. Minimal DevOps overhead: automated CI/CD pipeline that compares new model's ROC curves against production baseline.

## Model Drift and Personalization Drift Without Raw-Data Feedback Loops

* **Summary: Implement on-device baseline tracking and user-prompted recalibration events.** Without raw-data upload, prevention requires passive drift detection: track user's rolling 30-day median heart rate, SpO₂ resting level, and sleep duration as a "signature." When baseline shifts >15% (e.g., post-fitness improvement, new medication), the app prompts "Your patterns have changed—recalibrate?" A 5-minute supervised calibration session (user performs controlled breathing exercises while device learns personalized thresholds) re-anchors detection. This keeps users in the loop: they understand why the device behaves differently, and clinical teams later see "recalibration event at month 6" in logs rather than silent accuracy degradation.

* **Summary: Periodic "health checkup" integrations with clinician partners for out-of-band feedback.** Model drift accelerates when real-world outcomes diverge from training data. Partner with a network of cardiologists who use the device: they annotate confirmed events (arrhythmias diagnosed via ECG) in a separate clinical app. These annotations flow back as aggregated population statistics ("65% of confirmed events detected, 8% false positive rate in cohort age 50-60"), not raw data. Use population-level insights to trigger firmware updates: "New model improves detection in age 45-50 cohort by 3%, no degradation elsewhere." Users see transparency: "Feb 2026 update: better detection for your age group based on partner cardiologist feedback."

* **Summary: Federated learning of user-specific baselines, not global detection thresholds.** The model itself stays frozen; instead, distribute lightweight personalization: each device trains a small user-specific baseline model (100KB, trained overnight weekly) on local sensor patterns. Central service aggregates parameter distributions without touching raw data—answering "what's the distribution of resting heart rate across active users?" vs. specific values. When on-device model detects an anomaly, it compares against that user's learned baseline, not global reference. Clinical drift becomes visible: "50K users show 12% baseline shift in average age cohort"—triggering investigation before clinical impact.

* **Summary: Design for graceful degradation; assume model becomes less accurate over 24 months.** Honest product architecture acknowledges that on-device-only models degrade. Strategy: conservative detection thresholds at launch (higher sensitivity, tolerate false positives), intentionally biased toward under-detection rather than missing real events. As baseline drifts, false positive rate rises (but clinical outcome—user talking to cardiologist—remains good). At 18-month mark, communicate proactively: "Your device has learned your patterns well; an update makes detection 5% more accurate—update recommended." This reframes model obsolescence as a known feature lifecycle, not a failure.

## Firmware Update as a Trust Event and Governance Question

* **Summary: Transparent release notes tied to specific validated improvements, not generic "fixes."** Users have calibrated behavior to alert frequency—a change in alert patterns feels like a breaking change. Avoid "Improved accuracy" without specificity. Instead: "February 2026 update: alert sensitivity adjusted for users age 45-50 (data from 10K partner exams shows we missed 2% of confirmed events in this group; new model fixes this, no change for other ages). Your device age: 14 months. Recommended." Include before/after ROC curves, false positive rate delta, and opt-in beta testing for 1% of users 2 weeks before rollout. This builds trust that updates are clinical improvements, not arbitrary changes.

* **Summary: Implement opt-in gradual rollout with local detection metrics.** Rather than all-or-nothing updates, frame as: "Optional security and clinical update available. Current version handles 95% of events; update increases to 97% with <1% false positive change." Users who skip updates see banner "last updated 8 months ago" but device keeps working. For users who do update, log weekly alert deltas for 30 days—device measures "before update, 2.3 alerts/week; after, 2.1 alerts/week." Users can revert within 7 days if new alert patterns feel wrong. This gives agency: firmware updates become personalization choices, not mandates.

* **Summary: Governance: Clinical advisory board votes on update safety before release.** Non-technical trust requires external validation. Establish an independent board of 3-5 cardiologists who review each proposed firmware change: model ROC curves, cohort-specific deltas, false positive/negative tradeoffs, liability implications. Board votes: "Safe to release, recommend to all users" vs. "Safe to release, recommend to age >50 only" vs. "Hold; needs further validation." Publish decisions. Users see: "This update cleared our medical advisory board on [date]." Regulatory benefit: when FDA 510(k) submission arrives, pre-market clinical evidence is already embedded in published release notes.

* **Summary: Architecture for user-initiated rollback and staged deployment by demographics.** Firmware versioning supports explicit rollback: if users in a cohort report unusual patterns post-update, revert their device fleet to previous model without a full firmware downgrade. This requires infrastructure: devices track {version, model_hash, metrics}, central service tracks per-cohort alert statistics. Early rollout strategy: push to users age 60+ first (higher event density, easier to validate), then age 45-59, then younger. If age 60+ cohort shows unexpected true alert drop 2 weeks post-update, immediately rollback—younger cohorts never get the bad version. This makes updates feel safe because they are staged, not monolithic.

## Federated Learning as Just-In-Time Manufacturing

* **Summary: Federated learning's supply-chain analogy breaks down at device heterogeneity.** Toyota's lean manufacturing works because car parts are standardized; supplier quality variance is controlled. In federated ML, your "parts"—user physiologies—vary wildly: a 50-year-old sedentary user's baseline is incomparable to a 40-year-old marathon runner's. When you aggregate gradients from 100K heterogeneous users, the result averages over biological variance rather than learning coherent detection principles. Practical failure: a model update trained on heterogeneous population performs worse on "typical" users because it optimized for minority outliers. Mitigation: stratify federated learning by demographic cohorts (age, baseline fitness, medication profile), lose the efficiency gains of unified JIT, and reintroduce "buffering" by maintaining separate models per segment.

* **Summary: The "supply disruption" equivalent: users who never connect to updates become increasingly stale.** In Toyota's system, a supplier going offline halts production—visibility forces rapid resolution. In federated learning, a user whose device rarely syncs (poor connectivity, old hardware, simple neglect) becomes an "offline part"—their device runs outdated gradients indefinitely. At 100K users, 20-30% never update past v1.0 model because they skip prompts or lack storage. Central service trains a global model that assumes 80% participation but 30% of fleet runs on stale weights. This creates silent accuracy fragmentation: you can't explain why one user's alert rate diverged without auditing their device version history. Mitigation: mandate updates (liability and privacy risk) or accept fleet heterogeneity as permanent technical debt.

* **Summary: Federated learning demands cryptographic security infrastructure absent from typical wearable stacks.** JIT manufacturing requires trusted suppliers; federated ML requires honest participants. Without secure aggregation (cryptographic protocols that prevent the server from seeing individual gradients), users' physiological signatures could leak during gradient upload—a privacy breach worse than raw data. Secure aggregation requires differential privacy noise, secure multiparty computation, or trusted hardware—each adding compute overhead (5-15% battery), latency (gradient upload becomes multi-round handshake), and complexity (your 4-person firmware team now needs cryptography expertise). Compare: OTA firmware updates require signing (you already do this) and encryption (standard TLS). Federated learning requires novel cryptographic infrastructure validated for medical devices—18-month research roadmap before production.

* **Summary: Quality variance when "parts" are users with conflicting health goals and expectations.** A marathon runner wants sensitive arrhythmia detection even if false positive rate rises (they'll follow up with cardiologist). A sedentary user wants specificity (fewer false alarms, they're anxious). When you aggregate gradients from both, the resulting model optimizes for neither—it's a statistical average of conflicting objectives. Lean manufacturing solves this through standardized specs (all parts must meet tolerance X). Federated learning assumes all users benefit from the same global model, but wearable health is fundamentally personalized. Realistic approach: federated learning for coarse population-level anomaly signatures (new arrhythmia pattern the team hasn't seen), but local on-device personalization for user-specific thresholds. This reintroduces "buffering" (per-user model state) but avoids the heterogeneity mismatch.

## Federated Learning as Just-In-Time Manufacturing: (Continued)

* **Summary: Network disruptions are endemic; lean JIT assumes connectivity that wearables don't have.** Toyota's JIT works because suppliers are connected via logistics infrastructure with <24-hour supply cycles. Federated learning assumes devices can reliably sync gradients every 7 days. Your users include people with poor LTE coverage, international travelers, and airplane mode commuters. A device that skips 3 sync cycles loses gradient history—it can't meaningfully contribute to federated updates. Scale this to fleet: 30% of devices have >2 week sync gaps, creating temporal misalignment. The central service aggregates stale gradients alongside fresh ones, weighting old data equally. Over time, the federated model decays toward an average of stale user states, not current population behavior. JIT manufacturing avoids this by enforcing supplier synchronization; federated learning in wearables has no enforcement mechanism. Workaround: weight gradients by recency, but this complexifies aggregation logic and requires careful numerical tuning—no longer "lean."
