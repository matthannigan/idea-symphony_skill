## Synthesized Insights by Question

### Question 1: Battery as Keystone Constraint and Zero-Based Budget

**Full question**: The 7-day battery target is a keystone-features constraint — not a hardware spec but a determinant of which sensors run continuously vs. intermittently, which models run at full vs. quantized precision, and how often the companion app syncs. Given that radio and always-on sensing (not computation) typically dominate wearable power, what does a zero-based energy budget say about per-subsystem mWh/day across sensing, inference, display, and BLE, which subsystem poses the tightest constraint, and does the budget make continuous monitoring physically achievable or force fundamentally event-driven sensing? Has the team independently validated that 7 days with continuous PPG, accelerometry, temperature, and microphone plus on-device ML is achievable with commercial NPU hardware, or is this a goal being engineered toward without a confirmed path? And what is 7 days actually optimizing for, and would users accept 3 days for materially better detection accuracy?

* **Validate the 7-day target on real silicon before it becomes architecture; datasheets are aspirational, measured current draw is truth.** Six of the seven perspectives converge that the team must build a breadboard or reference prototype with the actual sensor suite and target NPU, run it under realistic duty cycles, and measure per-subsystem power, rather than inheriting numbers from spec sheets. The consensus estimate is sobering: with continuous PPG plus on-device ML, a 500-600 mWh cell realistically delivers 2-4 days, so 7 days is currently aspirational without a confirmed path. The recommended move is to treat physical validation as the first milestone (a multi-week continuous stress test across a 15-35°C range), because spec sheets assume ideal conditions and silicon under sustained load reveals thermal drift and creep that collapse the budget.
  * **Has the team run a 7-day test with all four sensors on the chosen NPU? If not, you are optimizing fiction, not physics.** Urban-water analogy: start from basin capacity and work backward, not from pump specs. *—The Analogist*
  * **Build a hardware breadboard, measure actual current draw under real loads, compare measured vs. spec'd; run a two-week continuous stress test in a temperature chamber (15-35°C). NPU datasheets rate nominal clock, not 24/7 thermal reality.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Run a dead-battery test: instrument an NPU dev board with the actual sensors, run continuous inference for 7 days. If they haven't, the target is aspirational, not architected.** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Start with a real teardown, not datasheet math; run a reference prototype 48 hours, scale to 7 days. At 3-4 days it's a solvable hardware problem; at 1 day the target needs redesign.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Datasheets are aspirational; silicon is truth. Build a reference implementation, measure end-to-end power at 1-second granularity, stress-test under movement and temperature variation before final ASIC selection.** *—The Technical Expert*
  * **840 hours of runtime; work backward from watt-hours. Validate the math independently rather than trusting NPU datasheets.** *—The First Principles Thinker*

* **Radio and always-on sensing, not computation, are the tightest constraints; budget bottom-up from a fixed radio allocation.** Four perspectives converge that BLE dominates the budget (commonly 40-60% of wearable power), PPG sensing is second (20-35%), and inference is a relatively modest 5-15%. The strategic consequence is that optimizing inference efficiency first is optimizing the wrong bottleneck. The practical method is to fix the radio budget as a constant (sync aggregated alerts once daily plus on-demand checks), then ask how much sensing and inference fit in the remainder. This directly answers the keystone question: the budget likely forces event-driven sensing by physics, not by choice.
  * **Roughly 40% radio, 35% PPG sensing, 15% inference, 10% other. The tightest constraint is BLE sync cadence and PPG duty cycle; sync aggregated alerts once daily, drop PPG to windows around anomalies.** *—The Technical Expert*
  * **BLE advertising/scanning is 40-60%, PPG 20-30%, inference 5-15%. Start from a fixed radio budget, then fit sensing and inference into what remains.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Radio dominates (BLE 20-100 mW), then continuous PPG, then inference spikes. If BLE and sensing eat 80%, there is no headroom for always-on high-fidelity ML.** *—The First Principles Thinker*
  * **From ~7 kJ total, radio takes 5-6 kJ, leaving ~1 kJ for sensing and inference; the gating decision on when PPG samples is worth far more than a 20% inference optimization.** *—The Systems Thinker*

* **The 7-day number is a business constraint masquerading as a spec; test the 3-day-for-better-accuracy tradeoff with real users before locking it.** All seven perspectives engage the question of what 7 days optimizes for (convenience, competitive positioning, clinical norms) and most argue it should be validated against user tolerance, not assumed. Several note that three days of clinical-grade detection may save more lives or look cleaner on a 510(k) than seven days of weak intermittent signals. The recommended action is a small structured user study presenting explicit battery-vs-accuracy tradeoffs before the spec is frozen.
  * **A 3-day device with marginally better detection might save more lives than a 7-day device with higher false-negatives; run the lives-saved-per-sensitivity-gain tradeoff.** *—The First Principles Thinker*
  * **Run a small user study (n=20-30): "Would you accept 3 days for 10% higher sensitivity?" Validate the number against user voice, which competitive/investor pressure may have bypassed.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Run a 1-week customer-discovery sprint; "would you recharge every 3 days if accuracy improved 20%?" reframes the budget from "over by 30%" to "on track."** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A/B test 3-day recharge vs. 7-day convenience before locking the spec; overnight-only continuous monitoring with daytime event detection may beat weak 7-day coverage.** *—The Technical Expert*

* **Treat 7 days as an iterative checkpoint and design for second-source hardware to de-risk the single-vendor dependency.** Two perspectives add a program-management dimension: aim for 5 days early and iterate toward 7 rather than burning runway on a speculative spec, and prototype against two NPU candidates so the product is not hostage to one vendor's datasheet performance.
  * **Aim for 5 days by Month 6, then iterate; prototype two NPU candidates and design firmware to support either, de-risking the vendor dependency and adding cost leverage.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Start with proven off-the-shelf components, prove the power story on real wrists, then custom-design the NPU once you know where the bottleneck lives; the target forces early validation that saves money downstream.** *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Thermal load, not just total energy, is a hidden constraint in a wrist form factor.** A single perspective surfaces that total energy is not the same as evenly distributed power: inference spikes generate localized heat, and cumulative thermal stress over a week degrades silicon and accelerates self-discharge, potentially slipping a 7-day target to 5-6 days. Validate that the chosen duty cycle is thermally compatible.
  * **A device that idles at 2 mW then spikes to 50 mW distributes thermal load differently than steady 3 mW; validate the thermal budget against the duty cycle.** *—The First Principles Thinker*

### Question 2: Thermodynamic Lower Bound on Inference and Analog Gating

**Full question**: Shannon's channel capacity and Landauer's principle imply a physical minimum energy per bit of computation. Given the decision entropy of arrhythmia detection, what is the theoretical minimum energy per inference, and how far above that bound are current NPUs operating (revealing whether the power problem is a hardware gap or algorithmic inefficiency, quantifiable as the break-even anomaly prevalence at which event-triggered batch inference saves more energy than continuous inference)? And is there a classical signal-processing threshold (e.g., an analog comparator on R-R interval regularity) that could gate the NPU to fire only on anomalous beats, eliminating what fraction of total inference energy by moving coarse detection earlier into the analog domain before any ML runs?

* **An analog or low-power R-R-interval gate ahead of the NPU is the single highest-ROI architecture move; it eliminates roughly 80-99% of inference in normal rhythm.** All seven perspectives converge on this, the strongest consensus in the cluster. A cheap continuous comparator (or firmware-computed R-R check) watching beat-to-beat interval variance wakes the expensive NPU only on irregular rhythm. With arrhythmia prevalence of a few percent, this cuts NPU duty cycle by 90-99%, relocating the coarse "anomaly present or not" decision to the thermodynamically cheapest domain and reserving the NPU for the fine "what kind of anomaly" question. A recovered consequence: the freed budget lets the model run at higher precision on the genuinely anomalous beats.
  * **A 3-line analog comparator firing on PPG dV/dt over consecutive beats costs ~10-20 µJ vs. ~1 mJ for inference; even 90% elimination yields a 10x inference-energy reduction. Hearing aids use exactly this multi-stage gating.** *—The Analogist*
  * **A dumb analog comparator on R-R intervals lets 90% of normal rhythms sail past; only the anomalous 10% wake the NPU. The analog path also has no quantization error.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Move coarse detection into analog: a comparator opens the gate ~10 times/day in normal rhythm, 100+ in irregular. Gating out 95% recovers most of the power budget.** *—The First Principles Thinker*
  * **A comparator on R-R deviation eliminates inference on 90%+ of normal beats, cutting inference from 10-15% to 1-2% of budget; a high-ROI retrofit that works even on cheaper MCUs.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **A 0.5 µW comparator pulses the NPU only on variance threshold breaches, cutting NPU on-time 95%+; the recovered budget lets the model run int16 instead of int8 on real anomalies.** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Analog bandpass + peak detector gating reduces inference firing 80-95% in healthy individuals; the challenge is tuning the threshold against false negatives.** *—The Technical Expert*
  * **A two-tier gate (analog reject of obvious-normal 99%, NPU only on edge cases) buys back 80% of inference energy.** *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **The thermodynamic floor proves the power problem is algorithmic and hardware inefficiency, not physics; the gap is the design space, not a wall.** Five perspectives compute the same conclusion from Landauer/Shannon: the theoretical minimum (picojoules or below, given the low entropy of a mostly-normal beat stream) sits a trillion-fold below real NPUs operating in micro- to milli-joules. The gap exists because general-purpose matrix math is overcomputed, not because physics forbids efficiency. The actionable diagnostic: measure how far above the floor you sit; a 1000x gap is a hardware/architecture problem, a 10x gap is a model-optimization opportunity, and either way the gate plus aggressive quantization closes most of it.
  * **The floor is ~10^-22 J per decision; NPUs burning milliwatts are trillions of times above it, doing overcomputed general-purpose matrix math.** *—The First Principles Thinker*
  * **NPUs run 10^12+ times above the ~3×10^-21 J/bit Landauer floor; the leverage isn't closing that gap but eliminating 80%+ of inference workload upstream.** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **The minimum is picojoules; real NPUs operate at microjoules, millions of times over, due to die area, leakage, and algorithmic redundancy, not physics; dedicated analog could reclaim 10-50x.** *—The Technical Expert*
  * **At 100-1000 pJ/decision vs. a ~1 pJ floor, NPUs are 100-1000x over; this is a hardware-efficiency gap that quantization and pruning narrow.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Real systems run 100-1000x above the theoretical ~100 pJ floor; the bottleneck is the architectural choice to run inference uniformly rather than adaptively.** *—The Analogist*

* **Quantize aggressively and batch inferences to spread thermal load; anomalies are high-amplitude and survive low precision.** Two perspectives add complementary efficiency levers beyond gating: INT8 (or lower) quantization likely preserves clinically relevant sensitivity while cutting inference power 4-8x, and batching inferences amortizes fixed wake/setup overhead while smoothing the thermal spike, at the cost of detection latency that is medically defensible for most arrhythmias.
  * **8-bit quantization likely preserves anomaly sensitivity (anomalies are high-amplitude) and drops inference power 4-8x vs. FP32; batching amortizes fixed overhead and smooths the thermal profile.** *—The First Principles Thinker*
  * **Batch 5-10 seconds of data and infer once per minute by day, once per 5 minutes at night, saving 30-50% of inference power at a clinically acceptable latency cost.** *—The Pragmatist*

* **Design the anomaly definition around the cheapest computation rather than the other way around.** A single reframe inverts the optimization: instead of asking whether the NPU can detect a predefined anomaly, ask what the most energetically efficient anomaly is to detect, which may be simpler, more robust, and more clinically useful.
  * **Flip Landauer: design the anomaly definition around the minimum-energy computation, not the model around a fixed anomaly.** *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Dissent: analog gating is brittle, and your energy accounting may be hiding that sensing, not inference, dominates the budget.** A single adversarial perspective surfaces two load-bearing cautions the convergence misses. First, analog thresholds drift with skin temperature and component aging and cannot be updated post-manufacture, so they can silently flood or starve the NPU unless built with hysteresis bands and periodic recalibration (or pushed into updatable firmware). Second, and more fundamental: if sensing is 50% of power and inference only 10%, optimizing inference (the consensus win) will not fix the 7-day budget at all; the team must build a 24-hour stacked power trace to confirm where energy actually goes before committing to inference-side optimization.
  * **Analog thresholds are temperature-sensitive and un-updatable; design hysteresis bands and daily recalibration, or move the coarse gate into firmware to keep it updatable.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **If sensing is 50% of power and inference 10%, optimizing inference won't fix the budget; build a 1-minute-resolution 24-hour stacked power trace to find the real target before optimizing the "sexy" part.** *—The Devil's Advocate*

* **Validate the gate offline against labeled data before freezing hardware; >1% missed anomalies is unacceptable for a health device.** Two perspectives converge on the same gating-validation experiment: replay a week of labeled patient PPG/ECG through the proposed analog thresholds in post-processing and measure missed true positives; under 0.5-1% loss justifies the analog complexity, above it the liability is disqualifying.
  * **Collect labeled PPG from the target population, run the gate offline; if it misses <1% of events and false-triggers <10%, it's a major power win at minimal accuracy cost.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Log a week of real patient data, apply the thresholds in post-processing; losing >1% of real anomalies is unacceptable, under 0.5% is worth the circuit complexity. A 2-3 week experiment before hardware freeze.** *—The Technical Expert*

### Question 3: Forced Simplicity as a Feature

**Full question**: If the 7-day battery rules out continuous high-frequency inference, how might intermittent or event-triggered sensing, rather than always-on monitoring, actually produce a more user-friendly device that feels less surveillance-like and more intentionally attentive?

* **Intermittent, event-triggered sensing reframes the device from surveillance watcher to attentive companion, and the constraint becomes premium positioning rather than an apology.** All seven perspectives converge that periodic or anomaly-triggered attention feels qualitatively different from always-on tracking: less creepy, less notification fatigue, more intentional. The shared recommendation is to market intermittency as a designed feature ("detects anomalies without constant monitoring," "checks in at moments that matter") rather than concealing it as a power compromise, and to make the periodicity visible so users feel the device's rhythm.
  * **Like a meditation bell, periodic check-ins feel restorative, not panopticon-like; "respects your privacy" and "focuses on moments that matter" beat "7-day battery" as messaging.** *—The Analogist*
  * **"We can't do continuous" becomes "we designed for meaningful monitoring"; intermittent sensing is premium positioning, an attentive companion not a paranoid watcher.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **A device sampling every 30 minutes delivers intentional attention moments; reframe as "we analyze your rhythm through periodic check-ins, not constant surveillance."** *—The First Principles Thinker*
  * **A device that "wakes up" on anomalous patterns feels like an attentive assistant, not a tracker; market intermittency as a feature, not a hidden constraint.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Batch inference into visible windows (9 AM, noon, 3 PM); users feel the device's periodicity, which is reassuring, and a weekly alert is taken more seriously than twice-daily noise.** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Intermittent sensing is a design anchor, not a limitation; the UX shifts from "always watching" to "attentive when it matters."** *—The Technical Expert*
  * **Event-triggered sensing (exercise, sleep onset, elevated HR) gives peace of mind without 24/7 surveillance; clinical studies show users prefer intentional monitoring.** *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Forced simplicity cascades into engineering, privacy, and regulatory wins: simpler models, cleaner data, easier 510(k) defense.** Several perspectives note the constraint propagates beneficially through the whole stack. Fewer sensors and event windows mean smaller, more interpretable models that train faster and are easier to defend to the FDA; intermittent PPG (e.g., during sleep) is cleaner PPG with less motion artifact; and less data leaving the device makes HIPAA compliance and BLE sync lighter. Intermittent event-triggered monitoring is also easier to defend in a 510(k) than continuous inference with its false-positive burden.
  * **Intermittent PPG is cleaner PPG (no motion artifact); fewer streams mean fewer calibration points and a more robust algorithm with fewer false positives.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Constrained sensing enables a 100KB model on 5-minute windows instead of a 2MB model on 24-hour streams; transparent enough for regulatory review.** *—The Technical Expert*
  * **Fewer sensors running fewer hours means lighter BLE, simpler privacy guarantees, and no server-side retraining pipeline; the simplicity cascades through the stack.** *—The Technical Expert*
  * **FDA guidance is skeptical of continuous monitoring's false-positive burden; "monitors only during high-risk states and flags confirmed anomalies" is easier to defend than "continuous inference on all beats."** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Limited battery forces local processing and natural data-deletion windows; "so private it practically deletes itself" is a marketing advantage competitors can't match.** *—The Constraint Flipper*

* **Event-triggered sensing widens the addressable market and enables faster model iteration by freeing per-decision compute.** Two angles add product and engineering upside: an event-triggered device serves both high-burden and rare-episode users well with a clear value story for each, and concentrating compute on events gives 2-3x the per-decision budget, allowing more expressive models and faster A/B iteration than a continuous low-power design.
  * **Event-triggered inference gives 2-3x the compute per decision, enabling more expressive models and faster A/B testing; better accuracy in 6 months beats a mediocre model on perfect battery. It also serves both high-burden and rare-episode cohorts.** *—The Pragmatist*

* **Friction and ritual can build trust better than invisible automation.** A single perspective inverts the usual usability assumption: requiring the user to wear or activate the device during defined windows creates agency and ritual ("I put on my monitor for sleep"), which builds habit and trust more durably than seamless passive surveillance.
  * **A device that requires intentional wear during key windows creates a trust-building ritual; not promising continuous coverage may be more honest and more trusted.** *—The First Principles Thinker*

* **Dissent: intermittency risks false reassurance, and worried 40+ users may want MORE monitoring, not less.** A single adversarial perspective challenges the cluster's core premise and is load-bearing because the target users are precisely the anxious cohort the reframe assumes wants detachment. "No alert" does not mean "healthy"; it means "not flagged," and a too-high trigger threshold in low-power mode can silently miss a real event. The framing "less surveillance, more attentive" assumes users want less monitoring, but health-conscious adults with cardiac family history likely want more assurance. The proposed mitigations turn intermittency back into transparency: an explicit in-app contract about what event-triggered means, a "scanned 847 times, ruled out arrhythmia 844 times" dashboard reframing intermittency as efficient triage, periodic reassurance pings, and a user-selectable continuous mode for high-risk periods. A pragmatic compromise is continuous low-power PPG sensing (cheap) with inference only on detected irregularity (expensive).
  * **Intermittent monitoring can create false security; "no alert" means "not flagged," not "healthy." Document the contract explicitly and let users opt into continuous mode for high-risk periods.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Worried 40+ users with cardiac history want more assurance, not less; "intentionally attentive" may feel like the device is sleeping on the job. A transparency dashboard reframes it as efficient triage.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Event-triggered monitoring introduces latency and uncertainty that drives anxious app-checking; add 4-hourly reassurance pings, or sense continuously at low power and infer only on irregularity.** *—The Devil's Advocate*

### Question 4: Stage Management and the Power Budget Allocation Problem

**Full question**: Theater stage managers coordinate lighting, sound, rigging, and cast so no two resource-intensive cues compete for the same moment. The wearable faces an analogous choreography problem: continuous PPG sensing, ML inference, Bluetooth sync, and display refresh all compete for a shared 7-day power budget. What stage-management principles (pre-computed cue sequences, resource reservation windows, graceful degradation when a cue runs long) could inform how the firmware scheduler allocates power across subsystems, and what physiological signals (sleep, exercise, stillness) could function as cue triggers that shift the device between power configurations?

* **Build a physiological-context state machine with pre-defined power "scenes," shifting the allocation decision from runtime contention to design-time choreography.** All seven perspectives converge on a firmware state machine keyed to physiological context (Sleep, Sedentary/Rest, Exercise/Active, Alert, Low-Battery), where each mode pre-assigns which subsystems run and at what cadence. Sleep mode runs low-frequency PPG for apnea and defers BLE to morning; exercise down-samples inference and relaxes anomaly thresholds to avoid false alerts; alert mode commits full power. The shared insight is that pre-computing scenes at design time eliminates the resource-contention peaks that arise when tasks compete at runtime.
  * **State machine SLEEP/ACTIVE/ALERT triggered by accelerometer context and time-of-day; never run all four subsystems simultaneously, like choreographed concert lighting cues.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Your body broadcasts cues (HR, motion, temp); sleeping device runs minimal inference, exercise cranks PPG, anomaly activates display and sync. Choreograph the battery to the user's physiology.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **A master state machine pre-scripts each state: sleep activates continuous PPG and audio, defers BLE; exercise down-samples inference; allocate resources proportional to detection need per state.** *—The First Principles Thinker*
  * **Three precomputed profiles (active/rest/sleep) with assigned cadences; the scheduler follows a timeline rather than deciding "infer now?" per beat, saving 20-30% of power; 2-3 weeks of firmware work.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Define power-mode "scenes" by context; sleep onset shifts to 30-second PPG with no BLE, exercise to 1 Hz PPG plus inference, moving the decision boundary from runtime to design time.** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Discrete states (Sleeping, Active, Night-Watch, Manual-Check) with reserved inference windows; accelerometer and sleep-phase detection gate transitions so no two power-hungry cues run at once.** *—The Technical Expert*

* **Use cheap, locally-computed physiological signals (HRV, accelerometer jerk, temperature drift) as the cue triggers that orchestrate expensive subsystems.** Several perspectives converge that low-cost derived features can drive high-cost decisions: HRV signals relaxed vs. stressed/exercising state, accelerometer motion onset detects activity, and skin-temperature circadian drift hints at sleep phase. A lightweight estimator (a few KB) running per minute categorizes context and sets the next interval's power allocation.
  * **HRV acts as a cue light (high HRV reduces inference frequency, low HRV raises it); accelerometer jerk and temperature drift are ~1 µJ feedback sensors orchestrating high-cost decisions.** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **A ~5KB Bayesian state estimator runs every minute to categorize physiological context, then adjusts the next hour's power allocation; HRV, temperature trends, and motion patterns are the inputs.** *—The Technical Expert*
  * **After 2-3 weeks the firmware learns when anomalies occur for that user and front-loads power reserves into those windows; users experience it as "the device is smarter about when it checks."** *—The Analogist*
  * **Adapt sensing frequency to recent history (more checks if anomalies in last 24 hours); users perceive personalization, not battery constraints.** *—The Pragmatist*

* **Reserve non-overlapping time windows for each subsystem to prevent current-draw spikes and brown-out.** Several perspectives converge on resource-reservation scheduling: assign BLE sync, inference batches, and high-frequency sensing to non-overlapping slots so subsystems never compete for current delivery, which causes voltage droop and forces throttling. Pre-booked windows also let the battery-management IC optimize charge delivery and make consumption predictable and testable.
  * **Reserve windows: BLE 0:00-0:05, inference 0:06-0:10, PPG baseline 0:11-0:15; scheduling-conflict prevention saves more power than any single optimization.** *—The First Principles Thinker*
  * **Schedule inference, BLE, and high-frequency sensing in non-overlapping windows via an event queue with reserved slots, not interrupt-driven chaos; this keeps current draw steady.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **Pre-book inference windows like theater lighting cues (reserve 50 mW at 8 PM for sleep, 100 mW at 6 AM for exercise); deterministic scheduling hits the 7-day target reliably with no race conditions.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **Software-pipeline the stages (BLE transmits yesterday's summary while PPG samples today's data via a separate DMA channel) so no subsystem dominates a single moment.** *—The First Principles Thinker*

* **Build graceful degradation as a built-in stabilizer so the battery declines smoothly instead of hitting a cliff.** Several perspectives converge on explicit battery-threshold fallback rules (e.g., below 20% reduce inference frequency or drop to sleep-only apnea detection; below 10% go silent except for a critical alert or haptic-only feedback). These transitions should be firmware-managed and largely transparent, building user trust through predictable behavior rather than surprise shutdowns, and they include "call an audible" handling when a cue (an inference job) runs long.
  * **Below 20% battery, reduce inference 50% and defer non-urgent syncs; a built-in stabilizer that extends perceived life and avoids a cliff edge.** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Below 20%, shift to sleep-only apnea detection; below 10%, preserve power for a low-power critical alert; predictable depletion behavior builds trust. One week to implement.** *—The Pragmatist*
  * **A power pyramid: core detection always runs (5 mW), enhanced inference if battery ≥60%, premium features only when docked; progressive capability kills battery anxiety.** *—The Constraint Flipper*
  * **If inference runs long, skip the next batch and widen the interval; if battery dips, drop to INT8 and lower PPG rate; degradations stay transparent to the user.** *—The First Principles Thinker*
  * **Below 20%, disable BLE except on-demand and drop to threshold-only detection; below 10%, haptic-only; queue overtime inference for the next low-power window rather than stalling.** *—The Technical Expert*

* **Reserve emergency capacity so a detected anomaly always gets full bandwidth, and validate the whole state machine in simulation before deployment.** Two complementary points: an explicit alert state should degrade non-critical functions (pause background sync, raise display polling, run inference at full power) so a detected arrhythmia gets full compute and comms even on a constrained budget, and the entire state machine should be simulated over thousands of synthetic user-days to catch cascading failures and confirm worst-case lifetime exceeds 5 days before any real firmware ships.
  * **Define the alert state explicitly (max inference, real-time display, immediate sync) and test that it doesn't break the 7-day target even if an anomaly occurs daily, like an ICU monitor reserving emergency capacity.** *—The Analogist*
  * **Simulate the 5-mode state machine on actual hardware for 7 days with synthetic data first; this catches cascading failures like "Sleep Mode still does a midnight sync that drains 30% of the night's budget."** *—The Systems Thinker*
  * **Run thousands of synthetic user-days (irregular sleepers, 3-hour runs); if worst-case is below 5 days, the budget is under-provisioned before a line of real firmware.** *—The Technical Expert*

* **Dissent: context detection is brittle, state machines thrash at boundaries, and the choreography itself costs power.** A single adversarial perspective surfaces three failure modes the convergence assumes away. Context detection is non-trivial: accelerometer sleep-detection fails on bedridden or sedentary users and exercise detection misses swimming, so validate detection accuracy offline (>85% before earning the right to switch modes) and let users manually tag activity to seed context. State machines thrash without hysteresis, oscillating between modes on sensor noise and burning energy on transitions, so require sustained-state dwell times and exponential backoff. And the scheduler is not free: a feature-rich power manager can consume 5-10% of MCU cycles, so profile it against a <2% budget or push power management to a dedicated PMIC. Real user behavior (an incoming call, an app sync colliding with inference) also defeats fixed cue sequences, arguing for an adaptive scheduler with soft resource caps that degrade rather than block colliding operations.
  * **Accelerometer sleep-detection fails on bedridden users; exercise detection misses swimming. Validate context detection offline to >85% before switching modes, and let users manually tag activity.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **State machines thrash at boundaries (noise flips stillness/active every 5 seconds, toggling the radio); require 5 minutes of stillness to enter sleep mode and add exponential backoff against false transitions.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **A feature-rich scheduler may eat 5-10% of MCU cycles managing transitions; profile it against a <2% budget or move power management to a separate PMIC to avoid firmware bloat.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
  * **Fixed cue sequences don't survive contact with real behavior (calls, app syncs colliding with inference); build an adaptive scheduler with soft resource caps that degrade one operation rather than block it.** *—The Devil's Advocate*
