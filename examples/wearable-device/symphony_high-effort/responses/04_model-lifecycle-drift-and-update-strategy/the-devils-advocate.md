# Model Lifecycle, Drift, and Update Strategy - The Devil's Advocate

## Update Strategy, Silent Drift, and Rollback Safety

* **The untested rollback assumption.** Your team plans cryptographic signing and rollback capability, but have you stress-tested that rollback actually restores the *user's prior alert patterns*? A user rolls back to firmware V1 after V2 silent-fails for 3 days—their baseline calibration is stale, their confidence eroded, and the old model may not alert correctly anymore. Mitigation: Design rollback to include user-initiated "re-calibration warmup" period (48 hours of conservative alert thresholds) before returning to normal sensitivity.

* **Drift detection requires ground truth you don't have.** You propose cryptographic chains to prevent model substitution, but your actual killer risk is *silent accuracy degradation* from physiological drift (aging, medication, comorbidities). On-device, there's no ground truth—no hospital ECG to validate against. Without population-level telemetry, you may not detect collapse until a patient event occurs. Mitigation: Ship with "confidence thresholds"—when the model's decision confidence drops below a floor across many alerts, trigger a firmware update check or ask users to pair with clinical partners for periodic validation cohorts.

* **Per-component strategy fragmentation creates compliance burden.** You outline different strategies for baseline calibration vs. core detector, but if calibration updates via local fine-tuning and detector updates via OTA, your regulatory and testing burden doubles. FDA will ask: which version did Patient X have? What's the audit trail? Did updates interact? Mitigation: Unified versioning schema—bundle all model components in each firmware release, treat the pair as a single regulated artifact, and maintain a clear change log per device.

* **Silent mode-shift in alert behavior during update windows.** Between when the old model degradation becomes apparent and a new firmware rolls out, users may receive fewer alerts, then suddenly more alerts post-update. This oscillation trains users to *distrust* the system. Mitigation: On detection of model degradation, proactively notify users ("we've detected drift, an update is rolling out") and temporarily lower thresholds to maintain alert volume during the gap—transparency over silent uncertainty.

## Update Cadence as Stabilizing and Destabilizing

* **User-level reset vs. population-level improvement is a false binary.** Each firmware update may reset per-user learned context (like a personalized PPG calibration), but you frame this as "loss." The inverse risk is equally dangerous: if you *preserve* user calibrations across a population-model update, you may embed old individual patterns that no longer reflect the user's physiology. Mitigation: Versioned calibration—tie user baselines to the firmware version that created them, and provide a "recalibration hint" after major updates to let users refresh calibrations without losing personalization entirely.

* **Cadence creates a separate attack surface.** Frequent updates mean frequent BLE/firmware transfers, more exposure to man-in-the-middle substitution. Infrequent updates mean longer windows of drift. There's a Goldilocks zone that's not obvious. Mitigation: Data-driven update triggering—don't use a fixed calendar cadence; instead, deploy updates when population-level drift metrics cross a threshold, paired with mandatory security audits before each push.

* **Users with poor connectivity get stranded in degraded modes.** If updates are periodic and mandatory, users in low-signal environments (rural areas, certain workplaces) may miss windows and operate with a known-stale model longer than peers in urban areas. Health equity issue. Mitigation: Design updates to be background-opportunistic with a grace period (update available for 30 days, applies when user is on WiFi), and clearly communicate to users which firmware version they're running and when the next update is available.

## Federated Learning's Hidden Coordination Cost, Infrastructure Timeline, and On-Device Training Necessity Audit

* **Federated averaging on a heterogeneous fleet converges slower and may silently diverge.** Your wearables will have firmware v1.2, v1.5, v2.0 all active simultaneously, on devices with different NPU hardware, battery states, and sensor drift. Standard federated averaging assumes some homogeneity. Without careful convergence analysis, partial updates from mismatched cohorts may harm the aggregate more than help. Mitigation: Implement cohort-aware federated learning where devices only aggregate with firmware/hardware siblings, and run offline simulations with intentional partial-dropout scenarios to verify non-divergence.

* **The federated infrastructure is maturing, but not for *this* use case.* TensorFlow Federated and similar frameworks are robust for text/image learning, but health-monitoring federated learning requires regulatory acceptance (HIPAA, FDA), privacy-preserving aggregation (differential privacy adds noise, reducing model fidelity), and real-time reliability guarantees. Timeline: 24–36 months if you start collaborating with academic partners *now*. Your 18-month runway assumes you *don't* need federated learning. Mitigation: Build firmware architecture to *decouple* update delivery from the aggregation strategy. Ship OTA updates for v1; design the payload format to support federated signals when infrastructure is ready—don't make it a retrofit.

* **Person-specific signal structure is empirically unproven for wrist PPG.** You propose on-device fine-tuning because "wrist-worn sensors are highly person-specific." But clinical literature on PPG artifact, sensor placement variability, and inter-individual physiology shows that *placement and sensor quality* matter far more than inter-individual signal structure. A well-calibrated population model often outperforms individual fine-tuning on noisy data. Mitigation: Run a prospective study with 50–100 users comparing a frozen population model vs. on-device fine-tuning over 6 weeks; measure accuracy, false-positive/negative rates, and battery cost. If population model wins, skip on-device training entirely and reinvest the compute budget in better sensor fusion.

## The ML Pipeline That Ages Well

* **Privacy-first models age *worse* because they can't learn.** Your privacy guarantee (no raw data leaves the device, no cloud training) is ironclad, but it creates a model-improvement wall. In 3 years, clinical knowledge about arrhythmia markers may improve, but you cannot retroactively improve the on-device model without risking users' privacy or requiring a firmware update they may not install. Compare this to a commercial ECG app that can continuously improve on new cloud data. Mitigation: Design a "privacy-respecting active learning" pipeline: on-device, identify ambiguous cases (low-confidence predictions) and ask users (opt-in) to export *anonymized feature vectors* (not raw data) once monthly. Aggregate these, retrain offline, and push improved models via firmware—you gain learning signal without raw data leaving the device.

* **The "well-calibrated population model" assumption may not age at all.** Aging, medication changes, and emerging comorbidities shift physiology over years in ways a static model doesn't anticipate. Your current plan assumes a single frozen model trained on young healthy adults generalizes to 60-year-olds with hypertension. It may work initially but degrade silently. Mitigation: Embed a "model performance monitoring" dashboard in the companion app—track aggregate alert patterns by cohort (age, reported conditions, medication changes) and flag when performance diverges. Push targeted firmware updates for underperforming cohorts, or ask users in high-drift cohorts to opt into periodic re-calibration sessions.

## Ecological Succession and Model Update Strategy

* **Early-adopter bias in edge-case labeling is real and dangerous.** You propose shipping a conservative high-specificity model to accumulate edge cases for a future successor. But who are your early adopters? Likely health-conscious, tech-savvy adults with atypical phenotypes (early arrhythmia detectors, biohackers). The edge cases *they* generate aren't representative of your broader target market (40+ adults with family history). You'll build a successor model optimized for early-adopter artifacts, not real-world diversity. Mitigation: From day one, anonymously segment edge-case data by user demographics (age, reported health status) and retain only the cases that are *underrepresented* in your training data. Explicitly weight toward older, higher-risk cohorts during successor model development.

* **Ecological succession requires you to tolerate early-stage failure.** Shipping a conservative (high-specificity) model means more false negatives initially. Users expecting an early-detection device will be frustrated when alerts are rare. Some may abandon the device, creating survivorship bias in your labeled dataset. The "successional" logic only works if users stay engaged despite suboptimal performance. Mitigation: Frame the product as "collaborative health monitoring" from launch—clearly communicate that v1.0 is conservative by design, explain the roadmap to more sensitive models, and build a research component where engaged users opt into deeper integration (periodic surveys, validated checks with clinical partners) to accelerate successor model training.

## Federated Learning's Absence as a Purity Argument

* **User-initiated anonymized exports are a compliance minefield.** You propose "user exports" as a privacy-respecting alternative to federated learning. But once a user exports their data, you've created a data-handling liability: where does it go? How is it stored? Can a user revoke consent after export? HIPAA requires detailed data-handling agreements, and users may not understand what "anonymized" means. One re-identification attack later, and your privacy-first model becomes a liability. Mitigation: If you pursue this path, use a trusted third-party aggregator (academic medical center or privacy-focused cloud provider) with pre-existing HIPAA infrastructure and data-use agreements. Never store exported data on your servers; let the third party handle aggregation and push back only the trained model weights.

* **Opt-in research programs create selection bias in your improvement feedback.** Users who opt in to share data are likely higher health-literacy, more engaged, and atypical. Models trained on opt-in cohorts may overfit to that population and actually degrade performance on the broader device fleet. Mitigation: Instead of pure opt-in, design a "fair sampling" mechanism—every Nth device automatically contributes anonymized feature vectors (hardware-level sampling, not user choice) to ensure your learning signal represents the actual fleet, not just the engaged subset. Users can still opt out globally, but default-to-contributing ensures representative signal.

* **Synthetic data generation on-device is compute-expensive and unproven.** You propose generating synthetic edge cases on-device to improve future models. But on a low-power microcontroller, running a generative model to create training data is a significant power cost, and there's no guarantee synthetic data improves model fidelity—it may introduce artifact and bias instead. Mitigation: Generate synthetic data *offline* during firmware development using real user edge cases collected under research partnerships, not on-device at runtime. Reserve on-device compute for inference and occasional fine-tuning of small calibration parameters, not generative modeling.
