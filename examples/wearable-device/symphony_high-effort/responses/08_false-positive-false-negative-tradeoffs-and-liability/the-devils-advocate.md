# False-Positive / False-Negative Tradeoffs and Liability - The Devil's Advocate

## Q1: The threshold with no safe harbor (cost asymmetry, operating-point methodology, products-liability exposure, and the alert-credibility death-spiral)

* **Undocumented threshold drift as liability vector.** You're treating sensitivity/specificity as a static tuning choice at launch, but real systems drift post-deployment. Battery chemistry degrades asymmetrically across sensors; users adapt by taking the device off during showers, skewing baseline training. After 18 months, you've silently shifted from 85% sensitivity to 78% without triggering alerts to clinicians or users. Mitigation: embed a "confidence degradation flag" that triggers a firmware update when detected sensor-drift crosses a threshold, and log every threshold adjustment to encrypted on-device audit trails reviewable by the user.

* **The asymmetry you can't price into insurance.** False positives cost the user trust and money (unnecessary clinic visits); false negatives cost the user their life, making your liability exposure theoretically infinite. You can't hedge this with standard product liability insurance because underwriters will demand clinical data you don't have yet. Mitigation: negotiate a "safe harbor" partnership with a cardiology practice early—publish a white paper co-authored with a licensed MD stating the device's operating characteristics, explicitly disclaiming diagnostic intent, and framing it as a patient-empowering monitoring tool rather than a detector, narrowing the scope of duty.

* **The alert-credibility spiral is nonlinear.** If users see 5 false positives in the first month, they don't just discount alerts by 20%; they stop acting on alerts entirely. Studies show alert fatigue causes humans to ignore critical alarms once trust crosses a threshold. You need to understand your product's specific fatigue curve—perhaps you can sustain 1 false positive per 100 true positives before behavioral collapse. Mitigation: run a controlled internal beta (6–8 weeks, 200 users) measuring alert-response rates and engagement drop-off for different false-positive ratios; use that data to set your launch threshold, not population-level ROC curves.

* **Personalized thresholds without the cloud create implementation debt.** You want adaptive thresholds based on "individual user's historical false-positive rate," but that requires on-device learning—tracking user's ground-truth feedback to retrain locally. This is a multi-year engineering effort (federated learning, privacy-preserving updates), and if you don't build it before launch, users will demand it post-launch, creating a maintenance nightmare. Mitigation: ship with fixed thresholds but architect the on-device learning pipeline (model versioning, update orchestration) from day one, even if disabled; set a public roadmap for personalized thresholds at 12 months post-launch.

* **Safe-harbor language is worthless without behavioral design.** Your companion app disclaimer saying "not a medical diagnosis" is one sentence users scroll past. Liability courts examine the totality of the user experience—if the device vibrates urgently and displays "ARRHYTHMIA DETECTED," the disclaimer doesn't shield you from duty-of-care claims. Mitigation: design the alert UX to reflect the actual confidence level ("anomaly detected, *may* indicate arrhythmia—consult your doctor within 48 hours") and use graduated urgency signals (subtle notification for low-confidence signals, urgent only for high-confidence events with validation signals).

## Q2: False-negative invisibility, slow degradation, and calibrating urgency without clinical authority

* **Slow-motion detection failure is undetectable without active monitoring.** You're monitoring the user, but who monitors the monitor? A false negative is silent until tragedy, and by then you can't prove whether the miss was a threshold problem, sensor degradation, or model drift. Your best defense is proactive drift detection, but you need ground truth to compare against—and you don't have it on-device. Mitigation: partner with users willing to share clinical ECG data periodically (monthly or quarterly sync with an optional cloud upload); use these anchors to detect whether the on-device model's miss rate is creeping upward, and trigger firmware updates when drift exceeds 5%.

* **Battery degradation creates unmonitored performance cliffs.** As the battery ages, the MCU voltage drops; the neural accelerator becomes less stable, introducing occasional silent computation errors. You might miss entire arrhythmia episodes not because the model is wrong, but because the hardware is operating outside its thermal envelope. Users have no visibility into this failure mode. Mitigation: log aggregate performance metrics on-device (% of inference cycles where confidence is below threshold, temperature spikes during computation); surface a "device health check" in the app monthly that warns users if their hardware is drifting outside design margins.

* **Clinical severity gradations require domain expertise you don't have.** Your device can't distinguish between a one-off ectopic beat (benign) and the start of atrial fibrillation (urgent). The difference is a question for a cardiologist, not a machine learning model. But if you don't surface this ambiguity to users, you risk either over-alerting (fatigue) or under-alerting (missing a real problem). Mitigation: design the alert system to communicate *degree of uncertainty*, not false certainty—e.g., "detected 3 irregular beats over 2 hours, similarity to your baseline is 60%; [Talk to doctor]" rather than "arrhythmia alert."

* **Engagement decay obscures miss rates.** Users who stop wearing the device after 6 months have zero false negatives *because they're not wearing it*. Your apparent false-negative rate looks good, but it's because engaged users drop out, leaving only the skeptics. This makes it almost impossible to detect drift in your miss rate. Mitigation: track and report cohort-specific miss rates (e.g., "30-day active users had 0 missed events in this cohort vs. 4 in the previous cohort"), and conduct quarterly calibration studies with a small engaged user group.

* **Legal discovery will demand miss-rate forensics you can't provide.** If a user suffers a cardiac event they didn't know was coming, plaintiff attorneys will demand logs of every inference your device ran, every confidence score, every threshold decision. If you didn't design your logging architecture with forensic rigor from the start, you'll face a "destroyed evidence" narrative. Mitigation: implement immutable, timestamped event logs on-device (encrypted, tamper-evident), including raw inference scores, threshold comparisons, and alert/no-alert decisions; include a user-accessible audit trail in the companion app so users can pull their own forensic records.

## Q3: The false-positive win — designing toward best outcome

* **The best-case alert flow reveals a design paradox.** A user receives a "worth mentioning to your doctor" notification during a routine day; they mention it at their next checkup; the doctor runs an ECG and confirms a subtle arrhythmia; they start preventative medication; 6 months later, they avoid a hospital stay. This is a genuine positive outcome. But it requires the user to trust the alert enough to act, yet not be alarmed into unnecessary panic—a tightrope. Most designs either err toward panic (high false positives, alert fatigue) or dismissal (high false negatives, missed events). Mitigation: design this notification with a "trust scaffold"—show the user what the device actually saw (heart-rate variability visualization, confidence score with explanation), then offer low-friction next steps ("share this with your doctor in your next visit" or "message your doctor now").

* **The outcome narrative depends on clinical validation you're deferring.** For a user to credit the device with "changing their life," there has to be clinical evidence that earlier intervention actually mattered. But you're launching as a consumer wellness device, so you're explicitly not claiming clinical value. You can't have it both ways—if you want the positive-outcome narrative, you need to start collecting the clinical data now to eventually support it. Mitigation: partner with a research hospital to run a prospective study (6–12 months) measuring whether users who act on wearable alerts have better outcomes than matched controls; publish results openly (even if negative) to build credibility.

* **The alert must compete for attention in a noisy environment.** Users receive dozens of notifications daily; a wearable alert is just one more. The psychological research on alert effectiveness shows that alerts that arrive in the context of an existing habit (e.g., when the user is already checking their health app) are far more likely to drive action. A random 3 a.m. vibration about a possible arrhythmia often gets dismissed. Mitigation: make alerts "sticky" by routing them through the companion app during waking hours (check for phone unlock patterns, suppress notifications during sleep), and include a "confirm you received this" flow to measure engagement and identify when alerts are being consistently ignored.

* **The positive-outcome design reveals a secondary liability risk.** If your device successfully alerts users and drives intervention, you've created a new liability surface: users now have a screening tool they might over-rely on, potentially skipping regular cardiology checkups because "my wearable would tell me if something's wrong." This is the inverse of false-negative risk—false confidence in the device. Mitigation: include language in the alert UX that reinforces periodic clinical screening ("this alert does not replace your annual checkup") and in the companion app, offer an integration with clinical reminder systems (send your doctor a secure message when you've had an alert, auto-schedule a follow-up appointment).

* **The measurable "best outcome" requires defining success upstream.** You can't design toward a positive outcome without knowing what that outcome is—better health metrics? User-reported trust? Faster time-to-clinical-action? Different success metrics lead to different threshold choices and alert designs. Mitigation: interview 20–30 target users (40+ with family cardiac history) about what outcome would make the device "worth wearing for years"; use those responses to define your product's success metric (not just ROC AUC), then optimize toward that metric during design and launch.

## Q4: Credentialing systems and the false-positive liability surface

* **Tiered alerts require a credentialing architecture you're not building.** You propose high-confidence arrhythmia signals to cardiologists as a clinical review tool, but cardiologists aren't your users—your device's *primary users* are consumers. This creates a split liability: are you a medical device claiming to inform cardiologist decisions (regulated, liability-bearing), or a wellness tool that *happens* to include a clinical review channel (less regulated, less liability)? You can't have both without explicit credentialing. Mitigation: establish a formal "cardiologist collaboration channel" with defined workflows—e.g., high-confidence signals are flagged for async cardiologist review, with documented turnaround SLAs; cardiologists sign a credentialing agreement acknowledging they're reviewing alerting signals, not making clinical diagnoses; log all cardiologist feedback for model validation.

* **The 510(k) boundary is not a clear liability shield.** Even as a consumer wellness device, if your target demographic is "40+ with family cardiac history," courts may infer that you're marketing to a clinically vulnerable population, and duty-of-care obligations may attach despite your non-medical positioning. A 510(k) clearance later doesn't retroactively inoculate you from claims about the consumer version's performance. Mitigation: document your regulatory strategy clearly now—commission a legal opinion (from a regulatory attorney) that outlines the liability scope of your current wellness positioning, and publish this analysis (with appropriate disclaimers) so users understand the device's intended use and limitations.

* **Credentialing tiers create training debt for users.** If you implement tiered alerts (tier 1: low-confidence "worth mentioning"; tier 2: medium-confidence "talk to doctor soon"; tier 3: high-confidence "call doctor now"), users need to learn to distinguish these tiers and act appropriately. Most won't—they'll treat all alerts the same. This defeats the credentialing model. Mitigation: implement behavioral nudges in the app that train users through repetition—show the alert tier explanation every time, vary the visual design sharply between tiers, and include a "quiz" onboarding flow that asks users to classify sample scenarios before they ever see a real alert.

* **The cardiologist partnership shields you only from the partnership scope.** If you partner with a cardiology practice and they review high-confidence alerts, you've established a clinical credentialing relationship for that tier. But you're still liable for low and medium confidence alerts that go to consumers, and the gap between those tiers is a liability minefield. Mitigation: have the cardiology partner co-author a white paper defining the clinical utility and limitations of each alert tier; use this white paper in user onboarding and in legal defense as evidence that you've disclosed the boundaries of the device's clinical applicability.

* **The liability surface doesn't shrink when you credentialize—it shifts.** Moving liability to credentialed cardiologists via a formal channel actually exposes them to liability for *not acting* on a high-confidence alert they missed or deprioritized. If you're going to create a credentialing channel, you're creating accountability for the credentialed parties. Mitigation: ensure your partnership agreement includes clear liability allocation—cardiologists review alerts as a courtesy or research collaboration, not as a clinical service under their standard of care; document this in the partnership agreement and in the alert user interface.

## Q5: Calibrated deception—the 48-hour forced cool-down

* **Deliberate information withholding is a minefield legally and ethically.** If you detect an arrhythmia episode and suppress the alert for 48 hours, you're making a clinical judgment (that waiting is harmless) that you have no authority to make as a device manufacturer. If that withheld alert corresponded to the start of a stroke-causing event, you're liable for negligence (failure to warn). The fact that your *intent* was beneficial doesn't shield you legally. Mitigation: if you want to explore delayed-alert strategies, do it *only* in a clinical research protocol with IRB approval and informed consent; don't ship it as a hidden feature to consumers.

* **The anxiety reduction benefit is unquantified and user-specific.** The hypothesis that a 48-hour delay reduces anxiety and improves outcomes is plausible but unproven. Some users might benefit; others might suffer severe anxiety wondering why the device detected something and didn't tell them. You can't generalize a hypothesis into a design decision without evidence. Mitigation: if you want to test delayed alerting, offer it as an *opt-in* user preference with clear explanation ("alerts delayed by 48 hours to reduce false-alarm anxiety"), collect data on user outcomes and anxiety levels, and publish results; don't use it as a default hidden mechanism.

* **"Deception" language itself signals a problematic design.** The fact that you're calling it "calibrated deception" suggests you know it's ethically uncomfortable. When you feel that discomfort, the right move is to stop and redesign, not to rename the problem. Users have a right to know what their device is detecting and when. Mitigation: reframe the problem: instead of delaying alerts, design a *confidence-based notification strategy* where high-confidence signals alert immediately, and low-confidence signals are held until confidence increases or the pattern repeats, with *full user visibility* into why an alert was delayed and what the device detected.

* **The experiment would require a control group and a long timeline.** To know if 48-hour delays actually improve outcomes, you'd need to run a randomized trial comparing delayed vs. immediate alerts over 12–24 months, measuring both false-positive anxiety and missed-event severity. You don't have time for this before launch, and you can't ship an experiment to production users. Mitigation: run a small ethics-approved pilot study (100 users, 12 weeks, clear informed consent) testing alert-delay preferences; measure anxiety and engagement as proxies for outcome, and use results to inform your launch design—but ship with *transparency*, not hidden mechanisms.

* **Discovery will weaponize "deception" language against you.** If this design choice ever surfaces in litigation, the opposing counsel will hang the word "deception" around your neck. Juries respond poorly to the narrative "the company deliberately hid information from users to manipulate their behavior." Even if the intent was benevolent, the framing is catastrophic. Mitigation: abandon the hidden-delay concept entirely; if you want to explore alert strategies, do it transparently with user choice and clinical evidence backing every design decision.

## Q6: Designed failure mode—engineered 1% error rate and trust paradox

* **A claimed 1% error rate makes you liable for every single error.** You're proposing to publicly disclose that your device is wrong 1 in 100 times. This sounds honest, but legally, it means you've admitted a known defect. If a user is in the 1% and suffers harm, you've pre-confessed that you designed a device you knew would fail them. Courts may interpret this not as transparency but as reckless indifference. Mitigation: don't frame it as "engineered error"—instead, frame it as "calibrated confidence": publish your sensitivity/specificity tradeoff as a chart showing which conditions you're optimized to detect (high confidence) and which have remaining uncertainty (low confidence); don't claim a global error rate, claim condition-specific performance.

* **The trust paradox hypothesis is unproven and user-specific.** You're hypothesizing that users trust a device that admits imperfection more than a device claiming perfection. This is psychologically plausible (studies on organizational transparency support this), but individual variation is huge. Some users will hear "1% error rate" and immediately stop trusting; others will appreciate the honesty. You can't make a global design choice based on an untested hypothesis. Mitigation: run a small A/B study (200 users, 6 weeks) comparing two companion-app onboarding flows—one emphasizing "we detect 99% of events" (aspirational framing) vs. "we detect events with X% sensitivity" (technical framing)—and measure engagement, alert-following behavior, and user-reported trust over time.

* **1% error rate is too coarse to be actionable.** The actual false-positive and false-negative rates are probably different (you might have 2% false negatives and 0.5% false positives, or vice versa). Users need to know *which kind* of error they're at risk of—missing a real event vs. getting a spurious alert—not a blended global error rate. Mitigation: publish condition-specific operating points instead: "for arrhythmia detection, we achieve 88% sensitivity at 95% specificity; for sleep apnea, 82% sensitivity at 93% specificity"—this is honest, specific, and actionable for different user contexts.

* **Public disclosure of a 1% failure rate invites regulatory scrutiny.** An FDA examiner reading your documentation will ask: "If you know the device fails 1% of the time, how did you justify clearing it?" If you're still in consumer wellness (pre-510k), you're creating a paper trail that explicitly documents a known defect, which may trigger enforcement action if downstream harm occurs. Mitigation: keep detailed failure-mode analysis internal (for your team's design decisions), but communicate *confidence and limitations* to users in accessible language, not statistical failure rates; let the technical documentation speak to regulators, and let user-facing communication speak to users.

* **The trust claim inverts if you frame it as marketing.** "We're engineered to fail 1% of the time" sounds like a feature if positioned as transparency, but sounds like a bug if positioned as a selling point. Litigation discovery will parse every statement to find inconsistency—if you used the 1% error rate in a marketing deck (claiming users trust you *because* you're imperfect), you've moved from transparency to manipulation. Mitigation: keep discussion of calibrated error rates in technical documentation and research contexts only; don't use it in consumer-facing marketing; let the device's actual performance speak for itself through user reviews and independent validation.

## Q7: False-positive tolerance as a calibration advantage

* **Consumer wellness tolerance window is narrow and closing fast.** You propose that looser thresholds now (higher sensitivity, more false positives) will build a richer training dataset for future clinical models. This is strategically sound, but there's a risk: if your consumer users get alert-fatigued and stop engaging with the device, you won't get the behavioral feedback you need to refine your clinical model. The dataset decays in quality over time as engaged users drop out. Mitigation: implement a tiered data strategy from day one—label different populations of users (beta testers, engaged users, casual users) and weight their feedback separately when building the clinical dataset; focus data collection on the cohorts that are most engaged and most likely to continue wearing the device.

* **"Building a dataset" is not a user benefit—it's a business benefit hidden in a design choice.** If your high false-positive rate is actually a strategy to collect edge cases for future clinical models, you're using your consumer users as unwitting research subjects. This is ethically fraught. You're telling them "we need to alert you to build a better device later," but their actual experience is alert fatigue. Mitigation: if you're genuinely using consumer deployment as a data-collection phase for clinical development, be transparent about this phase—position the consumer wellness launch as "phase 1" of a multi-year clinical development program, explain the data-collection strategy in onboarding, and offer a "clinical research participant" option for users willing to tolerate higher false-positive rates explicitly for this purpose.

* **The dataset quality decays without clinical ground truth.** You can collect edge cases (unusual heart-rate patterns, borderline signals), but without clinical ECGs or outcomes data to label them, you've built a dataset of ambiguous signals. When you eventually conduct clinical trials, you'll have to relabel everything retroactively, which is a massive effort. Mitigation: partner with a healthcare provider now to collect periodic gold-standard (clinical ECG) validation data from a subset of your consumer users (100–200 engaged users, quarterly checkups); use this small labeled dataset to anchor your edge-case training set, making it clinically meaningful.

* **Regulatory will view "we're collecting data for future clinical models" as an admission of current inadequacy.** If you submit a 510(k) application later, the FDA will ask: "If your consumer model was generating a valuable clinical dataset, why wasn't it sufficiently validated for clinical use to begin with?" The narrative becomes "we knew our device wasn't ready for clinical claims, so we shipped it to consumers to gather data first," which reads as regulatory arbitrage. Mitigation: don't frame the consumer launch as a data-collection strategy; frame it as a legitimate consumer wellness tool with known operating characteristics—separate from, not prerequisite to, future clinical development. Document your clinical development roadmap independently.

* **Alert threshold creep will bias your dataset.** If your initial model is calibrated to ~80% sensitivity (many false positives), and you gradually tighten thresholds based on user feedback (reducing false positives), you're changing the data-generation distribution over time. This makes your dataset heterogeneous and harder to use for clinical training. Mitigation: freeze your alert thresholds for the first 12 months of consumer deployment (don't adjust based on false-positive feedback); log all inference decisions at the frozen operating point to create a homogeneous dataset; after 12 months, perform a formal rebalancing based on the accumulated data, and document the version boundary in your dataset pipeline.

* **You need to own the data to use it for model development.** If users own their own health data (increasingly, they do), and they revoke permission for its use in model training, your dataset shrinks. If you've built your clinical development roadmap on the assumption that you have 24 months of continuous consumer data, you need a contractual framework that secures data-use rights upfront. Mitigation: in your terms of service, offer users a choice: "basic" (data encrypted on device, no research use) or "research contributor" (explicit opt-in to use their anonymized data for clinical model development, with the understanding they'll be among the first to get any future clinical-grade version). Make research participation valuable to users (early access to updates, premium features) so the incentive aligns.
