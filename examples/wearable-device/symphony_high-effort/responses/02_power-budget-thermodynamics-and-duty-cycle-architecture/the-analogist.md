# Power Budget, Thermodynamics, and Duty-Cycle Architecture - The Analogist

---

## Q1: Battery as Keystone Constraint and Zero-Based Budget

**Summary.** Explore the 7-day battery target as a hard constraint that shapes which sensors and inference modes are physically achievable, not as a goal to engineer toward. A zero-based energy budget starting from total capacity (not component specs) reveals which subsystem is the genuine bottleneck. Independent validation of feasibility with commercial hardware is critical. Consider whether users would accept shorter battery life (e.g., 3 days) in exchange for materially better detection accuracy.

### Response 1.1: The Urban Water System Analogy (Infrastructure Frame)

This reminds me of how cities approach water distribution under drought. The 7-day battery is your total water allocation; you don't start by speccing pumps and pipes — you start with the basin capacity and work backward. Municipalities discovered that trying to maintain uniform flow to all neighborhoods was inefficient; instead, they created tiered-demand zones where some neighborhoods operate on intermittent supply (neighborhood cisterns store during high-flow hours, release at night). Your wearable should mirror this: reserve your mWh/day budget for the critical inference windows (sleep, post-exercise recovery) and accept that daytime monitoring may be event-triggered only. The bottleneck in water systems is never the pipe itself; it's usually the treatment plant or the pumping schedule. Identify your wearable's analog (probably continuous PPG sensing or BLE sync) and architect around that, not around component marketing specs. Has your team actually run a 7-day test with all four sensors simultaneously on the chosen NPU? If not, you're optimizing fiction, not physics.

### Response 1.2: The Energy Industry's Reserve Margin Insight (Risk/Hedging Frame)

Power grids maintain a "reserve margin" — they don't operate at peak capacity because margin absorbs spikes and failures. Your zero-based budget should include a safety margin for model recalibration, unexpected sensor drift, and firmware updates. I've seen medical device teams engineer toward a 7-day spec only to discover that four months of real-world use, battery aging, and temperature variance reduce practical life to 5 days. The analogous approach: utilities assume a 15-20% reserve against peak demand; you might reserve 20% of your mWh budget for environmental headroom. This is a multi-quarter validation problem, not a design problem. Run hardware-in-the-loop testing at 5°C and 35°C under continuous monitoring for two weeks. The math says 7 days is possible; the physics will show whether 3-day cycles with better accuracy are actually the market solution.

### Response 1.3: The Pacemaker Design Lineage (Clinical Precedent Frame)

Pacemakers faced an identical problem in the 1980s: continuous sensing vs. event-triggered pacing. Cardiologists initially demanded round-the-clock monitoring, but device engineers quickly discovered that intermittent (demand-paced) architectures extended battery life 2-3x while maintaining clinical efficacy. The shift happened when clinicians accepted that detecting an arrhythmia within a few beats, not instantly, was good enough. For your wearable, ask cardiologists (and your eventual clinical partners) whether detecting an arrhythmia 30 seconds after onset — using event-triggered inference — meets their clinical bar. If it does, you've unlocked a 3-5x power saving. The regulatory parallel is instructive too: the FDA's guidance evolved from continuous monitoring toward risk-based intermittent monitoring. You might launch at 7 days with continuous sensing to compete in wellness, but clinical positioning could pivot to "30-second detection latency with 14-day battery" — a different optimization entirely that users might prefer.

### Response 1.4: The Sensor Network Deployment Model (Operations Frame)

Environmental sensor networks (soil moisture, air quality) use a pattern called "multi-scale temporal sampling": a few nodes sample continuously, most sample intermittently, and aggregation happens asynchronously. Applied to your wearable: have a lightweight analog heartbeat monitor run continuously (almost no power), use it to gate the expensive PPG + NPU inference (fire only when the analog monitor detects an anomaly), and batch inference results for async BLE sync. This is how wildfire detection networks operate — continuous 1-Hz thermal imaging is prohibitive, so they use motion-triggered high-res snapshots. Your zero-based budget should split into tiers: always-on (analog gating), intermittent (ML inference), and batched (radio/sync). This stratification often yields a 50-70% power reduction vs. uniform continuous operation. Validation step: implement a simple R-R interval comparator on analog hardware and measure how often it would have triggered the NPU in a 24-hour clinical dataset. That percentage tells you the maximum power savings available.

## Q2: Thermodynamic Lower Bound and Analog Gating

**Summary.** Shannon entropy and Landauer's principle define a physical floor on energy per inference. Quantify how far current NPUs operate above that floor to distinguish whether the power problem is fundamentally a hardware gap or algorithmic inefficiency. Explore whether a simple analog threshold (e.g., R-R interval regularity) could gate the NPU and eliminate what fraction of total inference energy by moving coarse detection earlier in the signal chain.

### Response 2.1: The Radio Receiver Sensitivity Analogy (Physics Framing)

This resembles how radio engineers approach signal detection at the quantum limit. Shannon's theorem sets a floor on energy-per-bit, but that floor is theoretical; real receivers operate 10-20 dB above it because noise, encoding overhead, and error correction add layers. Your NPU's thermodynamic gap is the same: the theoretical minimum energy to distinguish arrhythmia from normal rhythm is perhaps 100 picojoules (given the entropy of the decision), but the hardware, quantization, and inference overhead push real systems to 10-100 nanojoules per inference. That 100-1000x gap is your design space. Plug in the arithmetic: if your NPU consumes 1 mJ per inference and runs 100 times per minute, you're burning 100 mJ/minute on inference alone. A simple analog R-R interval comparator (power-law simpler: maybe 1 microjoule) triggers the NPU only on anomalous beats, reducing inference count by 95-99% in normal sinus rhythm. The bottleneck is rarely the physics; it's the architectural choice to run inference uniformly rather than adaptively. Measure this: log every inference and histogram the decision entropy in real clinical data. Are you actually computing new information each time, or repeating a "normal" classification thousands of times?

### Response 2.2: The Hearing Aids Surprise (Unexpected Hardware Solution Frame)

Modern hearing aids use an architecture that's counterintuitive: continuous audio capture (high power), but the signal processing is hierarchical. Low-power analog preprocessing filters sound into frequency bands and classifies each band (speech vs. wind vs. silence) before expensive digital DSP runs. This isn't optimization theory; it's multi-stage gating. Applied to your wearable: run a low-power analog front-end that computes R-R interval variance continuously (maybe a simple voltage comparator on the PPG). When variance exceeds a threshold, fire the ML inference; otherwise, the device "hears" a steady heartbeat and stays quiet. This cascade is why hearing aids can run for a week on a single battery. The analog stage eliminates perhaps 98% of inference cycles in normal operation. The regulatory advantage is subtle but real: if an arrhythmia occurs during a silent (non-inferencing) period, your device still detects it once the next inference window opens. This grace period (say, 30-60 seconds) is medically acceptable for most arrhythmias and avoids the false-negative liability of purely analog thresholding.

### Response 2.3: The Thermal Imaging Surveillance Strategy (Implementation Depth Frame)

Security systems that monitor for intrusion use a two-stage thermal detector: a coarse analog motion sensor (passive IR, almost free power) gates expensive thermal imaging. In your case, the coarse stage could be an edge-detection filter on raw PPG (identifying beat boundaries), and the expensive stage is the ML model that classifies each beat. Many wearable teams skip this because the math seems redundant (why compute beat edges twice?), but hardware engineers know that shifting computation to different substrates (analog comparator vs. digital DSP vs. ML accelerator) yields massive power gains because you avoid serialization overhead. Concretely: implement a 3-line analog comparator circuit that fires when PPG dV/dt (rate of change) exceeds a threshold for 2+ consecutive beats. This flags potential anomalies for NPU review. It costs 10-20 microjoules per heartbeat; ML inference costs 1+ millijoules. The fraction of cycles eliminated depends on your patient population, but even 90% elimination (a modest estimate) saves 0.9 mJ per beat out of 1.1 mJ total — a 10x reduction in inference energy.

## Q3: Forced Simplicity as a Feature

**Summary.** Intermittent or event-triggered sensing, imposed by battery constraints, might create a device that users prefer — one that feels less surveillance-like and more intentionally responsive. This reframes a hardware limitation as a UX advantage.

### Response 3.1: The Meditation Bell Metaphor (Intentionality Frame)

This reminds me of how meditation practices use interval bells: rather than constant ambient sound, a bell rings periodically to anchor attention. Many users find this more restorative than continuous monitoring. Your wearable could adopt the same principle — instead of constant PPG sampling, you might say "the device checks your heart every 5 minutes" or "it listens for anomalies during sleep and after exercise." Users who've grown fatigued by fitness trackers that quantify every step might actually prefer a device that's "smart about when it pays attention" rather than panopticon-like surveillance. Psychologically, this feels less intrusive. Technically, it's forced simplicity: no always-on display, no constant sync, no battery drain from ambient awareness. The trade-off is latency — you might detect an arrhythmia 2-5 minutes after it starts rather than instantly — but for non-acute arrhythmias (many are paroxysmal and self-limiting), this delay is clinically acceptable. The UX messaging matters: "respects your privacy" and "focuses on moments that matter" are stronger than "7-day battery."

### Response 3.2: The Mechanical Watch Complication Model (Minimalism Frame)

Watchmakers distinguish between complications (functions) and timekeeping; a calendar complication runs on-demand, not constantly. Your wearable could mirror this: the core function is "alert me if something's wrong," and the continuous monitoring is a complication you can deactivate. Intermittent sensing forced by battery realities becomes a feature: users disable continuous monitoring in low-risk contexts (sitting at desk, social settings) and enable it during high-risk windows (sleep, exercise, recovery). This is less paternalistic than a device that always knows your heart rate. Mechanically, a watch with fewer complications is also more reliable; fewer moving parts, fewer battery drain points. Your device gains the same advantage: intermittent inference is simpler firmware, simpler ML orchestration, simpler failure modes. The forced constraint becomes an aesthetic and operational virtue. Regulatory messaging: "Medically-grade monitoring, activated when you need it most" is honestly simpler to defend in a 510(k) than "continuous monitoring that sometimes delivers false alerts."

### Response 3.3: The Field Hospital Triage Precedent (Pragmatic Clinical Frame)

Field medicine uses triage — constant monitoring is impossible, so resources focus on high-risk patients and crisis moments. Your wearable could adopt this paradigm: baseline intermittent sensing during routine hours, elevated monitoring during sleep (highest arrhythmia risk), and surge monitoring immediately post-exercise. This is not a limitation; it's evidence-based targeting of when arrhythmias are most likely and most clinically significant. A 3-day battery with continuous monitoring is actually less useful than 7 days with smart intermittent monitoring, because the intermittent version captures the clinically relevant windows and gives users flexibility (take the device off for a shower, know you still have power for the night). The medical precedent: Holter monitors use 24-48 hour cycles precisely because event timing matters, and 7 days of mundane sinus rhythm is less valuable than 2 days of strategically-timed data. Your constraint becomes a feature: users buy a device that's designed around when they need it, not one trying to be always-on.

## Q4: Stage Management and Power Budget Allocation

**Summary.** Theater stage managers coordinate competing resource-intensive cues to avoid simultaneous peaks. Apply this choreography metaphor to firmware scheduling: coordinate sensing, inference, sync, and display across a shared power budget. Pre-computed cue sequences, resource reservation windows, and graceful degradation could inform scheduler design. Physiological context (sleep, exercise, stillness) could trigger power mode shifts.

### Response 4.1: The Concert Lighting Rig Analogy (Real-Time Coordination Frame)

A concert lighting rig has hundreds of fixtures but a limited power supply; the lighting designer doesn't run everything simultaneously because the grid can't deliver it. Instead, cues are choreographed: strobe lights fire during high-energy moments when background washes are dimmed, then reverse. Applied to your firmware: PPG sensing, ML inference, BLE sync, and display refresh each consume meaningful power. Never run all four simultaneously. Design cue sequences: during sleep, run continuous PPG + ML but disable BLE/display; post-exercise, run inference + BLE sync but reduce display refresh rate. The scheduler becomes a state machine: SLEEP (low-power intermittent PPG + inference, no comms), ACTIVE (event-triggered inference, batched BLE), ALERT (maximum PPG/inference, real-time display). Transitions are triggered by accelerometer context (movement, stillness) and time-of-day heuristics. This is standard in stage lighting; it's not standard in wearables because teams haven't thought of the power budget as a choreography problem. Implementation: pre-compute state transition rules (e.g., "if accelerometer silent for 60 seconds and HR stable, drop to SLEEP state") and reserve 2-3% of your mWh budget for state transition overhead.

### Response 4.2: The Traffic Light Intersection Optimization (Algorithmic Prioritization Frame)

Traffic engineers optimize signal timing to batch vehicle flows, avoiding gridlock where four directions compete for intersection capacity. Your wearable faces the same problem: four subsystems (sensing, inference, radio, display) compete for your power budget "intersection." Traffic signals use algorithms like SCATS that measure queue depth and extend green time where demand is highest. Analogously, your firmware could measure queue depth (pending inference tasks, queued BLE packets) and allocate power accordingly: if the ML inference backlog is high, prioritize that over BLE sync; if BLE has queued alerts, spike power there temporarily. This is "adaptive scheduling" in real-time systems, but the traffic metaphor makes it clear: you're not trying to give equal time to all directions; you're prioritizing based on real demand. In practice: instrument your firmware to log queue depths every second, analyze 24-hour logs to find patterns (e.g., "BLE queues spike at 8 AM when companion app syncs"), and then pre-allocate power windows to match those patterns. A user wearing the device during their morning commute knows sync happens during their shower; the device "knows" to stage its power consumption to match. This is choreography by data.

### Response 4.3: The Hospital Operating Room Schedule Analogy (Graceful Degradation Frame)

An operating room schedules multiple surgeries with constraints: the autoclave (sterilization) takes 20 minutes, anesthesia prep takes 10 minutes, and surgeries run 2+ hours. If an emergency case arrives, the scheduler gracefully degrades: low-priority surgeries move to later time slots, resources are reserved for the critical patient. Your wearable scheduler should operate similarly: normal operation runs your baseline cue sequence (scheduled sensing, batched inference, periodic sync). When an anomaly is detected, the device enters an "alert state" that gracefully degrades non-critical functions: background BLE syncing pauses, display polling increases, and inference runs at full power. This ensures that a detected arrhythmia gets your full computational and communication bandwidth without artificially constraining earlier, low-risk moments. Regulatory advantage: if you're ever asked "does your device respond quickly to detected anomalies?" the answer is "yes, because we reserve emergency capacity and degrade non-critical functions." This is how ICU monitors operate. Implementation: define your alert state explicitly (detect → trigger alert cue → max inference power, real-time display, immediate BLE sync) and test it in your power budget to confirm it doesn't break the 7-day target even if an anomaly occurs every day.

### Response 4.4: The Seasonal Electrical Load Management Pattern (Contextual Switching Frame)

Power utilities shift generation patterns seasonally: summer relies more on hydroelectric (cheap, reliable), winter emphasizes fossil fuels. Wearable firmware could adopt seasonal or contextual load shifts: winter months (users indoors, stress-related arrhythmias common) might emphasize continuous inference; summer (outdoor activity, fewer arrhythmias in healthy populations) might reduce inference frequency. More practically, the device learns user patterns: a user who exercises daily at 6 PM has predictable arrhythmia risk windows; the device pre-allocates power reserves for that window. This isn't magic; it's learned scheduling. Many wearable teams avoid this because it adds complexity, but consider: utilities manage this because it's economical. If you can predict the 10-20 highest-risk hours of the week for a given user (sleep, post-exercise, emotional stress), you can front-load power reserves during those windows and reduce power budget pressure elsewhere. Implementation: after 2-3 weeks of wearing the device, the firmware has a model of when anomalies occur in that user's physiology; it tunes its scheduling to those windows. This is closed-loop optimization of a choreography problem. Users experience this as "the device seems smarter about when it checks my heart," not as a limitation.
