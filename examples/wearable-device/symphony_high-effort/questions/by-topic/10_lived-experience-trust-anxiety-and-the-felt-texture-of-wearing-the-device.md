# Topic Cluster 10: Lived Experience — Trust, Anxiety, and the Felt Texture of Wearing the Device

## Questions

1. **The 2am alarm moment and alarm-fatigue as emotional erosion**: When an alert fires at 2am, what is the emotional texture — the spike of fear, the scramble for context, the uncertainty about whether to panic or dismiss — and how does the design of that notification moment either amplify or soften the dread; and over time, when false positives accumulate (during a workout, during an argument), people don't just stop trusting the alerts — they start *resenting the device itself*, and that resentment is a product failure no accuracy metric captures. At what point does a user emotionally disengage from alerts not because they decided to but because the accumulated experience of false alarms slowly trained them to feel nothing — and how does the design of alert cadence and communication need to treat that emotional erosion as a first-class risk, not a secondary UX concern?
2. **The 3am alert**: Imagine someone is asleep and the device detects a cardiac arrhythmia event — what does that first moment of notification feel like, and how does the design of the alert (sound, vibration, language on screen) determine whether they take lifesaving action, ignore it as a false alarm, or are left paralyzed by fear?
3. **The boy who cried wolf**: Imagine a user receives three false-positive arrhythmia alerts in a single week — trace the emotional and behavioral arc from the first alert (alarmed, calls doctor) to the third (annoyed, dismisses it) — and what does this erosion of credibility mean for the one real alert that may follow?
4. **The missed event**: Tell the story from the other direction: a user who trusts the device implicitly experiences a genuine sleep apnea episode that the model classifies as normal movement artifact — how does this false negative shape their future relationship with the device, their doctor, and their own body's signals?
5. **Living under the gaze, pre-patient identity, and the visible-device stigma**: What does it feel like to wear a device perpetually watching for signs of danger in your own body, and how might that low-grade awareness shift from reassuring to quietly oppressive over weeks and months — especially for people who carry the family-history knowledge that they might become sick and strap on that pre-patient identity every morning; does the device reinforce a fear-based relationship with the body or invite a different, more empowered one; does its job of "catching what you might miss" feel like a safety net, a surrogate for someone who loves you, or an admission that you can no longer trust yourself to notice; and (since a wrist-worn monitor is socially visible in a way a hidden implant is not) how will wearers feel when someone at a dinner table asks "what's that for?" — does the device's form factor and framing give them language and emotional confidence to answer, or make them feel defined by their vulnerability?
6. **False-negative grief and the betrayal arc**: For the person who wore the device faithfully and still had an event the device missed, what does that betrayal feel like — and how does the product need to reckon with the emotional weight of that failure, not just the clinical liability? How does this false negative shape their future relationship with the device, their doctor, and their own body's signals?
7. **The privacy comfort gap (felt vs. understood)**: People say they care about data privacy, but their emotional relationship to "my raw heartbeat data never leaves this device" is different from their intellectual understanding of it — does the on-device architecture feel like a gift, a wall, or something they'll never truly believe, and does the device's design surface that reassurance in a way the body can feel, not just the mind?
8. **The data conversation they never have**: When a user's cardiologist asks "can you share your device data with my clinic," what story does the user tell themselves about where their raw biometric data lives, who can see it, and whether that invisible architecture of on-device processing actually matches their mental model — and what happens to trust when those stories diverge?
9. **Family pressure and shared data**: A user's adult child insists on having access to the health alerts "just to be safe" — what boundaries does the product design create or erase around the user's right to private health information within their own household, and how might pressure dynamics around family caregiving undermine the autonomy of the person wearing the device?
10. **The gradual trust arc**: A 52-year-old with a family history of heart disease starts wearing the device — how does their relationship with it evolve from skeptical newcomer to reliant companion over the first 90 days, and what moments of friction or delight mark each stage of that trust-building journey?
11. **The invisible handshake (onboarding as values disclosure)**: Walk through the moment a user sets up the companion app for the first time and encounters permissions, sync settings, and privacy language — what narrative does this onboarding experience communicate about the company's values, and how might a single confusing toggle permanently color how much the user engages with health insights going forward?
12. **The day it becomes medical**: Picture a user who has worn the device for two years as a wellness tool and then learns their doctor is now using aggregated patterns from it in a formal clinical assessment — how does that shift in how the device is *used* change how the user feels about wearing it, what data they'd want captured, and whether they'd have consented differently at the start?
13. **Invisible diagnosis**: What if the entire interaction model were reimagined so that alerts, dashboards, and companion apps — the whole apparatus of "being monitored" — were replaced by something categorically different: a form of subtle environmental or somatic cuing that doesn't interrupt consciousness but simply shapes behavior, the way light shapes mood, without the wearer ever needing to think about their health at all?
14. **From detection to prevention at the cellular level**: What if we inverted the temporal frame entirely — rather than detecting arrhythmias or apnea events after they occur, the system developed a model of precondition states that exist hours or days before any event, and intervened not through alerts but through micro-adjustments to environment, behavior, or even the wearer's nervous system, making the detectable event itself a failure mode rather than a success?
