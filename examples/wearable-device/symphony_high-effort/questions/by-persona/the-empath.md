# Brainstorming Questions from The Empath: On-Device ML Health Monitoring Wearable

---

## The Weight of Being Watched

1. **Living under the gaze**: What does it feel like to wear a device that is perpetually watching for signs of danger in your own body — and how might that low-grade awareness shift from reassuring to quietly oppressive over the weeks and months of continuous use?

2. **The alarm moment**: When an alert fires at 2am telling you something might be wrong with your heart, what is the emotional texture of that experience — the spike of fear, the scramble for context, the uncertainty about whether to panic or dismiss it — and how does the design of that notification moment either amplify or soften the dread?

3. **False negative grief**: For the person who wore the device faithfully and still had an event the device missed, what does that betrayal feel like — and how does the product need to reckon with the emotional weight of that failure, not just the clinical liability?

---

## Trust Built in the Body

4. **Earning the wrist**: A wearable health device is asking for something intimate — constant physical contact, biometric access, emotional reliance. What does it actually feel like to hand that kind of trust over to a hardware startup, and what would need to be true about this device for that trust to feel earned rather than naive?

5. **The privacy comfort gap**: People say they care about data privacy, but their emotional relationship to "my raw heartbeat data never leaves this device" is different from their intellectual understanding of it. Does the on-device architecture feel like a gift, like a wall, or like something they'll never truly believe — and does the device's design surface that reassurance in a way the body can feel, not just the mind?

6. **The "watch over me" relationship**: There is something emotionally significant about a device whose job is to catch what you might miss about your own health. How does that caretaking dynamic land — does it feel like a safety net, a surrogate for someone who loves you, or an admission that you can no longer trust yourself to notice?

---

## Identity and the Condition You Don't Have Yet

7. **Pre-patient identity**: This device targets people with a family history of cardiac events — people who are not sick but who carry the knowledge that they might become sick. What does it feel like to strap on that identity every morning, and how does the device either reinforce a fear-based relationship with the body or invite a different, more empowered one?

8. **The stigma of the visible device**: A wrist-worn health monitor is visible in social situations in a way a hidden implant is not. How will wearers feel in the moment someone at a dinner table asks "what's that for?" — and does the device's form factor and framing give them language and emotional confidence to answer, or does it make them feel defined by their vulnerability?

---

## The Emotional Life of False Positives

9. **Alarm fatigue as emotional erosion**: When a device generates alerts that turn out to be nothing — a false positive during a workout, a spike during an argument — what happens to the emotional relationship with the device over time? My gut says people don't just stop trusting the alerts; they start resenting the device itself, and that resentment is a product failure no accuracy metric captures.

10. **The cry-wolf tipping point**: At what point does a user emotionally disengage from the alerts — not because they decided to, but because the accumulated experience of false alarms slowly trained them to feel nothing when the device fires? And how does the design of alert cadence and communication need to treat that emotional erosion as a first-class risk, not a secondary UX concern?
