# Strengths-Based Vision for the Focused Product - The Devil's Advocate

---

## Team Pride Moment

* **Nostalgia Is Not Signal.** Have we considered that what team members point to with pride might reflect engineering effort or personal attachment rather than user value? The social challenge feature might be "genuinely good" in the team's eyes because it was hard to build — not because users care. We could address this by requiring any "pride nomination" to be validated against a specific metric: does that feature have measurably higher engagement or retention than the others?

* **The Six-Person Consensus Trap.** One potential challenge is that a team of six will surface six different "that part is genuinely good" moments, each colored by whose area of ownership it is. The designer will point to the onboarding illustrations; the engineers will point to the workout tracker's sync reliability. This isn't a strengths inventory — it's a territory map. We could address this by separating feature pride votes from feature usage data and only treating convergence between the two as a real signal.

* **Burnout Distorts Perception.** Have we considered that burned-out teams systematically misidentify strengths? When a team has been maintaining 15 half-built features under runway pressure, "standing out" often just means "recently touched" — the thing that still feels fresh in memory. The last feature a developer refactored feels good because they understand it, not because it's the right bet. Mitigation: use a 90-day lookback on user engagement data rather than team memory as the primary input to any strengths inventory.

* **Pride Can Coexist with Irrelevance.** The team might identify the meal planner's recipe import tool as genuinely polished — and they may be right. But a fitness app's core loop almost certainly isn't meals. One potential challenge is that a genuinely good ancillary feature becomes the anchor for the relaunch when it should be cut entirely. We could address this by asking a second question alongside "what's genuinely good?": "what's genuinely good *and* directly connected to why someone downloads a fitness app in the first place?"

## Feature Selection Anchored in Strength

* **Engagement Is Not Retention.** Have we considered that "resonates with most engaged users" is measuring the wrong population? The top 5% of engaged users on a 2% DAU/MAU app represent an extreme minority who may not share any characteristics with the 92% who churned. Building a focused product around their signal could produce something that perfectly serves 3,600 users and no one else. Mitigation: weight your signal by users who engaged *and* stayed for 60+ days, not just those who engaged most intensely.

* **Sunk Cost Has a Structural Advantage in Any Decision Process.** One potential challenge with any "let signal guide selection" framework is that sunk costs enter the room disguised as operational arguments: "we can't cut the coaching module, it's deeply integrated." A governance structure that explicitly separates technical effort from user value is necessary — but someone in the room with authority to override the engineers when they say "but it's already built" is equally necessary. Have we named who that person is?

* **Three or Four Features May Still Be Too Many.** The question assumes three to four capabilities is the right scope. But with 8 months of runway and a team of seven, that might still be too much to execute to a high standard. One potential challenge is that the "focused product" becomes a six-feature product with two cuts rather than a genuinely minimal one. We could address this by forcing a ranked selection where only the top *two* features are funded to full quality, with the third and fourth held as conditional additions only after metrics prove the core is working.

* **Users Who "Resonate" with a Feature May Not Have Been the Target.** Have we considered that the feature with the strongest engagement signal may have attracted it accidentally — via a viral moment, an influencer post, or an App Store algorithm quirk — rather than because it genuinely fits the product's thesis? Mitigation: cross-reference engagement spikes with acquisition source data before treating any feature's usage numbers as a durable signal worth building around.

## The Future Users Are Grateful For

* **"Morning Coffee" Apps Are Extremely Rare.** Have we considered that framing the success vision as habitual, automatic daily use sets a bar that fewer than 1% of apps ever reach — and that planning toward it might distort near-term decisions? The team might overinvest in habit-loop mechanics (streaks, notifications, social accountability) trying to engineer daily use rather than building something users find genuinely valuable when they do open it. Mitigation: define a less extreme success metric for year one — "users open it when they need it and it works" — and reserve "morning coffee" as a year-three aspiration.

* **"What Would They Tell a Friend?" Favors Simple Stories.** One potential challenge with using word-of-mouth framing as a vision anchor is that it systemically favors features that are easy to describe over features that are deeply valuable. A user can easily tell a friend "it tracks my workouts" — they cannot easily say "it gives me a realistic picture of my fitness trajectory over 18 months." The latter might be more valuable. Have we considered that the vision test of "what they'd tell a friend" could bias the focused product toward shallow simplicity rather than deep usefulness?

* **App Store Reviews Reward Novelty, Not Sustained Value.** Have we considered that App Store review sentiment is heavily skewed toward the new-user experience and initial impressions? A three-year loyal user almost never writes a review unless prompted. The emotional arc we'd capture there reflects the onboarding experience far more than the sustained relationship. Mitigation: supplement the App Store review vision test with a different artifact — what does a user's *private journal entry* say about the app after three years of use?

* **The Grateful Future Assumes Users Who Stay.** One potential challenge with projecting forward to grateful users is that it sidesteps the harder question of why 92% of current users left within 30 days. A vision built around users who stayed and are grateful is implicitly a vision built around the current 2% — not around fixing whatever drove the 92% out. We could address this by running the future-vision exercise in two tracks: one for "retain the people who stayed," one for "win back the people who left."

## Radical Omission as Creative Act

* **Invention Requires Legitimacy the Team Hasn't Earned Yet.** Have we considered that "radical omission as invention" is a framing that requires a brand platform the team doesn't currently have? Apple could remove ports because users trusted Apple's judgment. A startup with 8% retention and burned bridges from a bloated product launching a radically stripped experience risks being read as "they ran out of money" rather than "they made a bold creative choice." Mitigation: earn the right to make the radical claim before making it — generate some proof of the core product's value before the relaunch narrative leans on intentional minimalism.

* **"Entirely New Relationship" Is a Marketing Story, Not a Product Decision.** One potential challenge with the "radical omission as creative act" frame is that it can seduce a team into spending design and positioning energy on a narrative rather than on the product. "We're inventing a new category" has killed many startups that used it to avoid the harder question of whether their product actually works yet. We could address this by separating the question into two phases: phase one, does the stripped-down product work at all? Phase two, is there a narrative worth telling about it?

* **Eliminating "Everything Familiar" Could Eliminate the Reason People Considered Downloading.** Have we considered that users who downloaded a fitness app — even this bloated one — were seeking something recognizable? They had a mental model of what a fitness app does. Departing radically from that model doesn't just differentiate the product; it creates a category mismatch that App Store search can't surface. Mitigation: map the "radically new relationship" concept against App Store discoverability — if no one searches for what you're describing, the invention is invisible.

* **The Team's Burnout Is a Creative Constraint, Not a Resource.** One potential challenge with "radical omission as invention" is that it's cognitively demanding work. It requires the team to hold a completely new product vision in mind while simultaneously executing a complex technical simplification under runway pressure. A burned-out team of seven has a limited capacity for creative reinvention alongside execution. We could address this by sequencing the creative ambition: nail the simplification first, run the product for 90 days, *then* ask the radical questions from a position of stability.

## The "Aha" Session

* **The Clean Screen Assumes Good Onboarding.** Have we considered that a stripped-down app with "one clear purpose" on a clean screen still fails if the first-time user experience doesn't explain what that purpose is, why it matters to them specifically, and what they should do in the next 30 seconds? The current app's problem isn't just feature count — it's that the product never clearly communicated its value. A minimal redesign can replicate that failure at smaller scale. Mitigation: the emotional arc from overwhelm to clarity isn't built into the product; it has to be designed, tested, and iterated on separately from the feature reduction.

* **Overwhelm-to-Clarity Is One Arc; There Are Others to Fear.** One potential challenge with framing the redesign around the overwhelm-to-clarity arc is that it sets up a comparison the new version might not survive. Users who felt "overwhelming" about the old version may bring skepticism to the relaunch — "they fixed the noise, but is there anything here worth staying for?" The clarity arc addresses the first 30 seconds; it doesn't address minutes 3 through 300. We could address this by mapping the full first-session arc: clarity at 30 seconds, first meaningful value delivered within 5 minutes, reason to return within the first session.

* **Existing Users Won't Experience the "First Time" Moment.** Have we considered that 180,000 people have already opened this app and formed an impression? For them, the "landing on a clean screen with one clear purpose" moment isn't discovery — it's a rebrand. Their emotional arc isn't overwhelm-to-clarity; it's skepticism-to-trust (or skepticism-to-abandonment). Mitigation: design two distinct first-time experiences — one for genuinely new users, one for returning users who need an explicit acknowledgment of what changed and why, before they'll engage with the simplified product on its own terms.

* **"Reliably" Is the Hardest Word in the Question.** One potential challenge is that making the clarity arc happen *reliably* — across different devices, connection speeds, user contexts, and prior exposure to fitness apps — is a systems problem, not a design problem. A beautifully designed first-session experience that breaks on an Android mid-range device or during a poor network moment will not produce the emotional arc. We could address this by defining "reliably" with a concrete performance benchmark before the relaunch: the first-session experience must deliver the intended arc for at least 85% of users on devices representing the bottom quartile of the current install base.
