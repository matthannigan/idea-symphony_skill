# Brainstorming Questions: Feature-Bloated Mobile App MVP Reset

---

## Topic Cluster 01: Diagnosing the Real Problem

**Cluster focus**: Test whether feature bloat is actually the cause of low retention before assuming simplification is the cure.

1. **Misattributed failure**: Have we confirmed that the 8% 30-day retention and 47-second sessions are caused by feature bloat at all — or might they reflect a flawed core value proposition that simplification alone cannot fix? The "overwhelming" feedback comes from users who stuck around long enough to complain; what about the 92% who churned, whose answer might be "I just don't want a fitness app at all" — and what evidence would distinguish complexity-driven churn from a value-proposition failure?
2. **Data gaps masking the real problem**: Do we actually have per-feature engagement data revealing which features users attempt versus abandon, and without it, isn't "strip to essentials" just a gut call dressed up as a data-driven decision?
3. **Retention signal contamination**: With 8% retention spread across 15 features, how do you separate the signal of a genuinely engaging feature from the noise of users who stayed because of a different feature they happened to also use?

## Topic Cluster 02: Reading the Engagement Signals

**Cluster focus**: Decompose the headline metrics (47-second session, 8% retention, 2% DAU/MAU) into actionable diagnostic signal.

1. **The 47-second session as ambiguous signal**: A 47-second average session could mean confused users leaving or efficient delivery of a quick check-in; how do you distinguish abandonment from fulfillment in your session data, and what would need to change in the first 10 seconds — when users feel the cognitive weight of 15 entry points — to reverse the pattern?
2. **47 seconds of truth**: A 47-second average session is usually read as failure, but what if it's a signal pointing toward a fundamentally different product paradigm — one where the entire value is delivered in a single, charged moment rather than sustained engagement? What product could be built around that constraint as a design principle rather than a problem to solve?
3. **Reward abandonment**: What if 8% 30-day retention is not a problem to solve but a product characteristic to exploit — an app designed to be used intensely for a month and then consciously set down? What would you build differently if the goal was to be the most useful app someone ever deleted? *The insight: designing for intentional lifecycle rather than perpetual engagement might produce a far more honest and beloved product.*
4. **Session length and retention curve as diagnostic**: How does the 47-second pattern decompose by feature in entry frequency, time-on-feature, and abandonment rate — and beyond the 8% headline, where in the user lifecycle is drop-off concentrated, with the curve shape suggesting a discovery, onboarding, or habit-formation problem?
5. **8% retention cohort stratification**: What does the 30-day retention distribution look like across user segments (users who found one feature they liked versus users who tried multiple), and does any single feature correlate with meaningfully higher retention when it is the first or most-used?

## Topic Cluster 03: Locating the True Core

**Cluster focus**: Find what users actually came for, beneath the 15 surface features.

1. **Why users actually opened it / peak-experience session**: In those 47-second sessions, what behavior did the 180K users exhibit — and among those who gave the most energized feedback, what were they doing in the app at the moment that interaction felt alive rather than frustrating?
2. **Retention bright spot**: Among the 8% of users who stuck around for 30 days, what pattern of behavior or feature combination do they share — and what does that tell us about what this product, at its best, already does remarkably well?
3. **Features as proxies for jobs-to-be-done**: Could the 15 features actually represent 2-3 underlying user jobs-to-be-done, and would framing the cut around those jobs lead to different decisions than framing it around feature names — and at 2% DAU/MAU there is already a small cohort using this app daily, so what are they actually doing in those sessions that may reveal the true core more reliably than any feature matrix?
4. **The "social fitness" assumption**: Why do we assume users want a social layer on top of fitness tracking at all? What evidence exists that the social feed was a user-requested desire rather than a team-initiated assumption about what would drive engagement?
5. **Single irreplaceable moment**: What if we completely reimagined the concept of "core feature" — not as a function users perform, but as an irreplaceable emotional moment that only this app creates? If 180K people downloaded this app, what singular feeling were they hoping to experience that no other product has ever given them?

## Topic Cluster 04: Strengths-Based Vision for the Focused Product

**Cluster focus**: Imagine the future the focused product makes possible, and let that vision pull selection forward rather than letting sunk costs push it.

1. **Team pride moment**: When has a member of this six-person team pointed to something in the app and said "that part — that's genuinely good"? What made it stand out amid everything else that felt half-built?
2. **Feature selection anchored in strength**: Given that some feature or interaction already resonates with your most engaged users, what decision-making structure would let that signal — rather than sunk costs or team attachment — guide which three or four capabilities make it into the focused product?
3. **The future users are grateful for**: If a year from now users opened this app the way they reach for morning coffee — automatically, eagerly, because it genuinely improves their day — what would they be doing in it, what would they tell a friend it's for, and if a loyal user three years from now wrote an App Store review capturing not what the app has but how it made them feel about their fitness life, what would that review say?
4. **Radical omission as creative act**: What if stripping down to the core wasn't a retreat but an act of invention — and the question isn't which features to keep, but what entirely new kind of relationship with users becomes possible only when you eliminate everything familiar about fitness apps?
5. **The "aha" session**: Picture the moment a user opens the stripped-down, relaunched app for the first time. They land on a clean screen with one clear purpose. Describe what they feel in the first 30 seconds — and contrast that with the story of what they felt the first time they opened the old version. What is the emotional arc from overwhelm to clarity, and what must the redesigned experience do to make that arc happen reliably?

## Topic Cluster 05: Methodology for the Cut

**Cluster focus**: How to actually decide which 3-4 features survive — criteria, evidence types, and the limits of pre-anchored answers.

1. **Where the line falls / three decision axes**: When the team says "identify the 3-4 core features," what criteria are they implicitly using — what users value, what the team can build well, or market differentiation? Data analysis, user interviews, and strategic vision aren't alternatives but different lenses (data tells you what users do, interviews tell you why, strategic vision tells you what you want to be true) — what happens if all three disagree, what systematic approach resolves the discrepancy, and which lens should have the final vote?
2. **The 3-4 feature assumption / smallest validating experiment**: What if user data points to 1 feature, or 6, and anchoring on "3-4" before analysis is complete biases the team toward rationalizing keeps and cuts? Before committing, what is the smallest experiment to validate that the chosen features together constitute a coherent product — meaning users who use them in combination retain at meaningfully higher rates than users who use any single one?
3. **Activation rate vs. post-activation engagement**: A feature could have high engagement among users who discover it and low discovery rates overall, making it look low-usage in aggregate while actually being high-value. How are you disaggregating activation rate from post-activation engagement before drawing conclusions about which features matter?
4. **The keystone species problem**: Ecologists distinguish between features present in an ecosystem and keystone species — the few whose removal causes cascading collapse. Which of your 15 features, if removed, would make users' remaining experience meaningfully less coherent, and does that structural dependency reveal what the app's true core actually is?

## Topic Cluster 06: The Passionate Minority and Niche Users

**Cluster focus**: How to weigh small but deeply attached user segments against the runway-bounded simplification mandate.

1. **Passionate minority calculation / Success to the Successful**: Small but passionate user bases for niche features represent a "Success to the Successful" dynamic — those users adapted their workflow around features the broader population never learned to use; removing one breaks their system without surfacing what made it work for them. Is their attachment evidence of a feature worth keeping, or evidence of a missing alternative in the market that this app accidentally filled? What quantitative threshold would distinguish a feature worth preserving from one that should be spun off or deprecated, accounting for team-capacity cost, advocacy and word-of-mouth value, and modeled churn among this vocal segment versus the retention gains simplification actually delivers?
2. **The dignity of niche users**: From the community's perspective, what does it feel like to have a product that was built for you suddenly decide you don't matter enough to retain, and how do we honor those users even when we can't serve them?

## Topic Cluster 07: Technical Debt, Team Capacity, and the Burnout Loop

**Cluster focus**: Treat the engineering reality and team psychology as load-bearing inputs to the cut, not afterthoughts.

1. **Technical debt feedback loop**: The team is burned out maintaining 15 half-built features, which reduces the quality of each feature, which reduces user engagement, which creates pressure to add more features to compensate — a reinforcing feedback loop. How do you break this compounding dynamic before the runway runs out?
2. **Integration debt and regression risk**: What does a dependency graph of the 15 features look like — specifically, which are load-bearing infrastructure that others depend on, does that constrain which cores are feasible to build around, and have we accounted for the risk that cutting interconnected modules introduces new instability into the features that remain such that the 8-month runway absorbs a destabilized core after aggressive removal?
3. **Capacity reallocation, the designer bottleneck, and team energy released by subtraction**: With 6 engineers spread across 15 half-built features, how much engineering time is currently consumed by maintenance versus new development, and how does that ratio shift on 3-4 features — what becomes possible in the 8-month runway that is not currently? With 1 designer supporting that team, what design debt is invisible in the feature count, and how much of the "overwhelming" UX is design execution rather than feature scope? What would it look like to frame the cutting process as a gift to the team — freeing people to do one thing excellently — and what rituals could make that transition feel like momentum rather than loss?
4. **Burnout, the ownership loop, and morale in reverse**: Engineers who built features they believe in resist removing them — not from stubbornness but because their craftsmanship is attached — and that resistance surfaces as slow execution, scope creep back into the simplified version, or quiet rebuilding through the back door. Burned-out teams also tend to favor cuts that reduce their own maintenance load over cuts that optimize for user value, producing different lists. And the harder psychological problem may be the opposite: the team no longer believes in any of the features and is at risk of under-committing to even the retained core. How do you make "killing your darlings" a shared act of craft rather than a management directive, what safeguard ensures the cutting process is user-driven rather than relief-driven, and how would we know whether we're executing a focused relaunch versus going through the motions?
5. **Burn the roadmap, keep the engineers**: What if the real resource being wasted isn't features or runway — it's the cognitive overhead of a team maintaining 15 half-built things in their heads simultaneously? What if you measured morale not in features shipped but in the number of abstractions an engineer has to hold in working memory? *The insight: feature bloat may be a technical debt problem that masquerades as a product strategy problem.*
6. **The engineer's grief**: Imagine a senior engineer who spent four months building the meal-planning feature — it's elegant code, users occasionally praise it, and now the PM is proposing it gets cut. What is the internal story that engineer tells themselves about this decision, and how does the way the team frames "cutting" versus "focusing" change whether that engineer becomes an advocate or a quiet saboteur of the simplified product?

## Topic Cluster 08: Choosing the Relaunch Path and Communicating the Change

**Cluster focus**: Decide between incremental, clean, or alternative paths — and design the communication that surrounds whichever path is chosen.

1. **Incremental simplification vs. clean relaunch**: When the team weighs incremental versus clean break, are they actually making a product decision or a team-psychology decision dressed up as one? A clean relaunch creates a before/after moment that gives the team permission to define a new success baseline; incremental pruning avoids a churn spike but keeps the team in maintenance mode for months. Given 8 months of runway, what are the measurable risks (user confusion, technical complexity, churn probability, team coordination overhead) on each path, what does the decision tree look like, and what is the decision clock?
2. **The incremental-vs-clean-break false dichotomy**: Have we pressure-tested a third path — a separate, new app targeting a narrower audience — that might preserve the existing user base while allowing an unconstrained fresh start? What assumption makes relaunch of the same product the default frame?
3. **Rebuilding trust, modeling churn, and the simplification announcement paradox**: The "relaunch" framing assumes existing users can be retained through communication alone, or alternately that the 180K base is largely unrecoverable and the simplified product is a new acquisition funnel — which is it? Users from the bloated version carry a mental model of the app as "chaotic," so what would it take to rebuild trust with lapsed users versus treating them as a lost cause? Telling existing users "we removed 11 features to make the app better" risks triggering loss aversion of users who weren't using those features but feel the product is shrinking — what framing shifts the story from subtraction to focus, does it hold up when a user discovers their specific feature is gone, and is there a way to design the removal process so that churn patterns become signal rather than just loss? For the 180K download base, which segments are at highest risk of churning when features are removed, what is the magnitude, and how does that factor into the runway calculation?
4. **Communication built on honesty / the existing user's grief**: For users who built habits around features that will be cut — even small, niche habits — what does that loss feel like from their perspective; the users who feel overwhelmed now trusted this product enough to download and give it a real try, so what would an honest, human message look like that honors that trust, explains the simplification, and invites them into what's coming — turning potential churn into anticipation?
5. **Deliberate amnesia / feature funeral**: What if, instead of stripping features out quietly, you announced a public "feature funeral" — a ceremony where users could eulogize the features being cut, vote on epitaphs, and bury them with fanfare? *The real insight: what if the act of removal is itself a product moment that turns loss into loyalty?*
6. **The three-body problem**: The constraints of user retention, team capacity, and runway aren't independent variables — they're coupled, like gravitational bodies whose trajectories only make sense relative to each other. If you optimized for each constraint in isolation, you'd get three different "right" answers; what configuration of feature cuts actually satisfies all three simultaneously rather than trading one off against the others?
7. **The urban renewal trap**: City planners learned the hard way that demolishing a "blighted" neighborhood and replacing it with something clean and rational often destroys the informal social infrastructure that made the place functional — the corner stores, the walking patterns, the informal gathering spots. What informal user workflows have formed around your app's navigation complexity that a clean relaunch might accidentally destroy, and how would you preserve the underlying need while rebuilding the structure?

## Topic Cluster 09: Inclusion, Voice, and Redefining Success

**Cluster focus**: Whose definition of value drives the cut, who has voice in deciding, and what success metrics should replace the current ones.

1. **Fitness for whom**: Social fitness apps often implicitly design for a narrow archetype — what assumptions about users' bodies, schedules, income (gym membership, equipment, food budget), and physical ability are baked into the "core" features being considered, and would the simplified product serve a narrower or broader population than the current one?
2. **Notification overload as a justice issue and the intrinsic-motivation question**: Users complained about "too many notifications" — but the people most harmed by aggressive notification design are often those with the least agency to manage it (users in demanding jobs, caregivers, people without notification literacy); how does the simplified product redesign its relationship with users' attention, not just reduce feature count? And if the team uses push notifications to re-engage users during the relaunch period, there is real danger that engagement becomes externally triggered rather than intrinsically motivated — making retention numbers look better while underlying habit-formation capacity stays weak. How do you distinguish these two dynamics in your metrics?
3. **Who gets to define core value, and who has voice in the cut**: Whose definition of value drives the determination of "true core" — the team's vision, the most vocal users, the data from the highest-engagement cohort, or something that accounts for users who haven't yet been able to engage deeply because the current app was too confusing to reach them? Is there an opportunity to involve users in deciding what to cut, and if so, which users should have voice given that the loudest are rarely the most representative?
4. **The silent majority, barriers to entry, and unarticulated loyalty**: Who are the 98% who downloaded and disappeared — what were they hoping for in those first sessions, and at what moment did they encounter "overwhelming" (the navigation, the onboarding, the first push notification, or the realization they didn't know which feature to open first)? Conversely, what do the usage logs actually show about which features even half-built ones people return to repeatedly — and are there features users have never explicitly praised that they'd only notice if they disappeared, because they've built workarounds so seamlessly the feature has become invisible infrastructure in their routine?
5. **Redefining success before cutting**: What if the team defined the target success metrics for the simplified product before deciding which 3-4 features to keep — how would working backward from "daily active users opening the app with intent" change which features survive? Current metrics (DAU/MAU at 2%, session length at 47 seconds) may be measuring the wrong things; what metrics would more accurately capture whether the focused app is delivering its core value, which will be directly comparable to current baselines and which will require recalibration, and how do you design the measurement framework so the relaunch does not produce a false-positive signal that masks underlying problems? What would retention look like if the product launched with only the single feature users most frequently navigated to, why do we assume removing features will improve retention rather than that the 8% rate is driven by the app simply not solving the core problem well enough, and what is the single most useful question the team could answer this week using data already in hand to gain genuine conviction about which feature deserves to survive?
6. **The abandonment moment**: Imagine Marcus, a casual runner who downloaded the app six months ago — he opened it three times, then stopped. Walk through his experience from the moment he first launched it to the moment he decided never to return. What was the exact scene where the app lost him, and what would have had to be different for him to still be opening it today?
