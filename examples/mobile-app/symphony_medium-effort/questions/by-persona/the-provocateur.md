# Brainstorming Questions from The Provocateur: Feature-Bloated Mobile App MVP Reset

---

## The Deliberate Destruction Gambit

1. **Designing for deletion**: What if the team's goal were to make users permanently delete the app within 30 days — what would have to be ruthlessly, brilliantly present in those 30 days to justify the whole journey, and what does that reveal about what the product actually owes its users right now? *The real insight: if the app had to front-load all its value into a single, time-bounded encounter, which features would survive and which only exist to pad the illusion of depth?*

2. **The obituary test**: Imagine this app shuts down next month and a journalist writes its obituary — not a takedown, but a genuinely affectionate eulogy for the one thing it did well that nobody else quite managed. What is that one thing, and why has it been buried under 14 other things that also died? *The insight: the feature worth keeping is probably the one that would be mourned, not the one with the highest usage count.*

---

## Inverting the Simplification Logic

3. **Adding as removal**: What if stripping features is exactly the wrong move — what if the real problem is that the app has 15 half-features when it needs exactly one feature executed so completely, so obsessively, so impossibly well that users feel the absence of everything else as a relief rather than a loss? *The insight: "simplification" might be misdirection — the goal might be depth, not reduction, and depth looks like addition in the right dimension.*

4. **The competitor's nightmare scenario**: Instead of asking which features to keep, what if you asked: which single capability, if executed at a world-class level, would make a well-funded competitor genuinely afraid to enter this space? Work backward from that fear — does the current feature list contain it, or has it been diluted into invisibility? *The insight: competitive moats are rarely built on breadth; they require an almost irrational concentration of effort on one thing.*

---

## The Wrong Problem Provocation

5. **Misidentifying the patient**: What if the 8% 30-day retention isn't a product problem at all — what if it's a distribution problem, and the app is being installed by completely the wrong people, which means every decision made from this data is a solution to a problem the target user doesn't have? *The insight: before cutting features, it's worth asking whether the data is contaminated by mismatched users — fixing retention for the wrong audience might destroy retention for the right one.*

---

## Embracing the Failure State

6. **The burn-it-down reframe**: What if the burned-out team is actually the most important data point in this entire situation — more important than DAU/MAU ratio, more important than user feedback — because a team that has lost the ability to care about the product cannot execute a relaunch no matter how elegant the feature list? What would it mean to redesign the product *around* what the team can sustainably love building? *The insight: feature selection driven by team energy and passion often produces better products than feature selection driven by analytics, because execution quality is a function of care.*

---

## Ritualistic Friction as Feature Strategy

7. **Making it harder on purpose**: What if the 47-second average session length isn't a symptom of a broken product but the correct session length for the one thing this app should actually do — and the real failure is that the team has been designing for longer sessions when the right experience is fast, sharp, and complete? What would a product optimized for 45-second mastery look like, and which existing feature is closest to that? *The insight: friction assumptions cut both ways — the app may have been designed for engagement when it should have been designed for instant completion, and those require completely opposite architectures.*
