# Brainstorming Questions from The Systems Thinker: Feature-Bloated Mobile App MVP Reset

---

## The Bloat Spiral: How Complexity Compounds

1. **The complexity trap**: The app currently has 15 features, none executed well — but how did it get there? What self-amplifying pattern allowed each new feature to feel justified at the time, and how does that same pattern risk repeating itself after the reset if it isn't named and interrupted?

2. **Feature debt as a gravity well**: Each of the 15 features likely has its own micro-community of users, code dependencies, and team muscle memory. When you remove a feature, what secondary effects ripple outward — through the codebase, through team identity, through the remaining features that implicitly relied on the removed ones? Where are the hidden load-bearing walls?

3. **The maintenance burden cycle**: A burned-out team maintaining 15 half-built features produces lower quality on each, which drives worse retention, which triggers pressure to add new features to compensate, which burns the team out further. At what point in that cycle does the team currently sit, and which intervention breaks it — cutting features, or first restoring team capacity?

---

## Feature Selection: Signal vs. Noise in Usage Data

4. **The engagement illusion**: 47-second average session length and 2% DAU/MAU suggest users open the app but find nothing worth staying for. Before interpreting any feature-level usage data, how do you distinguish "this feature has low usage because it's not valuable" from "this feature has low usage because it's buried three navigation levels deep and users never find it"? What would a fair measurement look like?

5. **The passionate minority problem**: Small but devoted user bases for niche features create a structural trap: their vocal advocacy inflates the perceived value of the feature beyond what usage data supports, while their predicted churn from removal inflates the perceived cost of cutting it. How do you design a decision process that hears passionate minorities without letting them veto the reset?

6. **Leading vs. lagging indicators**: Retention and session length are lagging indicators — they reflect what has already happened. What leading indicators (early behavioral signals in the first session, patterns among the 8% who do retain) would reveal which features are actually creating value before you commit to keeping them?

---

## Simplification Strategy: Incremental vs. Clean Break

7. **The partial-simplification trap**: Incremental feature removal carries a specific failure mode: you cut enough to alienate existing users without cutting enough to feel meaningfully simpler to new users. The app ends up worse than either version. What's the minimum viable simplification that creates a perceptibly different experience — and is it actually achievable through iteration, or does it require a clean break?

8. **The technical debt untangling**: Deeply integrated features don't remove cleanly — removing one can destabilize others in non-obvious ways, and the team may not know where those connections are until they start pulling. How do you map the coupling between features before committing to a removal sequence, and what does that investigation reveal about which features are truly independent versus load-bearing?

9. **Momentum after the cut**: Once the simplified product ships, what reinforcing dynamic determines whether it attracts a new user base fast enough to compensate for churned users? What's the rate-limiting step in that cycle — product quality, discoverability, word-of-mouth, or something else?

---

## Team Dynamics and the Psychology of Cutting

10. **Killing your darlings as a team sport**: Every feature was someone's priority at some point. When you cut 11 of 15 features, you're implicitly telling engineers and the PM that significant past work was wasted. How does the way you frame the cut — as strategic clarity versus failure acknowledgment — affect team morale and their commitment to executing the reset well?

11. **The sunk cost stabilizer**: Teams often resist cutting features not because the features are good but because the investment in them feels painful to abandon. This resistance is a balancing force that slows simplification — but it also surfaces genuine institutional knowledge about why features were built. How do you use that resistance as information rather than just an obstacle to overcome?

---

## Communication and the Existing User Ecosystem

12. **The churn anticipation loop**: Communicating the simplification to existing users before it ships allows you to manage expectations — but it also risks triggering preemptive churn from users who hear "we're removing features" before they can experience "this is actually better." What's the optimal sequence and timing of communication to minimize anticipatory abandonment while still honoring transparency?

13. **Redefining success after the reset**: The current metrics — 8% 30-day retention, 2% DAU/MAU — are products of a bloated, confusing product. After the reset, those same metrics will be measured against a fundamentally different user experience. What's the risk of using pre-reset metrics as the benchmark for the simplified product, and how do you set new success criteria that reflect what the focused product is actually trying to accomplish — without giving yourself cover to rationalize underperformance?
