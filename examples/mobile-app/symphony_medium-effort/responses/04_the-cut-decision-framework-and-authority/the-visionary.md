# The Cut Decision — Framework and Authority - The Visionary

---

## The Feature-Value Scoring Model

* **Paradigm: Inverse Weighting — Rarity as Power.** Traditional scoring treats frequency as proof of value. Flip it: what if the features users *couldn't live without* are precisely those they use least? A single, weekly "moment of truth" interaction that transforms behavior might outweigh daily habit-trackers that soothe anxiety. Score not by usage, but by the irreplaceability of the emotional or functional outcome it enables.

* **Emergence over Combination.** Weighted rubrics fragment meaning into dimensions that kill authenticity. Instead, ask: *if these 3-4 features lived together as an organism, what would they collectively feel like?* The real scoring model is narrative coherence—do they form an integrated philosophy about fitness, or remain scattered impulses? The surviving features should feel like they were designed together.

* **The Passionate Minority as Proxy for Discovery.** Those small, intense user cohorts aren't noise to be overruled—they're early signals of an entirely different use case your app doesn't yet know it's solving. Rather than downweight them, treat them as telescopes pointing toward a hidden market. Their devotion reveals features that are solving a real problem your metrics just can't see.

* **Execution as the Fundamental Unit.** "Executed well" has no standard until you define *which problem the user came to solve and whether we solved it completely.* A feature executed brilliantly is one where every pixel, every state, every notification is architected toward a single user outcome. Most of your 15 features don't meet this bar. Measure not depth of feature, but depth of user transformation.

## Decision Authority and the Data-vs-Interview Tiebreak

* **Authority Through Conviction, Not Position.** The 1 PM doesn't earn decision power by title. Decision authority emerges from whoever can hold the contradiction longest—whoever can honor both the retention data *and* the user who says "this saved my life." Distribute authority through a small council that rotates defending each position and must agree on a non-negotiable principle before cutting anything.

* **Three Methods, One Experiment.** Data says one thing, interviews say another—that's not a problem, it's a *revelation of scope*. Run a single, ruthlessly honest two-week experiment: ask your most-retained users directly, "If we shipped you only one fitness feature tomorrow, which would you pay for?" Don't ask what they use; ask what would hurt to lose. That becomes your arbiter.

* **Co-Design as Redefinition.** Stop gathering data *from* users. Sit beside three genuine users for a full week while they use the app. Watch them founder. Watch what they reach for when they're frustrated. Watch what makes them smile. The definition of "core value" isn't discovered through surveys—it emerges through witnessing.

* **Trust the Team's Product Intuition Over Your Own Instinct.** Those 6 engineers have spent months in the code; the designer knows where friction lives. A leadership decision imposed from above kills momentum. Instead, create a deliberative ritual where the team builds its own consensus on which features, if removed, would most fundamentally betray what they believe the app should be. Their conviction becomes your authority.

## The Smallest Proof of Concept

* **The Two-Week Silence Experiment.** Turn off notifications for half your user base and measure what *breaks*. If removing notifications from a "core" feature causes zero behavioral change in two weeks, you've just proven it's not core. The smallest experiment isn't building anything—it's removing something and watching what users do when they don't get reminded to care.

* **One Conversation, Honest and Long.** Pick your single most loyal user and ask them: "Pretend I'm killing the app in 30 days. Tell me which one thing you'll actually miss." Listen for the *tone* of their answer, not the feature name. Record it. Transcribe it. That 20-minute conversation reveals more about true value than months of analytics aggregation.

* **The Minimal Viable Cut.** Don't experiment with features—experiment with removal. Disable your bottom-performing feature for 7 days across 20% of your user base and measure churn. If retention stays flat, you've got your answer. If it drops, you've learned something precious: that feature has invisible value. Repeat until you've stress-tested every feature against actual user loss.

* **The Prototype Relaunch.** Build a skeletal version with only 4 features in a completely stripped interface. Test it with 50 users recruited specifically to say "no" if it doesn't solve their problem. Speed matters less than truth-telling here. Two weeks of brutal feedback beats months of polish on the wrong problem.

## The Restaurant Tasting-Menu Trap

* **Constraint as Courage Signal.** What if simplicity weren't a compromise, but a statement—a *declaration* that you've thought so deeply about this one thing that you've eliminated everything else? Users read this as confidence. A fitness app that *only* does one thing, executed with obsessive care, feels intentional rather than abandoned. The market will pay premium attention to that kind of clarity.

* **The Michelin Principle: One Dish, Every Day.** Don't chase "well-executed versions" of four features. Instead, ask: what single daily interaction could become *legendary*—so perfectly calibrated to human need that users would open the app for nothing else? Build that one thing until it feels inevitable. Everything else either supports that moment or dies.

* **Operational Simplicity as Competitive Moat.** That Michelin restaurant with 8 dishes doesn't just serve better food—it moves faster, trains faster, innovates faster. Your 6 engineers maintaining 4 obsessive features will ship updates three times faster than they maintain 15. Speed becomes a feature itself; users feel the momentum, the freshness, the care.

* **The Emotional Scarcity Play.** When the app does less, each feature you *do* offer feels precious. Users unconsciously value scarcity. Four features that feel intentional trigger more delight than 15 features that feel accidental. This isn't marketing—it's a fundamental shift in how users perceive the product's intention.

## Designing for Deletion

* **Invert the Goal.** What if your real metric for the first 30 days weren't retention, but *earned permission to continue existing?* Users who delete after 30 days didn't fail—you did. They gave you a month to prove the app deserved space on their phone. What would have to happen in those 30 days for deletion to feel like abandoning something sacred rather than removing clutter?

* **The Irreversible Moment.** Design for one interaction so transformative that deleting the app would feel like erasing a promise you made to yourself. Not another notification. Not another streak counter. But something like: *a moment where you realized something true about your body or capacity that you didn't know before*. If all 15 features don't contribute to that moment, they're ornament.

* **The Unsent Message Test.** Imagine every user has an unsent message in the app they'll never send—something true they wanted to capture. Which feature becomes the repository for that truth? Your app survives deletion because it's the only place users feel safe holding their real intentions. Everything else is infrastructure to protect that one sacred space.

* **Deletion as Feedback to the Designer.** If users delete, it's because the app promised something human and delivered something mechanical. The 30-day period isn't about proving retention—it's about discovering which feature failed to feel like it was *made for you*. That failure is your real data; redesign the entire experience around preventing it.

## The One Thing That Changes Everything

* **A Moment of Genuine Witnessing.** What if the single daily interaction weren't a *feature* but a *conversation*—a moment where the app says something true about who you're becoming, based on what you've actually been doing? Not a streak counter. Not a calorie log. But something like: *"You moved your body today when your schedule said you couldn't. That's who you're becoming."* That moment, once daily, rewires identity.

* **The Anticipation Collapse.** Imagine if the app could genuinely predict—not through algorithms, but through deep user modeling—the *exact moment* when you're most likely to skip your workout, and reached you three minutes before with something that makes you *want* to move. Not because you're motivated, but because the app anticipated your resistance and showed you something true about what happens when you choose anyway.

* **A New Category: The Behavior Mirror.** Forget "fitness app." What if you built something that shows users who they're becoming through movement—not data, but narrative? *"Over the last month, you've chosen to move your body on days when stress was highest. You're the kind of person who runs toward difficulty."* That single, truthful interaction per day creates an entirely new relationship between person and body.

* **The Permission Giver.** What if the one interaction was simply permission—*you don't have to be perfect*—delivered at the moment users feel most like quitting? Not motivational speak. Just honest acknowledgment: *"Even skipped workouts count. You're still building."* That single interaction, if delivered with radical honesty, becomes why users open the app daily. It becomes the category-defining feature.
