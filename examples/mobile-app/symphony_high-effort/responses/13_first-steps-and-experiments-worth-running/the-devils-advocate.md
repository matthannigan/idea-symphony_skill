# First Steps and Experiments Worth Running - The Devil's Advocate

---

## The Experiment Worth Running This Month

* **Beware the "retention trap" of early adopters.** Your most engaged users right now are the 2% who stuck with the chaos—they may have wildly different needs than the 98% who left. Running a focused experiment on users who already tolerate complexity risks validating the wrong core. Consider: can you run the experiment on *lapsed* users via re-engagement, or recruit fresh users entirely? Otherwise you're optimizing for a biased segment.

* **Your 30-day timeline may disguise flawed validation.** Short experiments reward novelty excitement, not genuine habit formation. An experiment that shows 20% of testers "love the simplified version" over a month tells you nothing about 90-day retention or whether the ritual sticks. Build in a longer-term follow-up checkpoint and define what "successful result" actually means—engagement metrics? Retention curve? User quotes about changed behavior?

* **Beware cannibalizing your own data.** If the experiment lives inside the cluttered current app (a new tab, a feature flag), users will still be distracted by the 14 other things competing for their attention. A true test requires isolation—either a separate app, a web-only version, or a walled-off fork. Otherwise you cannot tell if the core vision failed or if the surrounding noise killed it.

* **The team's existing skills may box the experiment.** Your engineers built 15 features in parallel; they may lack deep UX research or metric-rigor skills. Have you budgeted time to *interpret* the experiment results, or are you assuming the data will be obvious? False clarity kills as many products as ambiguity.

## The Product Worth Celebrating In 18 Months—And The Daily Ritual It Earns

* **Beware the "product-market fit fantasy."** Strong retention and evangelist users sound great, but at what scale? If you achieve 40% 30-day retention but only 5,000 monthly active users, you've won admiration from a niche—not found product-market fit. Define the target scale alongside the ritual. Is this a lifestyle app for 500K+ users or a devoted micro-community? The ritual that works for 10,000 passionate users may not scale to a venture-backed business model.

* **The daily ritual you're imagining may not survive contact with reality.** Users often *want* to do the thing but don't, due to friction, forgetfulness, or competing priorities. In 18 months, the risk isn't that the app fails to attract users—it's that the habit doesn't stick even for people who genuinely like it. Have you planned for the first-time habit formation barrier, or assumed that a well-designed product is enough?

* **Beware the "team energy" metric as a proxy for success.** A burned-out team building a niche product is still unsustainable long-term. Morale improves when the team feels *heard* and *focused*; focus on a simplified core may energize them, but it doesn't guarantee the product will generate revenue, grow, or sustain the business. Have you separated team satisfaction from actual business viability?

* **The user you're imagining may not match your data.** The 12-month-engaged user who opens the app weekly exists in your fantasy—your current data says 8% stick around 30 days. Building a celebration story around that ideal user is motivating but risky if it isn't grounded in early experimental evidence. Start with the 18-month vision but validate the ritual assumption within the first 60 days.

## Subtraction As A New Category

* **Beware the "meme-ability" trap of contrarian positioning.** "We do less so you do more" sounds compellingly different in a pitch deck, but user behavior research shows people rarely choose products for what they refuse to do—they choose for what they *enable*. The category definition may be clever marketing that resonates with early adopters and investors, but it could alienate mainstream users who care only about results. Test whether users *actually care* about the philosophy or whether they just care that the app works.

* **Subtraction as a category requires the subtraction to be visible and felt.** If users don't *notice* what you removed (because the core features work so well they don't miss the rest), then the category differentiation is invisible. Conversely, if users constantly hit the edge of what's missing, the subtraction feels like a limitation, not a virtue. The success of this category depends on nailing the exact point where restraint becomes a feature—that's hard to get right on the first try.

* **Refusal as value proposition is vulnerable to feature creep.** Once the app gains traction, stakeholders will pressure you to "just add one more thing" to unlock new user segments or revenue streams. Your identity becomes a liability when it blocks business opportunity. Have you thought through how you'll defend the refusal over time, or will the category dissolve the moment growth slows and pressure mounts?

* **This positioning requires deep understanding of what you're *not*—and why.** Users need to understand your philosophy, not just your feature list. That requires marketing, onboarding, and brand consistency. Can your small team support that level of narrative work alongside product development, or does clever positioning become an unfunded promise? The category exists only if you can afford to tell the story.
