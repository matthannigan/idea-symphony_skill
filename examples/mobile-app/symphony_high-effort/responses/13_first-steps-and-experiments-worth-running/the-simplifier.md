# First Steps and Experiments Worth Running - The Simplifier

---

## The Experiment Worth Running This Month

* **Single-Feature Test: One Workout, One Tracking Method.** Here's the radical version: stop testing the app at all. Instead, pick the single workout type your best users actually do — say, running — build a tracking screen that does *only* that, remove every other feature from the navigation entirely, and give it to 100 power users. Watch what they ignore, what they add back, what they forget. If 60% open it more than three times in two weeks, you've found something. This works because you're not testing the app; you're testing user habit formation with zero noise.

* **Onboarding Deletion Experiment.** What if you cut the entire onboarding flow — the goal-setting wizard, the preference questions, the tutorial overlay — and shipped a version that just asks "How many miles did you run today?" on the first screen? Measure whether new users churn *more* or *less* without the scaffolding. My guess: you'll discover that half your onboarding exists to fix problems you created with feature overload, not real user needs.

* **The "One Tab" Relaunch.** Build a version of the app with a single bottom tab: "Your Stats." Remove navigation entirely. Everything else (logging a workout, viewing history, settings) branches from there. Test it for two weeks with 200 active users. Count how many complain about missing features versus how many report clarity for the first time. You'll either confirm the core is strong enough to stand alone, or you'll find out which "hidden feature" people actually need visible.

* **Notification Silence Test.** Don't build anything new. Just disable all notifications (friend challenges, streak reminders, motivational messages) for a subset of users and measure churn. If retention improves, your feature bloat is actively making the product worse, and the right first step is removal, not iteration.

* **Lightweight Interview Sprint.** Skip the coded experiment. Spend a week calling 15 of your 8% retained users and asking: "What's the one thing you actually use the app for?" and "What feature confuses you that you've never touched?" Document ruthlessly. The patterns will be clearer than any analytics dashboard. Then build a 30-day test based on what you *actually heard*, not what the PM suspects.

## The Product Worth Celebrating in 18 Months

* **The App That Does One Thing Obsessively Well.** Eighteen months from now, the app is the best running tracker in the app store — full stop. Not for fitness generally, for running specifically. Users open it every morning before their run, log their mileage and pace in 15 seconds, and scroll back a year of consistent streaks. The ritual is: "I ran X miles yesterday, I'm on day 127 of my streak, am I running longer or faster?" The magic is that users deleted it, tried six competitors, and came back because nothing else was this simple. The team celebrates because for the first time, they own something instead of maintaining 15 half-built things.

* **The Social App That's Actually Social.** Imagine a simplified product: you log your workout, it shows up on a feed your friends can see, and you can comment or cheer. That's it. No leaderboards, no challenges, no marketplace — just the authentic thing that made you download a fitness app in the first place: seeing your friend also ran today. Eighteen months in, users evangelize it because it's the only fitness app that doesn't feel like a to-do list. Users send it to friends, not out of obligation, but because it genuinely connects people. The ritual: "Did Sarah get her run in today? Let me cheer her on."

* **The Accountability Tool for People Who Know What They Want.** The app survives because it stops trying to persuade. Users open it to record what they chose to do — not to be convinced, motivated, or tracked into compliance. The product is honest: we count your workouts, we show your streaks, we let you see patterns. That's all. Users stay because accountability doesn't require manipulation. Eighteen months from now, the team is working with a team of 8, not burned out, because maintaining clarity is less work than managing chaos.

* **The App That Became a Community Ritual.** What if the simplified core isn't a feature at all but a *weekly moment*? Every Sunday at 10 AM, a user opens the app, logs their week of workouts, and sees a one-number dashboard: "You completed 4 of 7 days. Nice." Then they see a feed of what their workout group did. That ritual — one moment a week that matters — becomes the thing that keeps people engaged. Users do workouts during the week, then come to the app to report and connect. The retention curve flattens because the app isn't demanding; it's just *there* when you need it.

## Subtraction as a New Category

* **"No Gamification" as the Brand.** What if the relaunch was framed as a radical refusal? "We don't do streaks. We don't do badges. We don't do leaderboards." You're not simplifying a fitness app; you're *inventing* the anti-app fitness app. Users who hate the manipulation of other products migrate to you *because of what you don't do*. The value proposition becomes: "The fitness app that doesn't try to trick you." The competitive edge isn't a feature; it's an absence. This is powerful because it's impossible for a feature-bloated competitor to copy — their business model requires the manipulation you're rejecting.

* **"No Connectivity" Until You Earn It.** Redefine the category around *earned* social. New users get only the core workout logging — fully local, no sync, no network. If they log for 30 days straight, *then* the app unlocks the ability to share with friends. The refusal to be social from day one becomes the point. Users appreciate simplicity before connection. This category doesn't exist: "The fitness app that stays personal until you're ready." Competitors can't copy it without breaking their own model.

* **"No Notifications, Ever" as a Selling Point.** Some apps refuse ads. Yours refuses *to interrupt*. Zero notifications. Zero push messages. Users open the app when they choose. Market this explicitly: "The only fitness app that respects your attention." In a world of notification spam, this absence becomes the most powerful feature you can offer. Users gravitate to you not because you do something special, but because you've stopped doing something everyone else does wrong.

* **The App That Only Works Offline.** What if your entire pitch is: "Your fitness data lives on your phone, not in the cloud"? No syncing. No accounts. No data harvest. Users own their data. The category you're inventing is *privacy-first fitness*. In an era of data concerns, this absence — no tracking of user behavior, no third-party integrations — becomes the defensible moat. You're not a fitness app; you're a fitness app for people who believe their health data is theirs.

* **"Fewer Users, Better Experience" as the Value Prop.** Reframe entirely: the app serves *dedicated* users, not casual ones. Simplify not for mass market but for people who actually care about running. Market it as "the running app for runners, not for people trying to become runners." This refusal to serve everyone serves the people you keep. Eighteen months from now, you have 50K engaged users instead of 180K abandoned ones. The category is *niche commitment* — and niche products often outcompete mass products because they serve one thing obsessively. Your simplification becomes *specialization*, and specialization is defensible.
