# First Steps and Experiments Worth Running — The Pragmatist

---

## The Experiment Worth Running This Month

* **Data-driven triage.** Pull 30-day retention and session flow data for each feature. Identify which 2-3 features drive sustained engagement vs. novelty-seeking. Run this analysis this week — it costs nothing and reveals which "darlings" actually earn user loyalty. Success: consensus on which features are real retention drivers vs. noise.

* **One-core beta cohort.** Pick your single strongest feature hypothesis (say, workout tracking) and create a parallel v2 onboarding that leads users straight there, hiding all others behind a feature gate. Push it to 500 power users. Success: cohort shows 2-3x retention lift over control group, and users report clarity.

* **Three-day sprint with focused stakeholder.** Run a 72-hour design sprint: one engineer, your designer, and your PM build a ruthlessly scoped "coach view" for one workout feature only. Deploy as a prototype. Success: internal team agrees this focused version is compelling and ship-ready; even a small cohort finds it meaningful.

* **Simplicity messaging test.** Create three landing page variants: one emphasizes the full feature count, another emphasizes "the workout tracker built for focused athletes," a third positions it as "anti-bloat." Drive 100 high-intent users to each. Success: at least one variant shows 25%+ higher signup-to-first-session rate than today's.

* **Retention repair on core loop.** Audit the top feature's onboarding and core user flow for friction. Remove three confusing UI patterns and re-launch to existing users with explicit messaging: "We've simplified. Try it now." Success: post-launch session length increases, users report less overwhelm.

## The Product Worth Celebrating in 18 Months

* **Depth over breadth.** The celebrated product focuses ruthlessly on one core loop — say, transforming workout data into actionable insights with a social accountability layer. Users open it 4+ times weekly to log sessions and see progress against their goals. The team says: "We learned that depth and mastery beat feature count. Every pixel serves the workout loop." Success metric: 30-day retention above 35%, DAU/MAU above 18%, session length above 3 minutes.

* **The ritual redefined.** Eighteen months in, the daily ritual is a user spending 7 minutes: log workout, see how they've progressed vs. last week, post one win to their accountability group, get one personalized insight. The team celebrates: "We killed the paralysis of choice. Users know exactly why they open the app." Launch 12-month retention hits 25%+ of cohort.

* **The skeptic's conversion.** The user who downloads today and re-engages after 12 months is someone who initially bounced, but returned because: (1) push notifications are rare and earned, (2) the core loop is genuinely useful (not addictive junk), (3) their friends are also using it for accountability. When recommending, she says: "It's the only fitness app I actually need. Everything else is noise."

* **Burnout flipped to ownership.** The team celebrates shipping—not maintaining. Six months in, the codebase has 60% fewer feature branches, no zombie code, and clear product strategy. Engineers report: "For the first time, our work feels focused. We're shipping incrementally, not firefighting." Retention gains signal product-market fit, morale recovers.

## Subtraction as a New Category

* **Anti-feature positioning.** Rather than "stripped-down fitness app," position it as "The No-Nonsense Workout Journal." Marketing emphasizes what it refuses: no social pressure, no infinite scrolling, no upsell notifications, no algorithm gaming your attention. The value prop: "Fitness tracking for people who hate fitness apps." Success: messaging resonates with users fatigued by bloat-ware.

* **Constraint as design principle.** Market the simplicity itself—not as a compromise, but as the product's superpower. "We deleted 12 features so you could focus on 1." Create a "roadmap of what we won't build" to reinforce commitment. Users appreciate the clarity; they know what they're getting and what they're not. Success: attracts users explicitly looking for restraint.

* **Minimum viable ritual.** Position the category as "apps that respect your attention." Pitch specifically to users who deleted five fitness apps because they were overwhelming. Emphasize: one login, one purpose, one notification per day (only if relevant). The appeal is permission to ignore the app on non-workout days. Success: high-intent user acquisition, lower churn from the guilt-driven users.

* **Transparency as trust builder.** Publish explicitly which features you considered and rejected, and why. Let users understand the philosophy: "We could add meal planning, but we won't. Here's why." This candor builds trust with users who've been burned by feature creep. Success: word-of-mouth from users who feel heard by the company's discipline.
