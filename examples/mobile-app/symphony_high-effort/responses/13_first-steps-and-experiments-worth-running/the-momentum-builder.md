# First Steps and Experiments Worth Running - The Momentum Builder

---

## The Experiment Worth Running This Month

* **The Seven-Day Core Ritual Test.** Start by identifying your single most engaged user cohort (likely the core 5% who have 30+ DAU sessions). Create a locked-down version with just one feature and measure daily opens, session length, and whether they return. Success isn't conversion rates — it's whether usage velocity increases. If your core fitness-tracker users jump from 47-second sessions to 3+ minutes with fewer features, you've found your seed. From there, you could extend the test to add one complementary feature and watch retention shift.

* **The Notification-As-Signal Experiment.** The "too many notifications" feedback is a data point about overwhelm, not the root problem. Redesign push strategy to send only one daily message — make it contextual, make it earned, make it matter. Track whether this alone improves DAU/MAU. If users come back for one perfect notification rather than eleven mediocre ones, you've proven that focus beats breadth. And once you've proven that principle, you can systematically rebuild feature set around that same ruthlessness.

* **The Competitive Substitution Test.** Pick your three best features. For 20% of your install base, disable access to everything else for two weeks and run a survival test. Ask them weekly: "If this app only had X, would you still open it?" The answers will be brutally honest and fast. The momentum comes from proving a feature is *essential*, not just *useful*. Start small risk, harvest signal hard.

* **The Welcome Funnel Reset.** Your 180K downloads with 8% retention suggests onboarding is broken. Build a new, one-feature-only welcome flow — get users to their first success (one completed workout logged, one streak started) in under 90 seconds. Success is when onboarding-to-DAU ratio jumps from 8% to 25%+ with the simplified flow. Once you prove users *want* to stick around when you don't overwhelm them, everything else follows.

## The Product Worth Celebrating in 18 Months

* **The Daily Ritual That Became Inevitable.** Imagine users opening the app at 6 AM, not because they have to, but because it's become part of their morning — the way they check the weather or their email. The 18-month celebration would name a single, irreplaceable ritual: maybe it's a streak-based workout check-in that became their accountability anchor, or a community challenge that transformed a solo activity into belonging. Users would say, "I don't think about it anymore — I just do it." The product would be so focused that its value is *invisible*. And here's what the team would celebrate: they shipped less, not more. They'd say, "We let go of 12 features we were proud of, and discovered that nobody actually wanted them anyway."

* **The Traction Moment That Built Momentum.** At the 18-month mark, the team is celebrating a metric inflection: DAU/MAU crosses 40-50%, daily session length stabilizes at 8-12 minutes, and word-of-mouth signup rate is now 30%+ of new installs. What changed? The team discovered that their "core" wasn't a feature — it was a transformation. Users open the app because it makes them *feel* a particular way (capable, connected, progressing). Everything in the product serves that feeling. The team would describe an 18-month journey where they stopped building features and started building identity. Users don't use the app; they *belong* to it.

* **The Team's Recovery as a Product Insight.** The team is celebrating, but not what they shipped — it's how they work now. Six months into this, morale cratered (burning features is hard), but around month 9-10, it flipped. Why? Because they started shipping faster, collecting real signal, and iterating on something users actually loved. By month 18, they're excited again. The celebration includes a shared understanding: simplicity is a *decision*, not a failure state. The product that users evangelize is also the one the team ships, supports, and improves with genuine joy. That momentum is the real victory.

* **The Unexpected Category.** The team imagined a workout app. What they built was actually something else: a *commitment device*. Users don't open it for information or coaching — they open it because it holds them accountable to themselves. The 18-month celebration acknowledges that the original product positioning was wrong, but the focus was right. By ruthlessly eliminating features that didn't serve accountability, they stumbled into a category that already has millions of users hungry for a solution. The momentum shifted from "let's make fitness features" to "let's be the most trusted place for people to keep promises to themselves."

## Subtraction as a New Category

* **The Refusal as Your Moat.** What if the relaunch story wasn't "we simplified" but "we said no"? Position it as: *The fitness app that refuses to be a social network. The app that refuses to sell you supplements. The app that refuses to be addictive.* Users are exhausted by apps that maximize engagement through notifications, infinite scroll, and FOMO. Here's the seed: build a category defined by discipline, not distraction. "Clarity fitness" — an app that respects your time and attention as sacred. From that foundation, you could extend into premium positioning: *You don't pay for more features. You pay for fewer notifications. You pay for an ad-free, distraction-free, judgment-free space.* And if that worked, you could build a community of users who chose this because they're tired of the other way.

* **The Design Constraint as Competitive Advantage.** What if you announced: "We will never have more than 5 core sections. We will never send more than 3 notifications per week. We will never show ads or sponsored content. We will never track you for marketing." Frame these as *features*, not limitations. Users would come *because* you're constrained, not *despite* it. The momentum builds from a bold public commitment. Competitors with 15 features look scattered; you look intentional. And if that positioning worked, you could expand by adding new *refusals* — "We don't collect behavioral data for resale" or "We don't manipulate psychology to increase time-in-app." Constraints become your identity.

* **The Anti-Feature Liberation.** Most apps fear simplicity — they see it as a business risk. What if you turned that into fearlessness? "We removed the marketplace because it wasn't serving users." "We killed the social feed because feeds are where attention dies." "We discontinued coaching because push notifications were ruining the experience." After each subtraction, collect testimonials from users *thanking you* for the removal. Build a library of "what we cut and why." Users aren't looking for more; they're looking for someone to protect them from more. Your differentiation becomes: *We are the app that listens to what you don't want.*
