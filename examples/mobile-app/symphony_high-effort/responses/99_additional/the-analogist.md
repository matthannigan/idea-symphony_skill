# Feature-Bloated Mobile App MVP Reset — The Analogist

---

## The Abandonment Scene

* **Retail store overwhelm.** This mirrors the moment a customer walks into a department store looking for a specific shirt, encounters 47 departments, unclear signage, and no clear path forward—they spin once, feel the cognitive friction of choice paralysis, and leave. The abandonment happens not because the shirt doesn't exist, but because finding it costs more mental energy than it's worth. For your app, that moment is likely when a returning user opens the interface, sees six tabs and a hamburger menu, and realizes they need to *navigate* to get value rather than *use* it immediately. The real diagnostic is: which single feature did they actually want on that third session, and where did they look for it first? If they looked in the wrong place, the IA defeated the feature itself.

* **Onboarding cliff in language learning.** The Duolingo research team found that apps lose users not at "hard lessons" but at the moment between download and first meaningful win—when the app shifts from novelty to *work.* Your social fitness app likely triggers this at session two: the first session is exploratory excitement, but when users return with *intent* (wanting to log a workout, track progress, join a challenge), they hit the friction of "where do I actually do this?" The abandonment decision happens when the app ceases to be a tool and becomes an obstacle to the user's own goal.

* **The subway map redesign trap.** Harry Beck's 1933 London Underground map succeeded not because it was geographically accurate, but because it answered a single user question clearly: "How do I get from where I am to where I want to go?" When London tried to make the map "complete" with every detail, usability collapsed. Your app likely abandoned the equivalent principle—there's no clear answer to "I want to track my workout, where do I tap?" The scene of abandonment is the moment they realize the app optimizes for *possibility* instead of *clarity*.

## The App That Refuses to Simplify

* **Constraint as liberation—the sonnet form.** Shakespeare didn't see the 14-line sonnet constraint as a limitation on expression; he used it as a *creative forcing function* that made every word carry weight. An "AI concierge" that locks users out of unrelated features isn't punishment—it's constraint design. The insight is: simplicity isn't the absence of features, it's the *relationship* between them. Instead of removing 11 features, define one feature as the weekly goal, and let it serve as a forcing function that makes the other features *coherent satellites* rather than independent tools. The app doesn't become simpler; it becomes *structured by narrative*—each feature serves one core goal per week, which users accept because it's clearly intentional.

* **Navigation through music composition.** In orchestral music, complexity isn't solved by removing instruments; it's solved by arranging them so each has a clear role and timing. The Berlin Philharmonic plays 100+ instruments by giving each section its moment and purpose. Your app could apply this: don't remove the 15 features, reorganize them so they *activate sequentially* based on what the user is trying to accomplish this week. Week one: set a fitness goal → Week two: the meal planner offers recipes that support it → Week three: social challenges align with that goal. The features still exist; they're just *orchestrated*, not simultaneous.

* **The Costco paradox—the focusing SKU.** Costco thrives despite (and because of) limited selection by anchoring around one thing per category. But its genius move is the "Chief Steward" role: one person per department who decides weekly which items are the *hero*—the featured, promoted, positioned offering. For your app, the "coherence feature" isn't an AI that locks things down; it's a *weekly ritual of focus*. On Mondays, the app says "Your focus this week is [goal]" and every other feature orients toward it—not hidden, but clearly *in service of* a coherent narrative. Users accept complexity when it's orchestrated toward their stated intent.

## Zoning Before Demolition

* **The surgical vs. systemic distinction.** Urban zoning is exactly right as your frame. Demolishing features from the existing IA is like removing a building while keeping the neighborhood zoning laws intact—you get a parking lot where a house was, but the zone still says "residential." You'll discover this when the next feature request arrives and slots into the same neighborhood logic. True rezoning first asks: "What can we build here?" and redesigns the conceptual territory before deciding what occupies it. For your app, that means before removing features, decide: "Are we a *daily driver*—one place you check every morning?" or "A *goal-specific tool*—you open it when you have a specific question?" Those are different zones entirely. Surgery removes the cancerous building; rezoning decides what kind of neighborhood you want to be.

* **Suburban sprawl vs. urban renewal.** Suburban sprawl happens when you add features incrementally without asking whether the *infrastructure* supports them. Urban renewal happens when you rethink the zone, rewrite the code, and then decide what goes where. Your app has suburban-sprawl IA: features were added to existing neighborhoods without asking if the streets could handle the traffic. Rezoning first means: "Do we zone for *depth* (go deep into one feature) or *breadth* (quick hits across many)?" That decision changes *everything* downstream about navigation, onboarding, notifications, even feature design itself.

* **Cathedral construction phases.** Medieval cathedrals weren't demolished and rebuilt; they were rezoned. The floor plan changed, the load-bearing walls shifted, the purpose evolved—sometimes over centuries. The key insight: demolition is binary (build or don't), but rezoning allows *phased transformation*. Your app could rezone in phases: Q1 decides whether we're a *tracking tool* or a *social tool*, Q2 reorders the IA around that zone, Q3 removes features that don't fit the new zone. This sequencing is different from feature-removal strategy—it's first establishing what kind of product you want to *be*, then removing what doesn't fit that identity. The zone change comes before the demolition.
