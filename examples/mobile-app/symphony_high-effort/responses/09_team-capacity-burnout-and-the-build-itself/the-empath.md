# Team Capacity, Burnout, and the Build Itself - The Empath

---

## Burnout Compounding Under Relaunch Pressure

* **Summary.** This team is running on fumes, and launching a relaunch sprint on top of maintenance triage feels like asking someone to sprint while they're still catching their breath. My gut says the realistic capacity available is maybe 60% of what the project plan assumes—not because people are lazy, but because exhaustion creates cognitive fog, decision paralysis, and a creeping sense that nothing will ever be good enough anyway. If the simplified product ships under those conditions, it won't just be technically degraded; it'll carry the emotional residue of desperation. That feeling transfers. Users sense when something was built by a team running on empty.

---

## The Emotional Cost of Killing Darlings

* **Summary.** When the team sees the cut list, someone will feel a visceral loss—not just "strategically we're deprioritizing this," but "I spent three months on that and now it's gone." The hardest cuts won't be the ones nobody cares about; they'll be the features someone fought for, believed in, and built beautifully. That unresolved grief—if it's not named and acknowledged—will seep into how the core features get built. Engineers will subconsciously overengineer what's left, trying to prove the cuts weren't wasteful. The team needs permission to mourn the work, not to pretend it never mattered.

---

## Morale Rebound Through Maintenance Burden Relief

* **Summary.** There's something deeply energizing about removing drag. If 6 engineers are maintaining 15 features and the current structure is fragmenting attention, cutting might release a surge of focus that feels almost euphoric—especially if the team can *see* the hours recovered: "We were burning 15 hours a week on feature X, and we're killing it, so suddenly we have 15 hours back to make the core sing." That momentum is real and powerful. My instinct says the morale win from reduced maintenance chaos could last 2–3 months and generate genuine speed. But it needs to be earned by the cuts being clear, not muddled.

---

## Team's Proudest Build

* **Summary.** This question matters more than any metric. There's a feature they looked at after shipping and felt proud—not because it's popular, but because building it felt *good*. Maybe it was technically elegant, or it solved a real problem cleanly, or the team collaborated beautifully on it. That pride is a signal about the team's actual strengths and what they want to do more of. Find that feature and ask: what made it feel good to build? That's the emotional DNA the core product should carry. The simplified version succeeds if it lets the team feel that pride again, more often.

---

## Incremental vs. Clean Break: The Emotional Gut

* **Summary.** Incremental simplification feels safe but carries a hidden weight—the constant negotiation with legacy features, the death by a thousand cuts, the feeling that nothing will ever be definitively simpler. A clean break is terrifying and exhilarating because it's decisive; it says "we're done with that era." The team's gut pull toward one or the other reveals what they actually believe is possible: incremental suggests "we can fix this piecemeal"; clean break suggests "we need to be born again." My instinct is that the team already knows which one would feel *true* to them. That feeling is data.

---

## Burnout as Scope Oracle

* **Summary.** Stop treating burnout as just a morale problem. The team's exhaustion is telling a story about which features are parasitic—not because they're unpopular, but because they create disproportionate drag relative to what users actually value or want to maintain. The engineer who is burned out from supporting feature X every week is giving you honest feedback that feature X is fighting you, not cooperating. Translate that exhaustion into a map: which three features, if killed tomorrow, would immediately release the most emotional and cognitive relief? That's your cut signal.

---

## Team Exhaustion as Organizational Immune Response

* **Summary.** What if the burnout isn't a problem to *solve* before the relaunch but a signal that the product architecture itself is broken? The team's exhaustion is their body saying "this doesn't work anymore." A relaunch that simply streamlines the current broken structure will fail because the problem isn't the number of features; it's that the codebase and feature interactions are incoherent. My instinct says the team's burnout stops the moment they ship a product so internally coherent that building feels generative instead of defensive. That's the real north star.

---

## The Day the Codebase Stopped Resisting

* **Summary.** Picture that engineer six months post-launch realizing the code *cooperates*. That moment of relief—"I built something and the system didn't fight back"—is what the team is actually craving. It signals that the current 15 features are creating architectural friction, technical debt, and hidden complexity that makes even simple changes feel risky. When the team imagines that future moment vividly, they'll recognize which current features are generating the most resistance at the code level. The ones that feel most fragile, most integrated in weird ways, most likely to break adjacent systems—kill those first.

---

## Manufacturing a Founding Moment

* **Summary.** Slack pivoted from a game studio partly because founders had emotional permission to recognize what actually mattered. This team needs the same psychological reset: permission to stop defending the old product and start building something new, even if technically it's a relaunch. That shift doesn't happen through messaging alone; it happens when the team feels genuinely *heard* about what was broken and *relieved* of defending it. The relaunch frames differently internally (liberation) vs. externally (refinement). If the team feels like they're starting fresh—with the support and clean break to prove it—they'll build with founding energy instead of legacy resignation.
