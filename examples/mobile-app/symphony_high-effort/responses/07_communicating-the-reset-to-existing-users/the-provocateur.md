# Communicating the Reset to Existing Users - The Provocateur

---

## The Announcement Backfire: What If Silence Were the Announcement?

* **Summary.** Stop announcing the removal entirely. Let users discover the missing features themselves, but make the core experience so radically better that within 72 hours they don't care. **The absurd version:** Go dark for 48 hours, then ship the reset with zero email, zero notification—just a silent push. **The insight:** This exposes whether you're actually confident the reset is better, or just hoping announcement strategy can mask a product that still doesn't work. If you can't trust users to figure out why the app is suddenly fast and clear without a 500-word justification email, the problem isn't communication—it's that the reset doesn't solve the core frustration. Real credibility: let the product speak.

* **Summary.** Announce the reset *only* to the 2% power users via phone call. Have a real human explain the decision, listen to their objection, and offer them a custom feature or a refund. **The absurd version:** Make 3,600 personal phone calls. **The insight:** Power users don't churn because of feature loss—they churn because they were never consulted and feel betrayed by abandonment. The majority of the 98% didn't notice those power-user features anyway. Concentrate communication effort where it actually matters: the people who have a real relationship with the product.

* **Summary.** Frame the announcement as a product death announcement, not a reset. "We made a product, we learned from how you used it, and we're starting over." Invite users to a "post-mortem" webinar where the team walks through the failures—what shipped that nobody wanted, what felt half-baked, what was a mistake. **The absurd version:** Celebrate the failures as learning. **The insight:** Users respond better to honest admission of mistake than to rebranding of the same failure as a "strategic focus." Emotional honesty beats spin every time.

* **Summary.** Communicate differently by effort level: power users (top 2%) get a custom migration email with alternatives; casual users (bottom 30%) get a cheerful, two-sentence notification about improved speed and clarity; everyone else gets nothing—just the better experience. **The absurd version:** Segment the announcement as carefully as you'd segment a feature. **The insight:** You're treating every user as if they had the same relationship with the product. They didn't. Spray-and-pray announcements create the wrong emotional tone for every segment.

## The Niche Migration Cliff: What If You Invited the Review-Bomb?

* **Summary.** Before launch, proactively contact the 50-100 power users of each removed feature. Offer them a choice: (1) a 90-day transition window with the feature still functional, (2) a direct integration or API to build their own version, or (3) a public co-design session where they help you build the next-generation version of that feature. **The absurd version:** Give users agency in their own abandonment. **The insight:** The review-bomb happens because users feel erased, not because the features left. Dignified exit options transform "we killed your feature" into "we're rethinking this together."

* **Summary.** Launch a "features we're mourning" campaign on your social channels. For each removed feature, post a 30-second video titled "We built this for you, and we're letting it go." Ask users to post their favorite memories using that feature under #[app]_lives_on. **The absurd version:** Celebrate what you're deleting. **The insight:** Publicly acknowledging the grief prevents it from exploding in reviews later. Users who feel heard don't attack; they migrate quietly. The emotional release valve matters more than the feature itself.

* **Summary.** Offer every user with >50 sessions in a removed feature a $5 credit toward a fitness app they actually prefer. Publish the list of recommended alternatives. **The absurd version:** Pay users to leave rather than fight them to stay.** **The insight:** The 2% DAU/MAU power users aren't your growth vector anymore—they're a retention anchor that's dragging down the user experience for the 98%. Gentle exit reduces churn and improves the product story.

* **Summary.** Create a "feature graveyard" section in the app where users can see which features are being sunset and for how long they'll remain available. Make it visible, make it concrete, make it sad—use photos of the feature's best moments. **The absurd version:** Make feature removal ceremonial rather than stealth.** **The insight:** Surprise removals feel like betrayal. Advance notice feels like respect. Even if users are disappointed, they're disappointed earlier, when they can adjust, not 60 days later when they've deleted the app.

## Churn vs. Harm: What If "Abandon" Is the Honest Word?

* **Summary.** Reframe the communication entirely. Instead of "we're simplifying to focus on what matters," say "we made promises we couldn't keep, and we're fixing it by being honest about what we actually are." Don't hide the removal—name it clearly. "The meal planner didn't work. The coaching marketplace wasn't coaching. We're deleting both because you deserve better." **The absurd version:** Lead with failure.** **The insight:** Users aren't leaving because features disappeared; they're leaving because they feel lied to. Honesty heals faster than spin. This works especially for users with lower tech literacy—it's respectful of their intelligence.

* **Summary.** Provide plain-language documentation for each removed feature that names alternatives and gives step-by-step export instructions. Assume the user has never used an API. Use your app's UI to guide the export, not a help article. **The absurd version:** Make leaving as easy as staying.** **The insight:** The difference between "churn" and "harm" is whether users feel abandoned by your decision or abandoned by the process of adapting to it. Make the process frictionless.

* **Summary.** Send a 60-second video (not an email) to every user of removed features—a real team member, on camera, saying: "We built something you used, and we're taking it away. If that frustrates you, it should. Here's what we're doing instead, and here are other apps that might work better." **The absurd version:** Lead with discomfort instead of hiding from it.** **The insight:** Low-literacy users respond better to human voice than text. Video conveys tone—sincerity or spin—in ways email can't hide. And naming the frustration directly inoculates against the sense of being discarded.

* **Summary.** Include in-app support access (chat, phone, or email) specifically for users of removed features, available for 30 days post-launch, to help them migrate manually or find alternatives. Staff it with real humans, not a bot. **The absurd version:** Invest in the goodbye as much as the new product.** **The insight:** Customers who are leaving but receive respectful support don't leave as angry. They tell friends "they handled it with dignity." That reputation matters more than keeping them.

## Trust Repair and Emotional Credibility: What If the Team Designed for Grief?

* **Summary.** Before launch, hold an internal "emotional simulation" where team members role-play as power users opening the app and discovering missing features. What's the first moment of confusion? The moment they realize what's gone? What would actually ease that moment beyond "we made it faster"? Design a specific 10-second tutorial that acknowledges the reset. **The absurd version:** Treat onboarding as therapy, not feature explanation.** **The insight:** Teams that design around emotion rather than function create experiences that feel intentional, not abandoned. The reset will feel like care, not erasure.

* **Summary.** Offer existing users a "legacy mode" for their first week—a UI toggle that shows them what *is* still available highlighted in color, with everything else greyed out and marked "removed in this update." Removes the shock of discovery. **The absurd version:** Let users tour the graveyard before adjusting.** **The insight:** Disorientation creates distrust. A clear map of what's changed lets users grieve without panic. The emotional arc moves from confusion → relief (if the core works), not confusion → anger.

* **Summary.** Have the PM and lead engineer record a personal video message to existing users: "We made something too complicated. We're making it simple. If we got it wrong, we want to hear from you." Embed it in the launch notification. **The absurd version:** Make the reset feel like accountability, not a rebrand.** **The insight:** Emotional credibility comes from vulnerability, not polish. Users sense whether the team actually believes in the reset or is just damage-controlling. Recorded vulnerability beats corporate email every time.

* **Summary.** In the first week post-launch, show returning users a personalized "what's new" card that acknowledges what they *personally* used before and which core features they'll still have access to. Make it specific to their session history, not generic. **The absurd version:** Prove you paid attention by showing what you removed from *their* experience.**** **The insight:** Generic "thanks for sticking with us" messaging is insulting. Personalization that says "we see you, and here's what we preserved for you" rebuilds trust.

## The Email You Dread Writing: What Story Would Make It Okay?

* **Summary.** Write two versions of the announcement email and A/B test them to a small segment (1000 users) before sending to all 180K. Version A: "We're focusing on what matters." Version B: "We're admitting we failed and fixing it." Measure which generates better engagement and fewer angry replies. **The absurd version:** Let users vote on the narrative.** **The insight:** You don't actually know which story feels true. Testing reveals whether users hear "focus" as "progress" or "retreat." The winning narrative is the one that earns credibility in advance.

* **Summary.** Start the email with the hardest sentence: "We removed 12 features you might have liked." Then explain why each removal makes the core experience better. Own the removal before contextualizing it. **The absurd version:** Lead with the bad news.**** **The insight:** Users know something's being taken away. Acknowledging it first builds trust. Burying it makes users feel manipulated. Honesty about loss actually increases perceived integrity.

* **Summary.** Offer three versions of the email: honest/humble, strategic/visionary, and apology/redemption. Let users choose which tone they want to hear by selecting their primary use case in a one-click survey. **The absurd version:** Let users choose the emotional story they believe.** **The insight:** Power users want transparency and strategy. Casual users want simplicity. Non-users who churn want acknowledgment of what went wrong. One email can't serve all stories.

* **Summary.** Include a hyperlink to a "decision log" in the email—publicly documented reasons for each removal decision: "We removed the meal planner because 3% of users used it, it required 40% of engineering time, and it delivered half the value of the core feature." **The absurd version:** Show your work.** **The insight:** Transparency about the *process* of removal, not just the fact of removal, gives users permission to accept the reset as necessary, not arbitrary. Data + rationale = credibility.

## The First Open After Relaunch: What If the First 60 Seconds Told the Whole Story?

* **Summary.** The moment users open the reset app, show a full-screen image or video (2 seconds, then auto-dismiss) that visually represents the core promise: a lapsed user running in the park, or lifting a weight, with one clean goal on screen. No feature tour, no explanation—just "this is what we're here for." **The absurd version:** Let the design speak louder than words.** **The insight:** Lapsed users won't read copy; they'll take a 3-second emotional read of whether this is finally the simple tool they wanted. A powerful visual in the first second does more trust repair than an onboarding flow.

* **Summary.** If the user returns and a feature they previously used is missing, show a single card: "You used [feature] before. Here's why we removed it and what to use instead." Make it personal, specific, and one-tap to an alternative. **The absurd version:** Acknowledge their specific loss, not a generic removal.** **The insight:** Personalization proves you're not just cutting features—you're redesigning around their actual needs. The alternative suggestion (even external) matters less than the acknowledgment that you noticed what they lost.

* **Summary.** Measure success not by "what did users tap first" but by "how many users opened, didn't immediately leave, and returned within 48 hours?" Lapsed users returning for a relaunch don't need a feature tour—they need the experience to justify why they should care again. **The absurd version:** Judge the relaunch by whether it re-engages, not by how it onboards.** **The insight:** A lapsed user's 60 seconds is a referendum on whether you fixed the original problem. Clarity, speed, and one clear action matter infinitely more than feature showcase.

* **Summary.** In the first 60 seconds, after the core feed/workout screen loads, show a single message: "Something looks different. Here's what changed." Link to a 90-second video that explains the reset without apologizing for it. Then get out of the way. **The absurd version:** Make the explanation optional, not mandatory.** **The insight:** Users who want context will click; users who want to try the app won't be forced through a pitch. The option to understand without being trapped in onboarding rebuilds agency.

## The Hospice Model for Features: What If Removal Had Dignity?

* **Summary.** For each of the 12 removed features, create a 30-day farewell window where the feature still works but is slowly deprioritized visually—greyed out, marked "ending," with a countdown. Day 1, the feature is prominent with a "farewell" badge. Day 30, it's hidden unless the user specifically searches for it. Sends goodbye messages to power users of that feature. **The absurd version:** Let features die slowly, with ceremony.** **The insight:** Abrupt removal feels like abandonment. Staged removal lets users grieve, export, and migrate on their own timeline. It's harder to build but transforms "killed features" into "respectfully sunset features."

* **Summary.** For each removed feature, host a 30-minute "feature retrospective" webinar where users who loved it can share what they built or achieved with it, and the team explains the reasoning for removal. Record and publish the video. **The absurd version:** Celebrate what you're deleting with the people who used it.** **The insight:** Features with passionate user bases matter emotionally, not functionally. A public farewell—where the team listens to what made the feature matter—transforms users from "abandoned" to "heard." They might still leave, but they leave as advocates, not detractors.

* **Summary.** In the codebase, don't delete removed features—archive them in a public "feature graveyard" repo with full documentation, UI screenshots, and user testimonials. Link to it from the app. **The absurd version:** Keep the dead features alive as monuments.** **The insight:** Users who invested time in a feature want to know it mattered. Publishing it transforms removal from erasure into legacy. Future users might even fork and improve those features independently.

* **Summary.** Offer the top 10 users of each removed feature an early-access invite to design the *next* version of that category of feature (18 months out). Turn removal into recruitment for the future roadmap. **The absurd version:** Let the people you're abandoning now help you build what comes next.** **The insight:** Power users don't just churn—they become critics of the vision. If you can reframe removal as "we're rethinking this with your input," they become co-designers instead of enemies. Churn transforms into retention in future.
