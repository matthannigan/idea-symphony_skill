# Runway as a Design Constraint - The Simplifier

---

## The 8-Month Runway as a False Floor and a Churn-Threshold Gate

* **Stop pretending 8 months is unchanging.** You're designing around a budget that will compress the moment you relaunch. Don't calculate runway by dividing current monthly spend by remaining cash. Relaunch requires spike spending: design overhaul, marketing blitz, QA for a drastically different product, possibly contractor help. The real runway after relaunch spend is likely 5–6 months, not 8. Design the product that works in 5 months, not 8.

* **Make the churn threshold explicit before you cut anything.** You have 180K downloads and 8% retention. If post-relaunch retention hits 12%, that's growth. But how much churn is unacceptable? Is losing 30% of users a failure, or does the product survive only if it retains 60%? This conversation needs to happen with leadership and finance now, not discovered mid-relaunch when user loss triggers panic cuts that undo all your simplification work.

* **The runway is the gating metric, not the timeline.** Stop thinking "ship in 8 months." Think "reach sustainable unit economics or die." If your simplified product can't show path to profitability or retention improvement by month 4, the remaining runway disappears not because of time, but because investors won't fund the next cycle. Use runway as a forcing function to cut ruthlessly now, not as a deadline to hit later.

* **Design backwards from the relaunch spend spike.** If relaunch costs 3 months of budget, you have 5 months post-launch to prove traction. Build only what delivers proof of concept in that 5-month window. Everything else is waste. Features that take 2 months to build but prove value only in month 7–8 don't exist in this timeline. Cut them now.

* **The churn cascade is inevitable; make it work for you.** Simplification will trigger user churn—people loved the marketplace feature, the meal planner, whatever their edge case was. Some will leave, and that's okay. But the churn needs to happen in month 1–2 when you're redesigning anyway, not month 4 when it creates panic. Make sure your communications and feature deprecation strategy front-load the pain so you can operate with clarity in the second half of your runway.

## A Product Designed for an 8-Month Runway

* **An 8-month runway doesn't shrink the idea; it clarifies it.** Forget "simplified version of the app." What's the minimal product that could earn a next funding round in 5 months? That product probably has 1–2 core features, not 3–4. It does one thing exceptionally well. If you're thinking "simplified app with 4 polished features," you're still thinking like someone with 12 months. An 8-month runway demands 1 feature executed so well that retention is 25%+.

* **The simplified version you're already considering is probably too ambitious.** You think you've cut ruthlessly by going from 15 features to 4. You haven't. Ask: what's the single feature that generated the original 180K downloads? Build that feature alone. Test it. Launch it. If it gains traction, you've earned the budget to add the next feature. The 8-month constraint makes it obvious that building 4 features in parallel is risk you can't afford.

* **Survival mode simplification looks different from optionality simplification.** With 8 months, you're not designing a product with nice-to-haves and optional paths. You're designing a product where every screen serves one purpose. No fancy onboarding—users land in the core experience immediately. No settings depth. No "more options" dropdowns. Every interaction is critical path. That's not just simpler; it's a completely different design ethos.

* **8 months forces you to choose between perfection and speed.** You can either spend 4 months designing and polishing a 2-feature product, or 2 months shipping a rougher 1-feature product and having 3 months to iterate on real user feedback. The latter wins. An 8-month runway is actively hostile to design perfectionism. Ship version 0.7, learn fast, rebuild based on what you find.

* **The 8-month constraint actually solves the "which features matter" debate.** You can't afford user interviews, cohort analysis, or A/B testing to figure out what to keep. You have to make a leap of faith based on instinct and launch. This removes the analysis paralysis. Your team knows, deep down, which 1–2 features feel core. Stop asking why and just commit to it.

## Eight Months as a Creative Brief

* **Turn the constraint into a scope-killing rule: "Does this decision add value visible in month 2?"** A 12-month runway lets you debate incrementalism. An 8-month runway demands a rule that kills scope instantly. Can users see and feel the change by the second month post-launch? No? Cut it. This one rule eliminates half the feature work because infrastructure, technical debt paydown, and "making it scalable" are pushed off until month 3+.

* **Use the deadline as the deciding vote in every scope debate.** Engineer argues for refactoring the backend architecture—legitimate concern, but does it matter in the first 2 months? Designer wants a sophisticated onboarding flow—beautiful idea, but does it drive retention in month 1? Put the 8-month deadline on the table and most debates resolve instantly in favor of speed and directness. The constraint is your best editorial tool.

* **The tightness of the constraint is liberating, not restricting.** You can't afford to ship multiple experiments. You can't ship a product and see what sticks. You're forced to commit to a vision and execute it cleanly. That clarity—"we are a workout-first app, period"—makes every decision downstream trivial. A 12-month timeline lets teams hedge bets. An 8-month timeline demands conviction, and conviction is much cheaper to execute.

* **Set a decision rule for cutting features mid-development.** "If we're not 70% done with a feature by month 3, we cut it and reassign people." The constraint isn't just a deadline; it's a ruthless decision mechanism. Make that rule public. It gives the team permission to kill half-built work and focus energy where it matters. Psychological permission to cut is half the battle.

* **Frame the constraint as competitive advantage.** Your competitors have 18-month runways and are still fighting about feature prioritization. You have 8 months and a forcing function that makes you ship faster and simpler. By month 5, when your simplified product has real user traction and your competitor is still designing features, you're not racing against time—you're winning a market. The deadline is a feature of your go-to-market strategy, not a threat.
