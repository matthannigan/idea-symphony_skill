# Defining the Core Value Hypothesis - The Provocateur

---

## The Irreducible Need Beneath the Bloat

* **What if none of the 15 features is the core?** The real irreducible need might be the emotional experience of *witnessing yourself change over time* — which none of your current features do well. Every feature competes for attention; none creates the singular clarity of "I am different than I was." Kill 14 features and ask: which one best mirrors progress back to the user with zero ambiguity? *The insight: maybe your core isn't a feature category at all, but a psychological mechanism.*

* **Imagine the app was designed for someone with advanced dementia.** What would you keep? Only features that create *immediate sensory reward or intrinsic progress*, not features that require memory, context, or accumulation of past sessions. The 15 features rely on longitudinal data you don't actually have (retention is 8%). What if your core is the one thing that creates value in a single session, no history required? *The insight: your bloat assumes users are building something; maybe your true core is immediate pleasure.*

* **What if "irreducible need" is the wrong frame?** Maybe the need isn't human—it's *organizational*. You kept adding features not because users asked, but because each founder had a different vision of what fitness *should be*. The core isn't buried in user behavior; it's buried in the team's original conviction. Which feature makes someone on your team genuinely angry or sad to lose? That's your real core. *The insight: core value is emotional territory, not rational deduction.*

* **Flip the user model backward.** Instead of "what do users need," ask "what can this app teach them they didn't know they needed?" Your 47-second sessions suggest users are tourists, not residents. What if the real core is something that *breaks their habit patterns* and creates productive discomfort? *The insight: maybe value isn't comfort; it's useful friction.*

## Assumed Core vs. Revealed Core

* **What if you held a vote and the team's answers didn't matter?** Imagine the PM, each engineer, and the designer each privately write down which feature they think is the true core—without talking to each other first. What if 5 out of 8 say the same feature? *Don't trust the consensus—act on the disagreement.* The features the team *argued about* are where the real tensions live. Disagreement is data about unstated product identity. *The insight: your hidden assumptions are visible only in conflict.*

* **Deliberately mis-ask the data question.** Instead of "which features have the highest usage," ask "which features have the highest *gradient*—where users went from 0% daily use to 60% in the shortest time?" You might find a feature with low total usage but explosive adoption curves. That speed of conversion is the core's signature. Low volume + steep adoption = something psychologically potent you're burying under noise. *The insight: don't count users; measure momentum.*

* **Imagine the app was designed by a different team.** If you fired everyone and hired a new team, gave them 6 months to rebuild from the existing user base, which existing features would they choose to keep? What would they discard immediately? Their choices would reveal which features feel like inherited debt vs. which feel like actual products. *The insight: freshness reveals what the original team is too close to see.*

* **What if the data and users are telling you something different on purpose?** Analytics show feature X is used 40% of the time (assuming it's core), but users in interviews say they actually love feature Y (even though it has 8% usage). Instead of resolving that conflict, *amplify it.* What if users are using X out of obligation and loving Y out of genuine delight? Metrics measure friction, not joy. *The insight: your core might be the feature people feel embarrassed to admit is important.*

* **Reverse the disagreement test.** Which feature could you cut tomorrow and the team would *celebrate*, not mourn? That feature isn't your core—it's your burden. Identifying what the team desperately wants gone reveals what's not worth fighting for. Then ask: why did we build something everyone secretly hates? The answer is your real core, inverted. *The insight: consensus around pain points shows where you should have been clear about identity.*

## The First-Loved Feature

* **What if "first-loved" is the opposite of core?** Users might have loved a feature in isolation precisely *because* it wasn't surrounded by 14 other features competing for attention. The feature you loved in week one might be hated now because it's jammed into a chaotic UX. Don't ask which feature was loved—ask which feature was *hurt most by the bloat*. That's your core. Resurrect it from the noise, and it might be loved again. *The insight: core value can be destroyed by context, not by the feature itself.*

* **Find the unsolicited positive feedback—then ask why it stopped.** If users originally praised feature Z, why aren't they talking about it anymore? Did engagement drop because the feature degraded? Because the team never iterated on it? Because better competitors launched? Or because the 14 other features cannibalized its visibility? Trace the mortality of that delight. *The insight: sometimes the core didn't fail; you buried it alive.*

* **Imagine users praised feature X for a reason totally different than you intended.** You built a workout tracker expecting people to love the exercise library. Instead, users loved the *notification reminders*—not for motivation, but because the notifications created structure in their chaotic mornings. That's the actual need: *temporal scaffolding*, not fitness expertise. The feature was loved for its hidden property, not its advertised value. *The insight: users reveal the real core through their misuse of what you made.*

## What If the App Didn't Exist Yet

* **Imagine you're inventing from scratch today, but you're banned from using the word "fitness."** No fitness app frameworks, no wellness category vocabulary. You can only describe the irreducible thing users are trying to do. Are they trying to *prove something to themselves*? *Compete with others*? *Follow a ritual*? *Feel physical agency*? That forbidden version—stripped of category language—is your core. *The insight: category names are cages that hide the real human need.*

* **What if you designed it to be used while doing something else—never as the primary activity?** Most apps assume "open app, use app." What if your core product was something that *lives alongside other activities* and gets checked between breaths? The 47-second session isn't a failure—it's the real use case. Design for that, not against it. That inverts everything: instead of building for deep engagement, you're building for *continuous micro-confirmation*. *The insight: maybe the core is ambient, not deep.*

* **What if the product was designed for exactly one person, and that person wasn't average?** Not "target users who want fitness tracking." Instead: "design for the person who is *already* obsessed with fitness and is only downloading because they're searching for one missing piece." Find the edge case that's actually a signal. You have 180K downloads and 8% retention—you're getting wrong people, not serving them badly. Focus on the person who would use it 5 times a day. Who is that? *The insight: core value often lives in extreme users, not averages.*

* **Imagine the single irreducible thing is not a feature, but a *social signal*.** Users download fitness apps partly to tell themselves and others "I'm the kind of person who does this." What if your core is the permission structure or identity marker, not the functional tool? Design the app so using it *is the signal*, not a byproduct of it. *The insight: status and identity often matter more than utility in retention.*

## The Product as Relationship, Not Tool

* **What if the app was designed to never ask you to open it?** Instead of 47-second sessions, imagine it sends you notifications that *are themselves the value*: hyperlocal information, unexpected encouragement, a random fact about your body, a weird challenge your friend is doing. The "app" is the notification; the app store is just a delivery mechanism. Usage time collapses to zero; value collapses to the microsecond of delight when the notification arrives. You've solved the 47-second problem by eliminating the opening problem. *The insight: maybe "session length" is the wrong metric entirely; maybe the product is asynchronous.*

* **Imagine the app was a person you had a conversation with once a week, like a coach or friend.** That relationship can be ambient (you think about it between conversations) because it's *personal*. What if your app's core is the creation of a parasocial relationship—that feeling that something or someone "knows you"? Right now you're offering 15 generic features; you could offer one genuine relationship. *The insight: ambient presence is created by personality and recognition, not by features.*

* **What if the app's value was a slow-burn habit that takes 90 days to notice?** Instead of short-term engagement metrics, imagine users experience a quiet, cumulative shift in their sense of agency that they can't attribute to any single feature. No spike in retention; just a 4% annual migration of "I think I'm a different person." You'd miss that completely with your current metrics. That's your core—something so ambient it's invisible to analytics. *The insight: the most powerful products often feel like they didn't do anything.*

* **What if users weren't supposed to progress, but to *return*?** Like a ritual or meditation, not a tool for getting somewhere. The 47-second session is perfect if you're designing for the repetition of a meaningful gesture. The issue is you're measuring it against linear progress (fitness goals), when it should be measured against consistency (ritual adherence). *The insight: cyclical value beats linear value for ambient presence.*

## The Identity Users Never Had a Word For

* **What if the category doesn't exist because it's taboo or uncomfortable to name?** There might be a need around *controlled failure*, *managed vulnerability*, or *performance of non-perfection*—things fitness culture actively punishes. Users downloaded your app but churn quickly because what they actually want (permission to be imperfect, to fail gracefully, to compare struggles rather than wins) is the opposite of what fitness apps offer. What if you relaunch as the app for people who *don't want* to be fitness people? *The insight: the latent need might be anti-product-category.*

* **What if users wanted to discover something about themselves that no product *can* deliver—only facilitate?** Not "get fit," but "find out what I'm capable of doing if I actually tried." That's a self-discovery experience, not a fitness product. You'd design it completely differently: less about features, more about *structured experiments in capability*. Randomization, blindness, reflection, surprise. *The insight: the real need might be epistemological, not functional.*

* **What if the 180K people who downloaded your app are actually a hidden community that doesn't know they exist?** They're not "fitness enthusiasts" or "wellness seekers"—they're *people searching for permission structures and social scaffolding to do hard things*. That's a much bigger category than "fitness," and it includes people who want to learn an instrument, write a book, or repair a relationship. Your app kept trying to be a category-specific tool when it could have been a *general difficulty-management system*. *The insight: relaunch by de-specializing, not by clarifying your niche.*

* **What if the category you're creating is "the app for when your life is chaotic"?** Not goal-oriented, but *stabilization-oriented*. Users might download because their life feels out of control, and they're looking for *any* repeated activity that proves they can complete something. Fitness is incidental; structure is core. You don't relaunch as a fitness app; you relaunch as a *commitment-keeping app* and let users fill in what they're committing to. *The insight: the identity might be about proving agency, not about the domain of the activity.*
