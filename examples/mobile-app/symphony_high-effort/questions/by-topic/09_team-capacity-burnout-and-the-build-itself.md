# Topic Cluster 09: Team Capacity, Burnout, and the Build Itself

## Questions

44. **Burnout compounding under relaunch pressure, the self-reinforcing fragmentation of attention, and burnout's seepage into the work itself**: The team is already burned out from maintaining 15 half-built features — a self-reinforcing fragmentation of attention where each addition diluted the app's identity, each split engineering attention further, each raised bug rates that triggered emergency patches. A relaunch requires a concentrated burst of design, engineering, and marketing work on top of that maintenance; have we modeled the realistic capacity available for the relaunch sprint, what risk does it carry that the simplified product launches in a degraded state because the team simply ran out of energy, and how much should we trust feature-prioritization judgment from a team in triage mode where calls may be shaped more by which features caused the most pain than by which ones users value most? Burnout isn't neutral — it seeps into the work; how does the emotional state of the people building the simplified product affect the quality and feel of what gets made, and what does the team need emotionally before they can build something genuinely good?
45. **Killing your darlings, sunk-cost weight, and the trust signal of cuts**: When the team looks at the list of features to cut, which one will feel like a genuine loss — not just strategically wrong, but emotionally painful — and what does that visceral reluctance reveal about what the team actually believes the product is for? The team has poured months of effort into 15 half-built features; how will it feel to publicly declare that work "done" by removing it, how might unresolved feelings about wasted effort quietly contaminate the simplification process itself, and when engineers and the designer are told that half of what they built is being cut, what emotional signal does that send about how their past contributions are valued — and how does that feeling affect whether they'll commit to building the new focused core with genuine care?
46. **The morale rebound and the maintenance cost per feature**: With 6 engineers maintaining 15 features, each consumes roughly 0.4 engineers of maintenance capacity on average. Simplification often releases engineering attention that was fragmented across unmaintainable surface area, and that release can produce a burst of quality and speed on the retained core. What is the team's current estimate of how many engineering hours per week are spent in maintenance and bug-fixing on features slated for removal; how does that burden distribute unevenly across the 15 (which features consume disproportionate engineering time relative to user value); and what does that imply both for which cuts recover the most team capacity fastest and for the realistic quality uplift the simplified version could achieve in 8 months?
47. **Team's proudest build**: Setting aside metrics, which feature or interaction did the engineering and design team feel most proud of shipping — what made it satisfying to build, and what does that pride signal about the team's genuine strengths?
48. **The fear behind incremental vs. clean relaunch, and the user-exodus map**: The question of whether to simplify incrementally or do a clean relaunch sounds strategic, but it also has an emotional dimension — incremental feels safer, while a clean break feels both exhilarating and terrifying. Which direction does the team's gut pull toward, and what does that pull reveal about what they actually believe is possible? What would users who currently rely on the features being cut actually do — move to competitors, abandon the behavior, or advocate loudly — and have we mapped that exodus before deciding how dramatic the reset should be?
49. **Team burnout as a scope oracle**: The team is burned out from maintaining 15 half-built features; burnout is usually framed as a morale problem, but what if it is also the most honest signal about which features are generating disproportionate maintenance drag relative to user value, and how could that exhaustion be translated into a prioritization heuristic?
50. **The team's exhaustion as signal, not problem**: What if the team's burnout from maintaining 15 half-built features is not an obstacle to solve before the relaunch but the most honest signal in the entire product — a kind of organizational immune response — and the relaunch's purpose is not to restore the team's capacity to build features but to create a product architecture so coherent that building becomes energizing rather than depleting?
51. **The day the team stopped fighting the codebase**: Picture a specific moment — six months after the relaunch — when an engineer on the team sits down to build a new feature and realizes, for the first time in years, that the codebase cooperates instead of resists. What changed in that experience, and what does imagining that future moment reveal about which of the current 15 features are most responsible for the drag that's burning the team out today?
52. **Pivot vs. restart — manufacturing a founding moment**: Successful pivots (Slack from a game studio, Instagram from Burbn) involved founders who recognized that one element of their existing product had disproportionate signal — there's a structural parallel between this external pattern and the internal challenge of getting a burned-out six-person team to invest emotionally in a "new" product that is also, literally, the old product — how do you manufacture the psychological conditions of a founding moment inside a team that has lived through the product's failure, and what does the answer imply about how the relaunch is framed internally vs. externally?
